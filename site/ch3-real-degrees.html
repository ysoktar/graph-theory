Real networks are not Poisson

If real networks were wired at random, how large could their best-connected node be? The Poisson tail answers this precisely, and real networks miss the answer by hundreds of orders of magnitude.

  • This page tests the Poisson prediction on data.
  • Nodes far above the predicted largest degree mean that chance wiring is rejected: some mechanism concentrates the links.

How large is the largest node? (Advanced Topic 3.B)

$$N\,P(K > k_{\max}) \approx 1, \qquad N\,P(K < k_{\min}) \approx 1.$$
$K$
the degree of a randomly chosen node (a random variable)
$P(K > k)$
the fraction of nodes with more than $k$ links, $\sum_{k' > k} p_{k'}$
$N\,P(K > k_{\max})$
the expected number of such nodes; setting it to 1 means "about one node"
$k_{\min}$
the smallest degree, found the same way from the lower tail

With $\langle k\rangle = 10$, $k_{\max} \approx 21$ for $N = 10^3$, 28 for $10^6$ and 34 for $10^9$. A power law with $\gamma = 2.5$ instead gives $k_{\max} \sim N^{2/3} = 10^6$ at $N = 10^9$.

A random society

A person knows about $\langle k\rangle \approx 1{,}000$ people. The figure plots $N p_k$, the expected number of people with $k$ acquaintances, if society were a random network.

For $N = 7\times10^9$ the most connected person has about 1,200 acquaintances and the least connected about 800. With $\sigma_k \approx 32$, two thirds of people know 968 to 1,032 others and 99.7% know 905 to 1,095. A random society has no celebrities and no hermits.

The book's networks against Poisson

Each network is compared with the Poisson distribution that has its own $\langle k\rangle$. Logarithmic bins (which widen as $k$ grows) and the CCDF $P(K \ge k)$ show the tail clearly; linear bins reproduce the notebook's plot (see How (not) to plot $p_k$).

For the collaboration network ($\langle k\rangle = 8.08$), Poisson predicts $k_{\max} \approx 21$. There are 244 nodes with $k \ge 50$, where Poisson expects $6.5\times10^{-19}$. The expected number of nodes with $k = 279$ is $10^{-309}$.

Every network exceeds its Poisson $k_{\max}$. The smallest gap is the power grid's (19 against 10), an engineered network; the largest is the WWW's (10,721 against 21).

  • Builds on the Poisson tail of Degree distribution.
  • Contrast: the natural cutoff of a power law, $k_{\max} \sim k_{\min} N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$, grows like a power of $N$ (Ch 4).
  • Explained by growth and preferential attachment (Barabási–Albert model, Ch 5).
  • A better null model, once hubs are accepted, is the configuration model, which keeps every degree.

Random wiring predicts that no scientist has more than about 20 collaborators, but the data have dozens above 100. Which processes could produce these hubs? (Notebook II.3.)

Preferential attachment: well-known scientists attract collaborators (Ch 5). Fitness: some nodes are intrinsically more attractive (Ch 6). Longevity: early nodes have had more time. Team size: a paper with hundreds of authors gives each of them hundreds of links at once, an artefact of the bipartite projection.
