Clustering

Your friends tend to know each other. The clustering coefficient measures how much, and it is the property that random networks most clearly fail to reproduce.

The $k_i$ neighbours of node $i$ can have at most $k_i(k_i-1)/2$ links among themselves. If $L_i$ of these links exist, the local clustering coefficient is the fraction that is present:

$$C_i = \frac{2L_i}{k_i(k_i-1)}.$$
$k_i$
the degree of node $i$
$L_i$
the number of links among $i$'s neighbours, which equals the number of triangles through $i$
$k_i(k_i-1)/2$
the number of possible links among the neighbours
$C_i$
the probability that two neighbours of $i$ are linked, a number from 0 to 1

For $k_i \le 1$ the formula is undefined; the book, the notebook and NetworkX then set $C_i = 0$. Two numbers summarise the whole network:

$$\langle C\rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} C_i, \qquad C_\Delta = \frac{3\times \#\text{triangles}}{\#\text{connected triples}}.$$
$\langle C\rangle$
the average clustering, in which every node counts the same
connected triple
a node together with two of its neighbours; node $i$ is the centre of $k_i(k_i-1)/2$ of them
$C_\Delta$
the global clustering coefficient (nx.transitivity, book Advanced Topic 2.A): the share of connected triples that are closed into triangles
$3\times$
each triangle closes three triples, one at each corner

Karate member 1 has $k = 9$ neighbours with $L_1 = 12$ links among them, so $C_1 = 24/72 \approx 0.33$.

The whole club has 45 triangles and $\sum_i k_i(k_i-1)/2 = 528$ connected triples, so $C_\Delta = 135/528 \approx 0.256$, while $\langle C\rangle = 0.571$.

One node, four neighbours

Clustering in the karate club

The leaders have low clustering ($C_0 = 0.150$, $C_{33} = 0.110$), while many low-degree members sit in closed triangles. A hub's neighbours come from circles that do not know each other. This fall of clustering with degree, $C(k)$, returns in Part II.

Average or global clustering?

$\langle C\rangle$ counts every node equally, while $C_\Delta$ weights each node by its $k_i(k_i-1)/2$ triples, so hubs dominate it. The windmill graph, made of $t$ triangles that share one node, shows the extreme case.

With $t = 10$, the 20 outer nodes have $C_i = 1$ and the centre has $C = 1/19$, so $\langle C\rangle \approx 0.955$ but $C_\Delta = 3/21 \approx 0.143$.

The book's eight networks

Why is $\langle C\rangle$ larger than $C_\Delta$ in the collaboration network ($0.633$ against $0.264$)? (Notebook I.7.)

Most scientists have few co-authors, often from a single paper, which closes all their triangles ($C_i \approx 1$); $\langle C\rangle$ counts them equally. $C_\Delta$ counts triples, $k(k-1)/2$ per scientist, so the few prolific authors, whose co-authors mostly do not know each other, dominate it. The windmill graph is the extreme case.
  • Clustering measures local cohesion: friends of friends tend to become friends.
  • It is the clearest test of a model: a random network has $C \approx \langle k\rangle/N$, near 0 for large $N$.
  • Plotting $C(k)$ against $k$ reveals hierarchy: hubs connect separate groups.
  • Counts triangles, whose number is $\tfrac16\mathrm{Tr}\,A^3$ (Adjacency matrix).
  • Is partly created by projection: each paper becomes a clique of its authors (Bipartite networks).
  • The random-network prediction $C = p$ is wrong by orders of magnitude (Clustering in random networks).
  • Motivates the Watts–Strogatz model, which combines high clustering with short distances.

A node has 5 neighbours, with 4 links among them. What is its clustering coefficient $C_i$?

  1. 0.8
  2. 0.4
  3. 0.2
  4. 4/25
The neighbours could have $5 \cdot 4/2 = 10$ links, so $C_i = 4/10 = 0.4$.

A node with $k = 9$ has 12 links among its neighbours. What is $C_i$?

  1. 0.33
  2. 0.17
  3. 1.33
  4. 0.15
$C = 2 \cdot 12/(9 \cdot 8) = 12/36 \approx 0.33$ (karate member 1).

Why do the karate club's leaders (nodes 0 and 33) have low clustering $C_i$?

  1. They have few triangles.
  2. $C_i$ is undefined for hubs.
  3. Their many neighbours come from groups that rarely know each other.
  4. They are on the edge of the network.
The number of possible links among neighbours grows like $k^2$, and a hub that joins separate circles closes only a small share of them.

In the windmill graph ($t$ triangles that share one centre node), which of the two network-wide numbers does the centre dominate?

  1. $\langle C\rangle$
  2. $C_\Delta$
  3. Both equally.
  4. Neither.
The centre is the middle of $t(2t-1)$ of the $t(2t+1)$ triples, almost all of them open, and $C_\Delta$ counts triples.

A random network with the collaboration network's $N$ and $\langle k\rangle$ has $C \approx 3.5\times10^{-4}$, while the real network has $\langle C\rangle = 0.633$. What do you conclude?

  1. The difference is noise.
  2. The real network is too small to compare.
  3. Random networks have distances that are too long.
  4. The links are not placed at random, so a different model is needed.
The real clustering is about 1,800 times the random value. Models such as Watts–Strogatz add clustering on purpose.

A network is a single triangle: three nodes, all linked to each other. What are $\langle C\rangle$ and $C_\Delta$?

  1. Both are 1/3.
  2. Both are 0.5.
  3. Both are 1.
  4. They are undefined, since $k = 2$.
Each node has $k = 2$ and $L_i = 1$, so $C_i = 2/(2 \cdot 1) = 1$; and $C_\Delta = 3 \cdot 1/3 = 1$.

A star has one hub linked to 10 leaves (nodes with a single link). What is the hub's clustering coefficient $C_i$?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 0.1
  4. 1/45
Of the 45 possible pairs of leaves, none is linked, so $C_{\text{hub}} = 0$. The leaves have $k = 1$, so their $C = 0$ by convention.
