Clustering

Your friends tend to know each other. The clustering coefficient measures how much.

For a node $i$ with $k_i$ neighbours, at most $k_i(k_i-1)/2$ links can exist among those neighbours. If $L_i$ of them do, the local clustering coefficient is the fraction present:

$$C_i = \frac{2L_i}{k_i(k_i-1)}.$$

$C_i = 0$ if no two neighbours are linked and $C_i = 1$ if they all are; $C_i$ is the probability that two neighbours of $i$ are themselves linked. (For $k_i \le 1$ the formula is undefined; the book, the notebook and NetworkX all set $C_i = 0$.) Two network-wide summaries are in use:

$$\langle C\rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} C_i \qquad\text{and}\qquad C_\Delta = \frac{3\times \#\text{triangles}}{\#\text{connected triples}}.$$

A connected triple is a node together with two of its neighbours. $C_\Delta$, the global clustering coefficient (book, Advanced Topic 2.A; nx.transitivity), is the fraction of those triples that are closed into triangles.

Average or global clustering?

The two summaries answer different questions and can disagree wildly. $\langle C\rangle$ weights every node equally. $C_\Delta$ weights each node by its number of triples, $k_i(k_i-1)/2$, so hubs dominate it. The friendship (windmill) graph, $t$ triangles sharing one node, shows the difference in its purest form.

The book's eight networks

Why is $\langle C\rangle$ larger than $C_\Delta$ in the collaboration network ($0.633$ against $0.264$)? (Notebook I.7.)

$\langle C\rangle$ counts every scientist equally, and most scientists have few co-authors, often from a single paper, which closes all their triangles ($C_i$ close to 1). $C_\Delta$ counts triples, and a scientist with $k$ co-authors contributes $k(k-1)/2$ of them, so the few prolific authors, whose many co-authors mostly do not know each other, dominate it. The windmill graph above is the extreme case: every leaf has $C_i = 1$, the centre has $C_i \approx 0$, and the centre holds almost all the triples.
