Bose–Einstein condensation

The fitness model maps exactly onto a gas of quantum particles. The mapping predicts two phases: one where the fittest nodes lead, and one where a finite share of all links piles onto the fittest. Which one occurs depends only on the fitness distribution (Section 6.4).

  • It explains winner-takes-all networks, which no earlier model produces.
  • One integral of the fitness distribution decides the phase.
  • It is also a case where the book's claim was later corrected: who exactly holds the condensate.

Bose–Einstein condensation (Section 6.4)

The fitness model maps exactly onto a Bose gas:

NetworkBose gas
node $i$ with fitness $\eta_i$energy level $\varepsilon_i = -\frac{1}{\beta_T}\ln\eta_i$ (fitter = lower)
a link pointing to node $i$a particle on level $\varepsilon_i$
a new node with $m$ linksa new level, and $m$ new particles

Then $k_i = m(t/t_i)^{f(\varepsilon_i)}$ with $f(\varepsilon) = e^{-\beta_T(\varepsilon - \mu)}$, and the self-consistency condition (6.5) becomes the equation for the chemical potential $\mu$ of a Bose gas:

$$\int g(\varepsilon)\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta_T(\varepsilon - \mu)} - 1}\,d\varepsilon = 1 .$$

$g(\varepsilon)$ is the density of levels (set by $\rho(\eta)$) and the fraction is the Bose occupation number. The "temperature" $1/\beta_T$ is a dummy: it cancels, and only $\rho(\eta)$ decides the phase.

  • Fit-get-rich (the equation for $C$ has a solution with $C > \eta_{\max}$): the fittest nodes become the largest hubs, closely trailed by the next fittest. Each holds a vanishing share of the links; the network is scale-free.
  • Bose–Einstein condensation (no such solution): $C$ sticks at $\eta_{\max}$ and the nodes of finite fitness absorb only part of the new links. The rest, a finite fraction, condenses on the fittest nodes: winner-takes-all, a hub-and-spoke network that is no longer scale-free.

With $\eta_{\max} = 1$, condensation happens when even $C = 1$ leaves the integral short of 1:

$$I = \int \frac{\eta\,\rho(\eta)}{1 - \eta}\,d\eta < 1, \qquad \text{condensate share} = 1 - I .$$

Worked example 7: when does a network condense? Take $\rho(\eta) = (1 + \zeta)(1 - \eta)^{\zeta}$, which puts less weight near $\eta = 1$ as $\zeta$ grows.

  1. $I = (1+\zeta)\int_0^1 \eta (1-\eta)^{\zeta - 1} d\eta = (1 + \zeta)\,\frac{1}{\zeta(\zeta+1)} = \frac{1}{\zeta}$.
  2. $I < 1$ exactly when $\zeta > 1$. So $\zeta = 0.1$ is fit-get-rich and $\zeta = 10$ condenses, with share $1 - 1/10 = 90\%$.
  3. Uniform fitness ($\zeta = 0$): $\int_0^1 \frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\,d\eta$ diverges at $\eta = 1$, so $I = \infty$: no condensation.

Paradox: fewer very fit nodes means condensation. When fit nodes are rare, the one that does arrive has no competitors.

Who holds the condensate? The book, following the 2001 physics paper, says the fittest node keeps a finite fraction of all links. For the tree version of the model ($m = 1$) this was later proved false: the condensation is non-extensive. The condensed share is spread over a succession of ever fitter latecomers, each of which is eventually overtaken, so the largest hub's share still vanishes, very slowly. The condition $I < 1$ and the share $1 - I$ are unaffected; only "one node takes it all" is. The simulations in the figure above show it: even at $10^6$ nodes the top hub of a $\zeta = 10$ network holds a few percent, not 90%.

According to the book, what is true of a winner-takes-all (condensed) network of $10^6$ nodes?

  1. The top hub's share of links vanishes
  2. A finite fraction of all links goes to the fittest nodes
  3. Hubs link only to hubs
  4. Degrees are equal
The analogue of Bose–Einstein condensation: a finite fraction $1 - I$ of the particles sits in the lowest levels. (Later mathematics showed that no single node keeps that fraction forever; it passes to ever fitter latecomers.)

The fitness distribution is $\rho(\eta) = 4(1 - \eta)^3$, i.e. $\zeta = 3$. Which phase is the network in?

  1. Fit-get-rich: scale-free, no dominant share
  2. Condensed: the fittest nodes take a finite share, $1 - 1/3 = 67\%$, of new links

  4. No giant hub can form
