How (not) to plot a degree distribution

A power law is a straight line on log-log axes, so it is tempting to plot the raw histogram and fit a line. The raw histogram hides the tail behind sampling noise. Advanced Topic 4.B gives two remedies: logarithmic bins and the cumulative distribution.

Four plots, one distribution

  • (a) On linear axes, everything beyond a few dozen links is squashed onto the axis.
  • (b) Log-log, one bin per degree: the tail flattens into a plateau at $p_k = 1/N$, where each degree is held by one node or none (then it cannot be plotted). Noise, not physics.
  • (c) Logarithmic bins grow with $k$, so each one collects enough nodes, and the estimate follows the line far into the tail.
  • (d) The cumulative distribution needs no bins and is again a straight line, with slope $-(\gamma - 1)$.

Logarithmic binning

With $b$ bins per decade, bin $n$ starts at $k_n \approx 10^{n/b}$, so each bin is a factor $10^{1/b}$ wider than the one before. The estimate in bin $n$ is

$$p_{k_n} = \frac{N_n}{N\,(k_{n+1} - k_n)}.$$
$b$
bins per decade (factor of 10 in $k$)
$k_n$
lower edge of bin $n$
$k_{n+1} - k_n$
bin width (number of integer degrees it covers)
$N_n$
number of nodes with degree in bin $n$
$N$
number of nodes

Each point is drawn at the geometric centre of its bin. Change $b$ in the figure: too few bins and the curve is coarse, too many and the noise returns.

Bin 100–199 holds 50 of $N = 10^5$ nodes: $p_k \approx 50/(10^5 \times 100) = 5\times10^{-6}$.

The cumulative distribution

The complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF) is the share of nodes with at least $k$ links:

$$P_k = P(K \ge k) = \sum_{q \ge k} p_q \sim k^{-(\gamma - 1)}.$$
$P_k$
share of nodes with degree $\ge k$
$q$
summation index over degrees $k, k+1, \dots$
$\gamma - 1$
CCDF exponent, one less than the $p_k$ exponent
  • Pro: every node contributes, there is no choice of bins to make, and the curve is smooth even where some degrees are missing.
  • Con: neighbouring points share data, so a least-squares fit to it still gives no reliable error bars.

Where the plateau comes from

Never fit a straight line to a linearly binned log-log histogram. The plateau pulls the slope towards zero, and the result depends on how far into the tail the fit reaches. On the next page it gives $\hat\gamma \approx 1.6$ for a sample with $\gamma = 2.5$.

  • A plot is the first test of whether a power law is plausible at all.
  • Log bins and the CCDF show the tail that the raw histogram hides.
  • Neither gives a reliable $\gamma$: that needs maximum likelihood (next page).

A log bin covers degrees 100 to 199 and holds 50 of $N = 10^5$ nodes. What is the estimate of $p_k$?

  1. $5\times10^{-4}$
  2. $0.5$
  3. $5\times10^{-6}$
  4. $50$
$p_k \approx N_n / (N \cdot \text{width}) = 50 / (10^5 \times 100) = 5\times10^{-6}$.

Why does a linearly binned log-log histogram of $N = 10^4$ nodes level off at $p_k = 10^{-4}$?

  1. The power law has a minimum value of $1/N$
  2. Each tail degree is held by at most one node, and $1/N$ is the smallest non-zero value
  3. Log axes stretch small values upwards
  4. Hubs are more common than predicted
With integer counts, a degree held by one node has $p_k = 1/N = 10^{-4}$; degrees held by none drop off the plot. The plateau is sampling noise.

The CCDF of a network is a straight line of slope $-1.5$ on log-log axes. What is $\gamma$?

  1. 2.5
  2. 1.5
  3. 0.5
  4. 3.5
$P(K\ge k) \sim k^{-(\gamma-1)}$, so $\gamma - 1 = 1.5$ and $\gamma = 2.5$.

You fit a least-squares line through a linearly binned log-log histogram of a sample whose true exponent is $\gamma = 2.5$. What do you expect?

  1. $\hat\gamma \approx 2.5$, with a reliable error bar
  2. $\hat\gamma$ well above 2.5
  3. $\hat\gamma$ random around 2.5
  4. $\hat\gamma$ well below 2.5, depending on the fitting range
The flat plateau pulls the slope towards zero: about 1.6 in the experiment on the next page.

With $b = 4$ bins per decade, by what factor is each log bin wider than the previous one?

  1. 4
  2. 2.5
  3. About 1.78
  4. 10
The factor is $10^{1/b} = 10^{1/4} \approx 1.78$.

Why do logarithmic bins estimate the tail better than linear bins?

  1. Wide bins at large $k$ collect many nodes, which averages out the noise
  2. They remove the hubs from the data
  3. They change the exponent to $\gamma - 1$
  4. They make the distribution Poisson
Dividing each bin's count by its width keeps the estimate of $p_k$ unbiased while the counts stay large.

A least-squares line through the CCDF comes with a small standard error. Can you trust that error bar?

  1. Yes, every point is independent
  2. No: neighbouring CCDF points share data, so the error is underestimated
  3. Yes, if the line is straight
  4. No, because the CCDF has the wrong slope
Least-squares errors assume independent points. Maximum likelihood gives an honest error bar.
