The data: eight real networks

The book tests every idea on ten "reference networks" (Table 2.1). The notebook and this site use eight of them, from biology, technology, society and information; the actor and citation networks are too large for a classroom. Measuring the same quantities across such different systems is how we learn which properties are general.

Explore a network

Compare the power grid with the WWW or email. The grid is sparse and almost lattice-like, no node above 19 links, and its degree distribution is close to the Poisson curve. WWW and email are dominated by a few hubs, orders of magnitude above Poisson in the tail. Chapters 3 and 4 explain the difference.

How the data were prepared

  1. Edge lists from the book's website (networks.zip, 141 MB).
  2. Each network made undirected and simple, as in the notebook: directions ignored, self-loops and repeated links removed, nodes relabelled 0…N−1.
  3. tools/preprocess.py computes degree histograms, components, clustering, distances, assortativity, power-law fits and null-model counterparts as small JSON files. The six smaller networks also ship as full edge lists (about 1 MB), so pages can analyse them live; the Internet and WWW (0.6 and 1.1 million links) ship as summaries only.

Five of the eight networks are directed in reality. What information is lost when we treat an email network as undirected? Which questions could no longer be answered?

Direction tells who initiates contact: mailing lists send far more than they receive, popular addresses receive far more than they send. One undirected degree merges both, and reciprocated pairs collapse into one link. Questions about information flow, reachability ("can a message get from A to B?") or the book's separate in- and out-degree exponents need the directed network.
  • Real data are the test every model must pass.
  • Eight domains with the same measurements show which properties are shared (sparsity, short distances, hubs) and which are not (the grid).
  • Part I defines each column of the table.
  • Part II compares every column with a random network of the same $N$ and $L$ (Real networks are not random).
  • Part III fits the degree tails with power laws (Estimating the exponent).
