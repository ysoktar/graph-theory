The data: eight real networks

Throughout the book, ten "reference networks" serve as test cases (Table 2.1). The class notebook, and this site, use eight of them, spanning biology, technology, society and information. The other two, the actor and citation networks, are too large for a classroom.

Explore a network

Compare the power grid with the WWW or the email network. The grid is a sparse, almost lattice-like web with no node above 19 links. The WWW and email samples are dominated by a few hubs. The degree distributions tell the same story: close to the Poisson curve for the grid, orders of magnitude above it in the tail for the others. Explaining that difference is the job of Chapters 3 and 4.

How the data were prepared

  1. The edge lists come from the book's website (networks.zip, 141 MB).
  2. Each network is made undirected and simple, as in the notebook: link directions are ignored, and self-loops and repeated links are removed. Nodes are relabelled 0…N−1.
  3. The script tools/preprocess.py (in the repository) computes degree histograms, components, clustering, distances, assortativity, power-law fits and null-model counterparts, and writes them as small JSON files. The six smaller networks also ship as full edge lists, about 1 MB in total, so pages can analyse them live in your browser. The Internet and the WWW, with 0.6 and 1.1 million links, ship as summaries only.

Five of the eight networks are directed in reality. What information is lost when we treat an email network as undirected? Which questions could no longer be answered?

Direction tells who initiates contact. Spammers and mailing lists send far more than they receive; popular addresses receive far more than they send. An undirected network merges both into one degree, so it cannot tell broadcasters from receivers, and reciprocated pairs collapse into a single link. Questions about information flow, reachability ("can a message get from A to B?") or the in- and out-degree exponents reported in the book need the directed network.
