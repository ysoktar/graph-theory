Real networks are sparse

In a network of $N$ nodes every pair could be linked, giving at most $L_{\max} = N(N-1)/2$ links. Real networks use only a tiny fraction of them, and the fraction shrinks as networks grow.

$$L_{\max} = \binom{N}{2} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2}, \qquad \text{density} = \frac{L}{L_{\max}} = \frac{\langle k\rangle}{N-1}.$$

A network with $L \approx L_{\max}$ is dense (in the extreme, a complete graph, $\langle k\rangle = N - 1$). A network with $L \ll L_{\max}$ is sparse. Every network in the book is sparse. Their average degree stays small, between about 3 and 10 in our data, while $N$ ranges over more than two orders of magnitude.

The eight reference networks

The real networks lie four to five orders of magnitude below the $L_{\max}$ line. Along the dashed line every pair of nodes is linked; the real networks run parallel to the grey lines of constant $\langle k\rangle$ instead, so $L$ grows like $N$, not like $N^2$.

What sparsity looks like

The adjacency matrix of the science collaboration network has $23{,}133^2 \approx 5.35\times 10^8$ entries, of which only $2L = 186{,}878$ are non-zero (0.035%).

Why it matters: storing a network

A dense matrix needs $N^2$ entries, sparse or not. An edge list or a sparse matrix (compressed rows: an index array of $N+1$ offsets and the $2L$ neighbour lists) needs memory proportional to $L$.

Why do we store networks as edge lists or sparse matrices? How much memory would a dense matrix need for the WWW sample ($N \approx 3\times10^5$), or for Facebook ($N \approx 3\times10^9$)? (Notebook I.3.)

For the WWW sample, $N^2 \approx 1.1\times10^{11}$ entries: about 13 GB even at one bit per entry, and 106 GB at one byte. Its 1.1 million links fit in an edge list of about 9 MB. For $N = 3\times10^9$, $N^2 = 9\times10^{18}$ entries is about an exabyte at one bit each, more than any computer holds, while an edge list with $\langle k\rangle \approx 200$ needs a few terabytes.
