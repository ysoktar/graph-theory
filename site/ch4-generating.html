Generating networks with a given degree distribution

To ask which properties of a real network follow from its degrees alone, we need random networks that share those degrees and nothing else: a null model. Section 4.8 gives two ways to build them.

The configuration model

Give node $i$ exactly $k_i$ stubs (half-links), then pair all stubs uniformly at random. Every network with the given degree sequence is then equally likely, but self-loops and repeated links can appear. The probability that $i$ and $j$ are linked is approximately

$$p_{ij} \approx \frac{k_i k_j}{2L}.$$
$p_{ij}$
probability of a link between nodes $i$ and $j$
$k_i, k_j$
degrees of $i$ and $j$
$L$
number of links; $2L$ = number of stubs
  1. A stub of $i$ picks a partner among the other $\approx 2L$ stubs.
  2. $k_j$ of them belong to $j$: chance $k_j/2L$.
  3. $i$ has $k_i$ stubs: $p_{ij} \approx k_i k_j / 2L$.

$k_i = 100$, $k_j = 50$, $L = 10^4$: $p_{ij} = 5{,}000/20{,}000 = 0.25$. Two degree-2 nodes: $4/20{,}000 = 0.0002$.

Degree-preserving randomization

Start from the real network and repeatedly swap the ends of two random links ($A$–$B$, $C$–$D$ → $A$–$D$, $C$–$B$). Every swap keeps all degrees exactly; after enough swaps all other structure is scrambled. Unlike the configuration model, no link is ever lost.

What the degree sequence explains

  • The degree sequence fixes $k_{\max}$ and $\langle k^2\rangle$, which $G(N, L)$ gets badly wrong.
  • Randomized networks are usually smaller than both the real network and $G(N, L)$: hubs act as shortcuts, while real networks spend many links inside tightly knit groups.
  • Collaboration: $\langle C\rangle$ falls from 0.63 to 0.002 and the assortativity from 0.13 to 0. Triangles and degree correlations come from other mechanisms: teams, communities (Ch 9), homophily (Ch 7).

Compare a network with its degree-preserving randomization: whatever survives is explained by the degrees; whatever disappears needs another explanation.

  • A null model tells whether a property is surprising given the degrees.
  • $G(N, L)$ is the wrong baseline for heavy-tailed networks: it destroys the hubs.
  • Lets us generate scale-free networks of any size to test the predictions of this chapter.
  • Generalises the random network: $G(N, L)$ fixes only $L$, the configuration model every $k_i$.
  • Null model for distances, clustering and degree correlations.
  • Does not explain where the degrees come from: that is the job of growth models.
