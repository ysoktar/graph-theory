The eight networks at a glance

Key messages

  1. Random networks are the null model. They get the existence of a giant component and the small-world property right, and fail on hubs and clustering.
  2. Real degree distributions are heavy-tailed. Plot them with log-binning or the CCDF, and fit them by maximum likelihood with a goodness-of-fit test, never by least squares on a raw histogram.
  3. Heavy tails mean a large $\langle k^2\rangle$ and no characteristic scale. This, rather than the exact functional form of the tail, drives robustness (Ch 8) and spreading (Ch 10).
  4. Degree-preserving randomization separates what the degree sequence explains from what it does not.
  5. Check everything: software defaults (powerlaw's $\gamma \le 3$), approximations (the closed-form MLE, the rounding sampler, the continuous CCDF), and generalisations from a single network.

Self-test

A network has $N = 10^4$ and $\langle k\rangle = 2$. Without further information, which statement is most likely true if it were a random network?

  1. It has no giant component
  2. It is connected
  3. It has a giant component holding about 80% of the nodes
  4. It has a hub with about 100 links
$\langle k\rangle = 2 > 1$: supercritical. $S = 1 - e^{-2S}$ gives $S \approx 0.80$. It is not connected, since $\langle k\rangle < \ln N \approx 9.2$, and its largest degree would be about 9.

On a log-log plot of the linearly binned $p_k$, the tail flattens into a horizontal line. What is the best explanation?

  1. The degree distribution has a cutoff
  2. Degrees in the tail are held by single nodes, so $p_k = 1/N$ for each of them
  3. The network is assortative
  4. The exponent is close to 2
It is sampling noise. Log-binning or the CCDF removes it.

A fit gives $\hat\gamma = 2.95$ with $k_{\min} = 36$ when $\gamma$ is restricted to $\le 3$, and $\hat\gamma = 3.40$ with $k_{\min} = 70$ without the restriction. Which is the result to report?

  1. 2.95, because scale-free networks have $\gamma < 3$
  2. The average, 3.18
  3. Neither; least squares should be used instead
  4. 3.40: the restriction excluded the best-fitting exponents
A fitting range is an assumption, not a result. The unrestricted fit has a smaller KS distance; the restricted one only reports the best fit among the exponents it was allowed to try.

After degree-preserving randomization, a network's average clustering drops from 0.6 to 0.002 while its average distance drops from 5.3 to 4.4. What do you conclude?

  1. Clustering is not explained by the degrees; the real network's triangles make paths longer
  2. The randomization was not degree-preserving
  3. The network is a random network
  4. Distances are explained by clustering alone
Whatever disappears under degree-preserving randomization is not a consequence of the degree sequence. The randomized network is smaller because links that formed triangles now act as shortcuts.

Which network is most robust to the random failure of 50% of its nodes?

  1. A random network with $\langle k\rangle = 1.5$
  2. A square lattice
  3. A scale-free network with $\gamma = 2.3$ and the same $\langle k\rangle$ as the others
  4. A ring
Its $\langle k^2\rangle$ is large, so the Molloy–Reed threshold $f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa - 1)$ is close to 1. A random network with $\langle k\rangle = 1.5$ breaks at $f_c = 1/3$.
