Communities

Real networks consist of groups densely linked inside and sparsely linked to each other: research fields, friend circles, cell modules. Chapter 9 finds them from the links alone.

  • Degrees say how many links; communities say who links to whom at a larger scale.
  • They reveal function: proteins that work together, where a group may split.
  • The random models of Chapters 3–5 have none, so we need a test that separates real groups from chance clumps.

Modularity (Section 9.4)

A community has more internal links than random wiring with the same degrees (the configuration model) would give it. Modularity scores a partition by that excess:

$$Q = \sum_{c} \left[\frac{L_c}{L} - \left(\frac{k_c}{2L}\right)^2\right].$$
$c$
a community of the partition
$L$
total number of links
$L_c$
links inside $c$
$k_c$
total degree of the nodes in $c$
$(k_c/2L)^2$
expected fraction of links inside $c$ under random wiring (both ends land in $c$)

Observed minus expected: $Q = 0$ for one all-inclusive group, $Q < 0$ for fewer internal links than chance, $Q < 1$ always.

Karate club, $L = 78$: factions with $(L_c, k_c) = (35, 81)$ and $(32, 75)$. $Q = (0.449 - 0.270) + (0.410 - 0.231) = 0.358$.

Spectral bisection misplaces only members 2 and 8. Louvain finds four groups with $Q = 0.420$, above the real split: maximising $Q$ is not the same as predicting the split.

Algorithms

  • Spectral bisection: choose node values $x_i$ minimising $\sum_{\text{links}} (x_i - x_j)^2$; the solution is the Laplacian's Fiedler vector (Embeddings). Its sign splits the nodes.
  • Label propagation (Raghavan, Albert and Kumara 2007): each node adopts its neighbours' most common label until stable. Fast; random.
  • Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008): move nodes to raise $Q$, merge communities into super-nodes, repeat. The standard for large networks.

Limits of modularity

Resolution limit (Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007). Merging communities $A$, $B$ joined by $l_{AB}$ links changes $Q$ by

$$\Delta Q = \frac{l_{AB}}{L} - \frac{k_A\,k_B}{2L^2}.$$
$l_{AB}$
links between $A$ and $B$
$k_A, k_B$
their total degrees

With $l_{AB} = 1$, merging raises $Q$ whenever $k_A k_B < 2L$, i.e. $k < \sqrt{2L}$: small groups are merged however separate they are.

Collaboration network: $L = 93{,}439$, $\sqrt{2L} \approx 432$. A group of 40 scientists with 8 links each ($k = 320$) falls below it.

Random networks score high too (Guimerà, Sales-Pardo and Amaral 2004): see the table above.

A high $Q$ alone is no proof of communities. Compare with $Q$ of a random network of the same size and density.

  • Null model: the configuration model, as for degree correlations.
  • Explains high clustering: your friends know each other because they share a group.
  • Special case of a spectral embedding: bisection uses one eigenvector; more give coordinates.
  • Slows spreading: few links cross between groups.

Every node is its own community. What is $Q$?

  1. Slightly negative
  2. Exactly 1
  3. Exactly 0
  4. Undefined
$L_c = 0$, so $Q = -\sum_i (k_i/2L)^2$; $-0.050$ for the karate club.

$L = 10$, two groups, each with 4 internal links and total degree 10. What is $Q$?

  1. 0.8
  2. 0.15
  3. 0.3
  4. 0
Each group: $0.4 - (10/20)^2 = 0.15$; $Q = 0.3$.

Why subtract $(k_c/2L)^2$?

  1. To keep $Q$ in $[-1, 1]$.
  2. Otherwise one big group would score best.
  3. To favour small groups.
  4. To count triangles.
The internal fraction alone is 1 for the trivial partition. Subtracting the chance fraction rewards only surprising concentration.

$L = 5{,}000$. Two separate groups, total degree 50 each, share one link. Does maximising $Q$ merge them?

  1. No: one link is too few.
  2. It cannot decide.
  3. It splits them into singletons.
  4. Yes: $k_A k_B = 2{,}500 < 2L$.
$\Delta Q = 0.0002 - 0.00005 > 0$; both lie below $\sqrt{2L} = 100$.

Louvain finds $Q = 0.3$. What to check before claiming communities?

  1. A second algorithm's $Q$.
  2. $Q$ of a random network with the same $N$ and $L$.
  3. That $p_k$ is a power law.
  4. That $Q > 0.5$.
Sparse random networks reach $Q \approx 0.3$ or more under optimisation; only a $Q$ well above that baseline is evidence.
