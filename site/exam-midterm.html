The chapters form one argument:

  1. Ch 2 gives the measurements: degree, distance, clustering.
  2. Ch 3 asks what pure chance gives: the random network is the null model. It gets giant components and short paths right, hubs and clustering wrong.
  3. Ch 4 describes the hubs: power laws, a huge $\langle k^2\rangle$, ultra-short paths.
  4. Ch 5 explains where power laws come from: growth plus preferential attachment.

Ch 1 · Why networks?

No formulas; expect short-answer questions. The chapter says why the rest matters.

Must know

  • Complex system: collective behaviour hard to predict from the parts. Behind each one is a network (cell, brain, society, Internet, power grid).
  • Cascading failure: a failure shifts load to neighbours, which may fail in turn (2003 blackout: 45 million people in eight US states plus 10 million in Ontario).
  • Interconnectivity brings benefits (cheap power, fast information) and vulnerability (failures and viruses travel).
  • Two forces behind the field around 2000: network maps became available; their properties were universal.
  • Network science vs. graph theory: empirical and data-driven; also interdisciplinary, quantitative, computational.

Exam-style problems

Problem. Describe the 2003 blackout as a network process: what are the nodes, the links and the mechanism? Using this picture, explain why inspecting each power line on its own cannot predict how far a blackout will spread.

  1. Nodes: generators, substations, consumers. Links: transmission lines.
  2. Mechanism: a line fails; its load moves to neighbouring lines; overloaded lines fail and move the load further. The cascade's size depends on where it starts and on the nodes' capacities.
  3. Each line's fate depends on its neighbours' loads, so how far a failure travels is set by the wiring and by the flow on it, not by any single line. Predicting it needs the network's structure, the dynamics on it and their combination: robustness (Ch 8).

Problem. Erdős and Rényi modelled networks as random in 1959. In a random network the degrees follow a Poisson distribution, $p_k = e^{-\langle k\rangle}\langle k\rangle^k/k!$, which makes nodes far above $\langle k\rangle$ practically impossible. Around 2000, maps of the WWW, the Internet and metabolism became available, with hubs of thousands of links. What did this contradiction imply, and why could it not be seen before 2000?

  1. A Poisson network with $\langle k\rangle \approx 7$ essentially never has a node with even 50 links; real maps had nodes with thousands. So real networks are not random: some mechanism builds hubs (Ch 4–5).
  2. Seeing a tail needs networks with thousands to millions of nodes. Before digital maps (around 2000), studied networks had tens or hundreds of nodes, too few for any tail to show.
  3. The same heavy tails then appeared in very different systems (universality), so one theory could cover them all. That is when network science became a field.

Problem. Model a city's metro system as a network. What are the nodes and links? Directed, weighted? Name one question the network answers that a list of stations cannot.

  1. Nodes: stations. Links: track between consecutive stations.
  2. Undirected (trains run both ways); weights could be travel time.
  3. Questions: fewest stops between two stations (distance); which station closure disconnects the system (bridges, robustness).

Common mistakes

  • Saying network science started in 2000. Its roots (Euler, Erdős–Rényi, sociology) are old; the field emerged then.
  • Treating a network as a list of its parts. The questions network science answers (paths, bridges, cascades, spreading) are about the links between the parts.

Ch 2 · Graph theory

The vocabulary and measurements used everywhere later. Expect hand computation on a small network.

Must know

Degree and average degree.

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} k_i = 2L, \qquad \langle k\rangle = \frac{2L}{N}$$
$k_i$
degree of node $i$: its number of links
$N$, $L$
number of nodes, number of links
$\langle k\rangle$
average degree

Consequence: the number of odd-degree nodes is even. Directed: $k_i = k_i^{\text{in}} + k_i^{\text{out}}$ and $\langle k^{\text{in}}\rangle = \langle k^{\text{out}}\rangle = L/N$.

Degree distribution and moments.

$$p_k = \frac{N_k}{N}, \qquad \sum_k p_k = 1, \qquad \langle k^n\rangle = \sum_k k^n p_k$$
$N_k$
number of nodes with degree $k$
$p_k$
fraction of nodes with degree $k$ (probability that a random node has degree $k$)
$\langle k^n\rangle$
$n$-th moment; $n=1$ is $\langle k\rangle$, $n = 2$ measures spread

Adjacency matrix.

$$A_{ij} = \begin{cases}1 & i\text{–}j \text{ linked}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}, \qquad k_i = \sum_j A_{ij}, \qquad \#\triangle = \tfrac16\operatorname{Tr}A^3$$
$A_{ij}$
entry in row $i$, column $j$; symmetric if undirected
$(A^n)_{ij}$
number of walks of length $n$ from $i$ to $j$
$\operatorname{Tr}A^3$
sum of the diagonal of $A^3$: closed 3-step walks; each triangle is counted 6 times (3 starts × 2 directions)

Sparsity.

$$L_{\max} = \binom{N}{2} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2}, \qquad \text{real networks: } L \ll L_{\max}$$
$L_{\max}$
links of the complete network (every pair linked once)

So store edge lists, not $N\times N$ matrices.

Weighted and bipartite. Weighted: $A_{ij} = w_{ij}$, the link's strength. Bipartite: two node types, links only between types. Its projection links two nodes of one type if they share a neighbour; the number shared is the weight.

Paths. $d_{ij}$ is the number of links on the shortest path from $i$ to $j$ (found by breadth-first search, BFS). Diameter $d_{\max}$: the largest $d_{ij}$.

$$\langle d\rangle = \frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i \ne j} d_{ij}$$
$\langle d\rangle$
average distance over all ordered pairs (connected pairs only)

Connectedness. A component is a maximal set of mutually reachable nodes; the largest is the giant component. A bridge is a link whose removal splits a component.

Clustering.

$$C_i = \frac{2L_i}{k_i(k_i-1)}, \qquad \langle C\rangle = \frac1N\sum_i C_i, \qquad C_\Delta = \frac{3\times\#\triangle}{\#\text{connected triples}}$$
$L_i$
links among the $k_i$ neighbours of node $i$
$k_i(k_i-1)/2$
neighbour pairs of node $i$; $C_i$ is the linked share
$\langle C\rangle$
average local clustering
$C_\Delta$
global clustering (transitivity); a connected triple is a node with two of its neighbours

Euler (Königsberg). A connected network has a path using every link once if and only if it has 0 or 2 odd-degree nodes.

What this lets you do: describe any network with a few numbers and compare networks of any kind.

Exam-style problems

Problem. Two triangles joined by one link: links 1–2, 1–3, 2–3, 3–4, 4–5, 4–6, 5–6. Find (a) degrees, $\langle k\rangle$, $p_k$; (b) $L/L_{\max}$; (c) every $C_i$, $\langle C\rangle$ and $C_\Delta$; (d) $d_{\max}$ and $\langle d\rangle$; (e) the bridges; (f) whether an Euler path exists.

  1. Degrees: $k_1 = k_2 = k_5 = k_6 = 2$, $k_3 = k_4 = 3$. Sum $14 = 2L$ ✓. $\langle k\rangle = 14/6 = 2.33$. $p_2 = 4/6 = 0.67$, $p_3 = 2/6 = 0.33$.
  2. $L_{\max} = 6\cdot5/2 = 15$; $L/L_{\max} = 7/15 = 0.47$.
  3. Nodes 1, 2, 5, 6: both neighbours linked, $C = 1$. Node 3: neighbours 1, 2, 4; only 1–2 linked: $C_3 = 2\cdot1/(3\cdot2) = 1/3$. Same for node 4. $\langle C\rangle = (4 + 2/3)/6 = 0.78$.
  4. Triangles: 2. Triples: $\sum_i k_i(k_i-1)/2 = 4\cdot1 + 2\cdot3 = 10$. $C_\Delta = 3\cdot2/10 = 0.6$. (Smaller than $\langle C\rangle$: the low-degree nodes with $C = 1$ weigh more in $\langle C\rangle$.)
  5. Distances: 7 pairs at 1 (the links), 4 at 2 (1–4, 2–4, 3–5, 3–6), 4 at 3 (1–5, 1–6, 2–5, 2–6). $d_{\max} = 3$; $\langle d\rangle = (7 + 8 + 12)/15 = 1.8$.
  6. Bridge: 3–4 only. Every other link lies on a triangle.
  7. Odd-degree nodes: 3 and 4. Exactly two, so an Euler path exists, from 3 to 4.

Problem. Same network. Without multiplying matrices, find $(A^2)_{11}$, $(A^3)_{14}$ and $\operatorname{Tr}A^3$.

  1. $(A^2)_{11}$ counts 2-step walks 1 → $x$ → 1: one per neighbour. So $(A^2)_{ii} = k_i = 2$.
  2. $(A^3)_{14}$: walks 1 → $x$ → $y$ → 4 with $y \in \{3,5,6\}$. From 1, $x \in \{2,3\}$. $x = 2$ gives $y = 3$ ✓. $x = 3$ needs $y$ adjacent to both 3 and 4: none. Answer 1 (the walk 1–2–3–4).
  3. $\operatorname{Tr}A^3 = 6 \times \#\triangle = 6 \times 2 = 12$.

Problem. (a) Can a network have degrees 3, 3, 3, 1, 1? (b) Can a simple network have degrees 3, 3, 1, 1?

  1. (a) Sum $= 11$, odd. A sum of degrees is $2L$, always even. Impossible.
  2. (b) Sum $= 8$, even, so $L = 4$ passes the first test. But each degree-3 node must link to all three others. Then each degree-1 node gets a link from both degree-3 nodes: degree 2, not 1. Impossible.
  3. Lesson: an even sum is necessary, not sufficient.

Problem. The WWW sample (undirected, simple) has $N = 325{,}729$ and $L = 1{,}090{,}108$. Compute $\langle k\rangle$ and $L/L_{\max}$. How many numbers does an adjacency matrix store, and how many an edge list?

  1. $\langle k\rangle = 2L/N = 2{,}180{,}216/325{,}729 = 6.69$.
  2. $L_{\max} = N(N-1)/2 = 5.30\times10^{10}$; $L/L_{\max} = 2.1\times10^{-5}$. Sparse.
  3. Matrix: $N^2 = 1.06\times10^{11}$ entries, almost all 0. Edge list: $2L \approx 2.2\times10^6$ numbers, about 50,000 times fewer.

Problem. People A, B, C, D attend events E1 = {A, B, C}, E2 = {B, C, D}, E3 = {C, D}. Draw the person projection with weights. Which pair is closest?

  1. Bipartite degrees: A 1, B 2, C 3, D 2; events 3, 3, 2. Both sum to 8 = $L$ ✓.
  2. Shared events: A–B 1, A–C 1, B–C 2 (E1, E2), B–D 1, C–D 2 (E2, E3); A–D 0.
  3. Closest: B–C and C–D (weight 2). Projection degrees: A 2, B 3, C 3, D 2. The projection has triangles (A, B, C) made by one event alone: projections inflate clustering.

Common mistakes

  • $\langle k\rangle = L/N$: wrong for undirected networks; each link adds to two degrees.
  • Confusing $\langle C\rangle$ (average of node values) with $C_\Delta$ (ratio of totals): they differ.
  • Using $k_i^2$ instead of $k_i(k_i-1)$ in $C_i$; nodes with $k_i < 2$ have no $C_i$ (taken as 0 by NetworkX).
  • Averaging distances over disconnected pairs: $d_{ij} = \infty$; average over connected pairs.

Ch 3 · Random networks

Why we need it: the null model. It says what chance alone produces, so we can tell which features of real networks need an explanation.

Must know

$G(N,p)$: $N$ nodes; each of the $L_{\max}$ pairs is linked independently with probability $p$. ($G(N,L)$: exactly $L$ links placed at random; same results for large $N$.)

$$\langle L\rangle = p\,\frac{N(N-1)}{2}, \qquad \langle k\rangle = p(N-1)$$
$p$
probability that a given pair is linked
$\langle L\rangle$
expected number of links ($L$ itself is binomial)
$N-1$
possible partners of one node

Degrees: binomial, then Poisson.

$$p_k = \binom{N-1}{k}p^k(1-p)^{N-1-k} \;\approx\; e^{-\langle k\rangle}\frac{\langle k\rangle^k}{k!}, \qquad \sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$$
$\binom{N-1}{k}$
ways to choose which $k$ of the $N-1$ others are neighbours
$e^{-\langle k\rangle}\langle k\rangle^k/k!$
Poisson limit, valid for $\langle k\rangle \ll N$; depends only on $\langle k\rangle$
$\sigma_k$
standard deviation of the degrees; $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1+\langle k\rangle)$

Degrees cluster around $\langle k\rangle$; $k_{\max}$ grows very slowly with $N$. No hubs.

Giant component.

$$S = 1 - e^{-\langle k\rangle S}$$
$S$
fraction of nodes in the giant component; $S > 0$ only if $\langle k\rangle > 1$
RegimeConditionLargest component
Subcritical$\langle k\rangle < 1$small, $\sim \ln N$
Critical$\langle k\rangle = 1$$\sim N^{2/3}$
Supercritical$1 < \langle k\rangle < \ln N$giant ($SN$) plus small pieces
Connected$\langle k\rangle > \ln N$everything

Small world.

$$\langle d\rangle \approx \frac{\ln N}{\ln\langle k\rangle}$$
$\ln$
natural logarithm. Reason: about $\langle k\rangle^d$ nodes lie within $d$ steps; set $\langle k\rangle^d = N$

Clustering.

$$C = p = \frac{\langle k\rangle}{N-1}$$
$C$
neighbours of a node are linked with the same probability $p$ as any pair; independent of $k$, vanishes as $N$ grows

Watts–Strogatz: rewire a ring lattice with probability $p$; a few shortcuts give short paths while keeping high clustering.

Scorecard: right about giant components and short paths; wrong about hubs (degrees far too narrow) and clustering (far too low).

Exam-style problems

Problem. $G(N,p)$ with $N = 1{,}000$, $p = 0.005$. Find $\langle L\rangle$, $\langle k\rangle$, $\sigma_k$, the regime, $S$ (one or two iterations), $\langle d\rangle$ and $C$.

  1. $\langle L\rangle = 0.005 \times 499{,}500 = 2{,}497.5$.
  2. $\langle k\rangle = 0.005 \times 999 = 4.995 \approx 5$; $\sigma_k = \sqrt5 = 2.24$.
  3. $\ln N = 6.91$. Since $1 < 5 < 6.91$: supercritical. A giant component, but not connected.
  4. Iterate $S \leftarrow 1 - e^{-5S}$ from $S = 1$: $1 - e^{-5} = 0.9933$, then $1 - e^{-4.966} = 0.9930$. So $S \approx 0.993$: about 7 nodes outside.
  5. $\langle d\rangle \approx 6.91/\ln 5 = 6.91/1.609 = 4.3$.
  6. $C = p = 0.005$.

Problem. Same network. How many isolated nodes do you expect? Compare with $1 - S$. How likely is a node with $k = 10$?

  1. $p_0 = e^{-5} = 0.0068$; $Np_0 = 6.8$ isolated nodes.
  2. $1 - S = 0.007$, i.e. 7 nodes: almost everything outside the giant component is an isolated node.
  3. $p_{10} = e^{-5}\,5^{10}/10! = 0.018$: about 18 nodes. Twice the mean is already rare.

Problem. If the WWW sample ($N = 325{,}729$, $\langle k\rangle = 6.69$) were random, how many nodes would have degree 100? The real sample's largest degree is 10,721. What does this show?

  1. $\log_{10} p_{100} = \log_{10}\!\big(e^{-6.69}\,6.69^{100}/100!\big) \approx -78$.
  2. Expected count $Np_{100} \approx 10^{-73}$: none, in any network that could ever exist.
  3. Real hubs are therefore not chance. The Poisson model must be replaced (Ch 4) and the hubs explained (Ch 5).

Problem. $N = 10^4$ nodes, links added at random. How many links until a giant component appears? Until the network is connected?

  1. Giant component at $\langle k\rangle = 1$: $L = N\langle k\rangle/2 = 5{,}000$.
  2. Connected at $\langle k\rangle = \ln N = 9.21$: $L = 10^4 \times 9.21/2 \approx 46{,}000$.
  3. One link per node already gives a giant component; full connection needs about nine times more.

Problem. The collaboration network has $N = 23{,}133$, $\langle k\rangle = 8.08$, measured $\langle C\rangle = 0.63$ and $\langle d\rangle \approx 5.4$ (this site's measurement). Compare with a random network of the same $N$ and $\langle k\rangle$. Which property does the random model explain?

  1. Random distance: $\ln 23{,}133/\ln 8.08 = 10.05/2.09 = 4.8$. Close to 5.4: the small world is explained by randomness.
  2. Random clustering: $8.08/23{,}133 = 3.5\times10^{-4}$. Real is about 1,800 times larger: not explained.
  3. Watts–Strogatz reproduces both; hubs remain unexplained (Ch 4–5).

Common mistakes

  • Giant component at $\langle k\rangle = \ln N$: no, that is connectedness; the giant component needs only $\langle k\rangle > 1$.
  • Using the Poisson form when $\langle k\rangle$ is comparable to $N$: then use the binomial.
  • Writing $\log_{10}$ in $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$: the ratio is the same in any base, but $\ln N$ in the regime test must be natural.

Ch 4 · The scale-free property

Why we need it: real degree distributions have heavy tails. The power law describes them and predicts hubs, a huge $\langle k^2\rangle$ and ultra-short paths.

Must know

Power law.

$$p_k \sim k^{-\gamma}, \qquad \ln p_k = -\gamma\ln k + \text{const}$$
$\gamma$
degree exponent; most real networks between 2 and 3. A straight line of slope $-\gamma$ on log–log axes
$\sim$
"proportional to, for large $k$"

Continuous form and its CCDF (complementary cumulative distribution):

$$p(k) = (\gamma-1)\,k_{\min}^{\gamma-1}\,k^{-\gamma}, \qquad P(K \ge k) = \Big(\frac{k}{k_{\min}}\Big)^{-(\gamma-1)}$$
$k_{\min}$
smallest degree from which the power law holds
$P(K \ge k)$
fraction of nodes with degree at least $k$; slope $-(\gamma-1)$ on log–log axes, needs no binning

Largest hub (natural cutoff).

$$k_{\max} = k_{\min}N^{1/(\gamma-1)} \qquad \text{vs. exponential } p_k: \; k_{\max} = k_{\min} + \frac{\ln N}{\lambda}$$
$k_{\max}$
degree beyond which we expect about one node: $N\,P(K \ge k_{\max}) \approx 1$
$\lambda$
decay rate of an exponential distribution $p_k \propto e^{-\lambda k}$

Moments.

$$\langle k^n\rangle = \frac{\gamma-1}{n-\gamma+1}\,k_{\min}^{\gamma-1}\big(k_{\max}^{\,n-\gamma+1} - k_{\min}^{\,n-\gamma+1}\big)$$
$n$
moment order; $\langle k^n\rangle$ grows with $k_{\max}$ (hence with $N$) when $n > \gamma - 1$
Regime$\langle k\rangle$$\langle k^2\rangle$$\langle d\rangle$
$\gamma = 2$divergesdivergesconst
$2 < \gamma < 3$ (scale-free)finitediverges$\ln\ln N$ (ultra-small)
$\gamma = 3$finite$\sim \ln N$$\ln N/\ln\ln N$
$\gamma > 3$finitefinite$\ln N$ (like random)

"Scale-free": with $\langle k^2\rangle \to \infty$, $\sigma_k \gg \langle k\rangle$. A random node's degree can be anything; the average sets no scale.

Plotting and fitting. Linear bins hide the tail in noise; use log bins or the CCDF. Least-squares on a log–log plot is biased; use maximum likelihood:

$$\hat\gamma \approx 1 + n\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\frac{k_i}{k_{\min}-\frac12}\Big]^{-1}, \qquad \sigma_{\hat\gamma} \approx \frac{\hat\gamma-1}{\sqrt n}$$
$n$
number of nodes in the tail, $k_i \ge k_{\min}$
$-\frac12$
correction for integer degrees (good for $k_{\min} \gtrsim 6$)
$\sigma_{\hat\gamma}$
standard error

$k_{\min}$ is chosen to minimise the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance $D$ between data and fit.

Generating networks with given degrees. Configuration model: give each node $k_i$ stubs, pair stubs at random.

$$p_{ij} \approx \frac{k_ik_j}{2L}$$
$p_{ij}$
expected number of links between $i$ and $j$; $2L$ is the number of stubs

Degree-preserving randomization swaps link ends: same degrees, everything else random. A null model for "what do the degrees alone explain?"

Exam-style problems

Problem. $N = 3\times10^5$, $k_{\min} = 1$. Estimate $k_{\max}$ for (a) an exponential $p_k$ with $\lambda = 1$, (b) a power law with $\gamma = 2.1$.

  1. (a) $k_{\max} = 1 + \ln(3\times10^5) = 1 + 12.6 = 13.6$.
  2. (b) $k_{\max} = N^{1/1.1} = e^{12.61/1.1} = e^{11.46} \approx 95{,}000$.
  3. Same size, same smallest degree: the power law allows a hub 7,000 times larger.

Problem. Power law with $\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 1$, $N = 10^6$. Find $k_{\max}$, $\langle k\rangle$, $\langle k^2\rangle$ and $\sigma_k$. Interpret.

  1. $k_{\max} = (10^6)^{1/1.5} = 10^4$.
  2. $\langle k\rangle = \frac{1.5}{-0.5}\big(10^{4\cdot(-0.5)} - 1\big) = -3\,(0.01 - 1) = 2.97$.
  3. $\langle k^2\rangle = \frac{1.5}{0.5}\big(10^{4\cdot 0.5} - 1\big) = 3 \times 99 = 297$.
  4. $\sigma_k = \sqrt{297 - 2.97^2} = \sqrt{288.2} = 17.0$.
  5. $\langle k\rangle = 3 \pm 17$: the average tells little. $\langle k^2\rangle$ keeps growing as $\sqrt{k_{\max}}$ with $N$. This ratio $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 100$ returns in robustness (Ch 8).

Problem. A CCDF has $P(K \ge 10) = 0.1$ and $P(K \ge 100) = 0.004$, on a straight log–log line. Estimate $\gamma$.

  1. Slope $= \log_{10}(0.004/0.1)/\log_{10}(100/10) = \log_{10}0.04 = -1.40$.
  2. CCDF slope is $-(\gamma - 1)$, so $\gamma = 2.4$.
  3. Common error: reading the CCDF slope as $-\gamma$ (would give 1.4).

Problem. Tail degrees ($k_{\min} = 6$): 6, 6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 25. Estimate $\gamma$ by maximum likelihood with its error.

  1. Terms $\ln(k_i/5.5)$: 0.087, 0.087, 0.241, 0.241, 0.375, 0.492, 0.598, 0.780, 1.003, 1.514. Sum $= 5.42$.
  2. $\hat\gamma = 1 + 10/5.42 = 2.85$.
  3. $\sigma = 1.85/\sqrt{10} = 0.58$. So $\gamma = 2.8 \pm 0.6$: ten points cannot tell 2.5 from 3.

Problem. A configuration-model network has $L = 1{,}000$ and two hubs with $k = 100$ and $k = 50$. How many links do you expect between them? What goes wrong in a simple network?

  1. $p_{ij} = 100 \times 50/2{,}000 = 2.5$ links.
  2. A simple network allows at most 1. Forbidding multi-links removes links between hubs, so hubs end up linked to small nodes: structural disassortativity (Ch 7).

Common mistakes

  • CCDF slope is $-(\gamma-1)$, not $-\gamma$.
  • Fitting a straight line to a linearly binned log–log histogram: biased by the noisy tail.
  • "Scale-free" means $\langle k^2\rangle$ diverges ($\gamma < 3$), not just "has hubs".
  • The configuration model reproduces hubs but does not explain them: you put the degrees in.

Ch 5 · The Barabási–Albert model

Why we need it: Ch 4 describes power laws; the BA model explains them with a mechanism. That mechanism predicts $\gamma$, shows which ingredient is essential, and is the base for Ch 6.

Must know

Model. Start with $m_0$ nodes. Each step: (1) growth, add a node with $m \le m_0$ links; (2) preferential attachment, each link goes to node $i$ with probability

$$\Pi(k_i) = \frac{k_i}{\sum_j k_j}$$
$\Pi(k_i)$
probability that one new link attaches to node $i$
$\sum_j k_j$
total degree, $\approx 2mt$ after $t$ steps
$m$
links brought by each new node

Degree dynamics (continuum approximation):

$$\frac{dk_i}{dt} = m\,\Pi(k_i) = \frac{k_i}{2t} \;\Rightarrow\; k_i(t) = m\Big(\frac{t}{t_i}\Big)^{\beta}, \quad \beta = \frac12$$
$t$
time = number of nodes added ($N \approx t$)
$t_i$
arrival time of node $i$, when $k_i = m$
$\beta$
dynamical exponent: every node grows like $\sqrt t$; older nodes stay ahead (first-mover advantage)

Degree distribution. From $P(k_i < k) = P\big(t_i > t\,(m/k)^{1/\beta}\big) = 1 - (m/k)^{1/\beta}$:

$$p_k \approx 2m^{1/\beta}k^{-\gamma} = 2m^2k^{-3}, \qquad \gamma = 1 + \frac1\beta = 3$$

Exact (book Eq. 5.11):

$$p_k = \frac{2m(m+1)}{k(k+1)(k+2)}, \quad k \ge m$$
$p_k$
independent of $t$ and $N$ (stationary); $\gamma = 3$ for every $m$ and $m_0$
$p_m$
$= 2/(m+2)$: the most common degree is the smallest

Other properties: $k_{\max} \sim mN^{1/2}$; $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln N/\ln\ln N$; $\langle C\rangle \sim (\ln N)^2/N$ (larger than random, still vanishing).

Both ingredients are needed (Section 5.6):

  • Model A, growth without preference ($\Pi = $ const): $k_i$ grows like $\ln t$; $p_k = \frac{e}{m}e^{-k/m}$, exponential. No hubs.
  • Model B, preference without growth ($N$ fixed): not stationary; ends as a complete graph.

Measuring and generalising preferential attachment (Sections 5.7–5.8). Compare two snapshots: $\Delta k_i/\Delta t \sim \Pi(k_i)$. Reduce noise with the cumulative function

$$\pi(k) = \sum_{k_i=0}^{k}\Pi(k_i), \qquad \Pi(k) \sim k^\alpha \Rightarrow \pi(k) \sim k^{\alpha+1}$$
$\alpha$
attachment exponent; no preference: $\pi(k)\sim k$; linear: $\pi(k) \sim k^2$
  • $\alpha = 0$: exponential (Model A). $0 < \alpha < 1$: stretched exponential, fewer and smaller hubs.
  • $\alpha = 1$: power law (BA). $\alpha > 1$: hub-and-spoke; the top hub keeps a finite share of links, $k_{\max} \sim t$.
  • Measured: Internet and citations $\alpha \approx 1$; co-authorship and actors $\alpha \approx 0.9$ (sublinear).

Where preference comes from (Section 5.9): following a random link reaches a node with probability $\propto k$ (link selection, copying), so local random choices create global preference.

Exam-style problems

Problem. BA network, $m = 3$, at $t = 1{,}000$. What is the probability that one new link lands on a node of degree 60? On a node of degree 3? Expected new links to the degree-60 node in the next step?

  1. $\sum_j k_j \approx 2mt = 6{,}000$.
  2. $\Pi(60) = 60/6{,}000 = 1\%$; $\Pi(3) = 3/6{,}000 = 0.05\%$: 20 times smaller.
  3. The new node brings 3 links: $m\,\Pi = 0.03$ expected links.

Problem. $m = 2$. Node $i$ arrives at $t_i = 100$. Find $k_i$ at $t = 10^4$ and $10^6$. When does it reach 100 links? A node arriving at $t = 10^4$: how far behind is it forever?

  1. $k_i(10^4) = 2\sqrt{100} = 20$; $k_i(10^6) = 2\sqrt{10^4} = 200$.
  2. $2\sqrt{t/100} = 100 \Rightarrow t = 100 \times 50^2 = 250{,}000$.
  3. Ratio $k_i/k_j = (t_j/t_i)^{1/2} = (10^4/100)^{1/2} = 10$ at every $t$: in BA a latecomer never catches up (fixed by fitness, Ch 6).

Problem. Derive $\gamma$ from $k_i(t) = m(t/t_i)^\beta$ for a general $\beta$. What would $\beta = 0.8$ give?

  1. $k_i(t) < k \iff t_i > t\,(m/k)^{1/\beta}$.
  2. Arrival times are uniform on $[0, t]$, so $P(k_i < k) = 1 - (m/k)^{1/\beta}$.
  3. Differentiate: $p_k = \frac{1}{\beta}m^{1/\beta}k^{-(1/\beta + 1)}$, so $\gamma = 1 + 1/\beta$.
  4. $\beta = \frac12$: $\gamma = 3$. $\beta = 0.8$: $\gamma = 2.25$. Faster growth of the leaders, a flatter tail. This is what fitness does (Ch 6).

Problem. Exact BA distribution with $m = 3$: find $p_3$, $p_4$ and the fraction of nodes with $k \ge 10$. Hint: $\frac{2}{k(k+1)(k+2)} = \frac{1}{k(k+1)} - \frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}$. Largest hub for $N = 10^6$?

  1. $p_3 = 2\cdot3\cdot4/(3\cdot4\cdot5) = 0.4$ (check: $2/(m+2) = 0.4$ ✓). $p_4 = 24/(4\cdot5\cdot6) = 0.2$.
  2. With the hint the sum telescopes: $P(K \ge k) = \frac{m(m+1)}{k(k+1)}$.
  3. $P(K \ge 10) = 12/110 = 0.109$: 11% of nodes.
  4. CCDF $\sim k^{-2}$, i.e. slope $-(\gamma - 1) = -2$ ✓. $k_{\max} \sim mN^{1/2} = 3{,}000$.

Problem. Two networks: (a) $\pi(k) \sim k^{1.9}$; (b) $\pi(k) \sim k^{2.5}$. Find $\alpha$ and predict the degree distribution. Which of Model A, Model B, BA has (i) no hubs, (ii) no stationary state?

  1. $\pi \sim k^{\alpha+1}$: (a) $\alpha = 0.9$, sublinear: stretched exponential, hubs smaller than BA. (b) $\alpha = 1.5$: superlinear, hub-and-spoke; a few super-hubs grab a finite share of links.
  2. (i) Model A: exponential $p_k$. (ii) Model B: degrees keep growing until the network is complete.
  3. Only growth and linear preference give a stationary power law.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking $\gamma$ depends on $m$: $m$ cancels from $dk_i/dt = k_i/2t$.
  • Using $\sum_j k_j = mt$: each link adds 2 to the total degree, so it is $2mt$.
  • "Preferential attachment alone gives power laws": without growth (Model B) it does not.
  • Reading $\pi(k) \sim k^2$ as $\alpha = 2$: the cumulative function adds one, so $\alpha = 1$.
