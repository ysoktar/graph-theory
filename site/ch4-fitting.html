Estimating the degree exponent

Fitting a straight line to a log-log plot is the most common way to estimate $\gamma$, and one of the least reliable. This page tests five estimators on data whose exponent we know, then applies the statistically sound recipe to the book's networks.

A controlled experiment

To judge an estimator we need data with a known answer. We draw $n$ degrees from a discrete power law with exponent $\gamma$ and $k_{\min} = 1$, and estimate $\gamma$ five ways (notebook III.3):

  1. least squares on the linearly binned histogram, slope $= -\gamma$;
  2. least squares on the log-binned histogram, slope $= -\gamma$;
  3. least squares on the cumulative distribution, slope $= -(\gamma - 1)$;
  4. the closed-form maximum-likelihood approximation of Advanced Topic 4.C, on the tail $k \ge 6$;
  5. the exact discrete maximum-likelihood estimate, found numerically.

Linear bins (1) are biased far low in every sample. Log bins (2) and the cumulative distribution (3) are better on average, but depend on bins and fitting range and give no trustworthy error bar. Exact maximum likelihood (5) is centred on the truth with the smallest spread.

20 samples, $n = 10^4$, $\gamma = 2.5$: (1) 1.32–1.98, (2) 2.21–2.61, (3) 2.29–2.77, (5) 2.48–2.55.

Maximum likelihood (Advanced Topic 4.C)

The likelihood $\mathcal L(\gamma)$ is the probability of the observed degrees if the exponent were $\gamma$. For the tail of a discrete power law:

$$\ln \mathcal L(\gamma) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln p_{k_i} = -n \ln \zeta(\gamma, k_{\min}) - \gamma \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln k_i .$$
$k_i$
degree of node $i$ in the tail ($k_i \ge k_{\min}$)
$n$
number of nodes in the tail
$p_{k_i}$
power-law probability of degree $k_i$
$\zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})$
Hurwitz zeta function (normalisation)

Derivation, in three steps:

  1. Independent degrees: $\mathcal L = \prod_i p_{k_i}$, so $\ln \mathcal L = \sum_i \ln p_{k_i}$.
  2. Insert $p_k = k^{-\gamma}/\zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})$: $\ln p_{k_i} = -\gamma \ln k_i - \ln \zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})$.
  3. Sum over the $n$ tail nodes.

The estimate $\hat\gamma$ maximises $\ln\mathcal L$; there is no closed formula, but the curve has a single peak, easy to find numerically. Its standard error is approximately

$$\sigma = \frac{\hat\gamma - 1}{\sqrt{n}}.$$
$\hat\gamma$
maximum-likelihood estimate of $\gamma$
$\sigma$
standard error of $\hat\gamma$

Internet: $n = 1{,}286$ tail nodes, $\hat\gamma = 3.40$, so $\sigma = 2.40/\sqrt{1286} = 0.067$.

Treating degrees as continuous gives the closed-form approximation used in the book:

$$\hat\gamma \approx 1 + n \left[\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln \frac{k_i}{k_{\min} - \tfrac12}\right]^{-1}.$$
$k_{\min} - \tfrac12$
continuity correction for integer degrees

It is accurate only for $k_{\min} \gtrsim 6$. With $k_{\min} = 1$ it gives about 2.0 for $\gamma = 2.5$ (Clauset, Shalizi and Newman 2009).

Real data: the Clauset–Shalizi–Newman method

A real degree distribution is rarely a power law from $k = 1$; at best its tail is, above an unknown $k_{\min}$. Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (SIAM Review 51, 661, 2009) proposed three steps:

  1. Fit. For every candidate $k_{\min}$, fit $\gamma$ by maximum likelihood on $k \ge k_{\min}$ and compute the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance $D$. Keep the $k_{\min}$ with the smallest $D$.
  2. Test. Draw synthetic data sets from the fitted model, refit each, and compute $p$, the share with $D$ at least the observed one. If $p < 0.1$, the power law is ruled out.
  3. Compare. Likelihood-ratio tests against alternatives such as the log-normal or the exponential.
$$D = \max_{k \ge k_{\min}} \bigl|S(k) - P(k)\bigr|$$
$D$
Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance: largest gap between data and model
$S(k)$
observed share of tail nodes with degree $\ge k$
$P(k)$
fitted power law's share with degree $\ge k$
$p$
goodness-of-fit $p$-value from the bootstrap

Check the defaults of a fitting routine. The Python powerlaw package (2.0) restricts the exponent to $\gamma \le 3$ unless told otherwise and silently discards any $k_{\min}$ whose best exponent exceeds 3. For the Internet this moves the result from $k_{\min} = 70$, $\hat\gamma = 3.40$ to $k_{\min} = 36$, $\hat\gamma = 2.94$: the "scale-free regime" comes from the default, not the data. Try the toggle above.

Three bugs in the powerlaw package

Checking the bootstrap of step 2 against Python turned up three defects in the random-number generators of powerlaw 2.0.0, which any bootstrap built on generate_random() inherits. The notebook is not affected: its only call uses $k_{\min} = 1$, where the generator happens to be exact. Reproductions, a proposed fix and validation scripts are in research/powerlaw-discrete-generator.

Is the Internet scale-free?

By the CSN criterion the power law is ruled out for three of the eight networks and borderline for the Internet, and where it is plausible it often covers only a few per cent of the nodes. Are these networks "scale-free"? Does it matter?

Broido and Clauset (Nat. Commun. 10, 1017, 2019) tested almost 1,000 networks and concluded that "scale-free networks are rare". Holme (Nat. Commun. 10, 1016, 2019) and Voitalov, van der Hoorn, van der Hofstad and Krioukov (Phys. Rev. Research 1, 033034, 2019) replied that what matters for hubs, distances, robustness and spreading is whether the tail is heavy, i.e. whether $\langle k^2\rangle \gg \langle k\rangle^2$. Here $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle^2$ ranges from 1.45 (power grid) to 121 (email). Both sides agree that a straight line on a log-log plot proves nothing.
  • $\gamma$ drives every prediction of the scale-free model, so a biased $\hat\gamma$ gives wrong predictions.
  • Maximum likelihood gives an unbiased $\hat\gamma$ with an error bar.
  • The bootstrap test says whether a power law is plausible at all, which no fit can.
  • Builds on plotting: the plot suggests, the fit decides.
  • Feeds $\hat\gamma$ and $k_{\min}$ into hub sizes, universality and the role of γ.

The bootstrap test of a power-law fit returns $p = 0.45$. What can you conclude?

  1. The distribution is a power law with probability 0.45
  2. The power law is ruled out
  3. The power law is plausible; the data cannot reject it
  4. The power law fits better than a log-normal
A large $p$ means deviations as large as the observed one are common in samples from the fitted law. It neither proves the power law nor rules out alternatives.

A tail of $n = 400$ nodes gives $\hat\gamma = 3$. What is the approximate standard error?

  1. 0.1
  2. 0.005
  3. 0.15
  4. 2
$\sigma = (\hat\gamma - 1)/\sqrt n = 2/20 = 0.1$.

Why is $k_{\min}$ chosen by minimising $D$ rather than set to 1?

  1. A larger $k_{\min}$ always reduces the standard error
  2. Too small includes the non-power-law head (bias); too large wastes data (noise)
  3. The likelihood is undefined for $k_{\min} = 1$
  4. $\gamma$ is fixed once $k_{\min}$ is known
Real distributions follow a power law only in the tail. The KS scan picks the $k_{\min}$ above which the fitted law matches the data best.

A power law beats the exponential in a likelihood-ratio test ($R > 0$, $p < 0.001$). Is a power law therefore plausible?

  1. Yes, the test proves it
  2. No, $R > 0$ rules it out
  3. Only if $n > 1{,}000$
  4. Not established: that needs the bootstrap goodness-of-fit test
A likelihood ratio only says which of two models is less bad. Collaboration, email and the WWW beat the exponential yet fail the bootstrap test.

The tail grows from $n = 400$ to $n = 1{,}600$ nodes at the same $\hat\gamma$. What happens to $\sigma$?

  1. It quarters
  2. It stays the same
  3. It doubles
  4. It halves
$\sigma = (\hat\gamma - 1)/\sqrt n$; four times more data gives $\sqrt 4 = 2$ times smaller error.

Restricting the Internet fit to $\gamma \le 3$ moves $\hat\gamma$ from 3.40 to 2.94. What does that change in Chapter 4's terms?

  1. The predicted regime: from random-like (finite $\langle k^2 angle$) to scale-free (divergent $\langle k^2 angle$)
  2. Nothing: both exponents describe the same network
  3. The number of nodes in the network
  4. Only the plot colours
$\gamma = 3$ is the boundary between divergent and finite $\langle k^2\rangle$ (role of γ), so a default can change the conclusion.

What does the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance $D$ measure?

  1. The average squared error between data and model
  2. The probability that the power law is true
  3. The largest gap between the observed and fitted cumulative distributions
  4. The difference between two estimates of $\gamma$
$D = \max_k |S(k) - P(k)|$ over the tail. The $k_{\min}$ with the smallest $D$ is kept.
