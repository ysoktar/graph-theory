Degree distribution: binomial → Poisson

In a random network every node has the same chances, so its degree distribution can be written down exactly. Its shape is the yardstick against which real networks will be measured.

A node has $N-1$ potential partners and links to each independently with probability $p$. Its degree is therefore binomially distributed:

$$p_k = \binom{N-1}{k}\, p^k (1-p)^{N-1-k}.$$

Real networks are sparse ($\langle k\rangle \ll N$). In that limit, with $\langle k\rangle = p(N-1)$ held fixed, the binomial becomes the Poisson distribution (book, Advanced Topic 3.A):

$$p_k = e^{-\langle k\rangle}\,\frac{\langle k\rangle^k}{k!}, \qquad \sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}.$$

The Poisson form depends on a single parameter, $\langle k\rangle$. Networks of very different size but the same average degree have the same degree distribution.

Simulated random networks

  • Points for $N = 500$, $5{,}000$ and $50{,}000$ fall on the same Poisson curve: $p_k$ depends only on $\langle k\rangle$.
  • Switch to a logarithmic vertical axis: the probabilities fall off faster than exponentially beyond the peak (as $\langle k\rangle^k/k!$). For $\langle k\rangle = 10$, a node with twice the average degree is already rare ($P(k \ge 20) = 0.35\%$), and one with ten times the average essentially never occurs ($P(k \ge 100) \approx 5\times10^{-63}$).

When is Poisson a good approximation?

For small networks the binomial is visibly narrower than the Poisson curve, because a node cannot have more than $N-1$ neighbours: its variance is $\langle k\rangle(1-p)$ instead of $\langle k\rangle$. Once $N$ is a few dozen times $\langle k\rangle$ the two are indistinguishable. In every real network of the book $N$ is more than 100 times $\langle k\rangle$ (the closest is E. coli metabolism, with $N/\langle k\rangle \approx 114$).

In a random network with $\langle k\rangle = 100$, between which degrees do almost all nodes lie (within three standard deviations)?

  1. 0 and 200
  2. 70 and 130
  3. 97 and 103
  4. 90 and 110
$\sigma_k = \sqrt{100} = 10$, so $\langle k\rangle \pm 3\sigma_k = 70 \dots 130$. In a random network the average degree is a good description of every node.
