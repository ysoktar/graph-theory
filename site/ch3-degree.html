Degree distribution: binomial → Poisson

In a random network, how different can two nodes be? Every pair of nodes is treated alike, so the whole degree distribution can be computed exactly. It is the yardstick against which the book measures every real degree distribution.

  • It predicts the full degree distribution $p_k$ (Part I), not just the average degree $\langle k\rangle$.
  • It tells us how improbable a real hub would be if links were placed by chance (next page).

The exact answer: a binomial distribution

  1. A node has $N-1$ possible partners, and each is linked to it with probability $p$.
  2. The probability that one particular set of $k$ partners is linked, and no other, is $p^k(1-p)^{N-1-k}$.
  3. There are $\binom{N-1}{k}$ such sets, so:
$$p_k = \binom{N-1}{k}\, p^k (1-p)^{N-1-k}.$$
$p_k$
the probability that a node has degree $k$
$N-1$
the number of possible partners of a node
$\binom{N-1}{k}$
the number of ways to choose $k$ neighbours among them
$p$
the probability that a given pair is linked

Its mean is $\langle k\rangle = p(N-1)$ and its variance is $\sigma_k^2 = p(1-p)(N-1)$.

For $N = 1{,}000$ and $p = 0.01$: $p_{10} = \binom{999}{10}\,0.01^{10}\,0.99^{989} \approx 0.1257$.

The sparse limit: a Poisson distribution (Advanced Topic 3.A)

Real networks are sparse: $\langle k\rangle \ll N$. Keep $\langle k\rangle$ fixed and let $N \to \infty$, so that $p = \langle k\rangle/(N-1) \to 0$:

  1. For $k \ll N$, $\binom{N-1}{k} \approx (N-1)^k/k!$, where $k! = 1\cdot2\cdots k$.
  2. $p^k = \langle k\rangle^k/(N-1)^k$, so the factor $(N-1)^k$ cancels.
  3. $(1-p)^{N-1-k} \approx (1 - \langle k\rangle/(N-1))^{N-1} \to e^{-\langle k\rangle}$, because $(1-x/n)^n \to e^{-x}$ (with $e \approx 2.718$).
  4. Multiplying the three factors gives:
$$p_k = e^{-\langle k\rangle}\,\frac{\langle k\rangle^k}{k!}, \qquad \sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}.$$
$p_k$
the Poisson distribution, which counts rare independent events
$\langle k\rangle$
its only parameter: $N$ and $p$ have dropped out
$e^{-\langle k\rangle}$
$p_0$, the fraction of isolated nodes
$\langle k\rangle^k/k!$
first rises with $k$, then falls faster than any exponential
$\sigma_k$
the standard deviation of $k$; the variance equals the mean

With $\langle k\rangle = 9.99$, Poisson gives $p_{10} \approx 0.1251$ (the exact binomial value is 0.1257). With $\langle k\rangle = 10$, $\sigma_k \approx 3.16$, 73% of the nodes have 7 to 13 links, and $p_0 = e^{-10} \approx 4.5\times10^{-5}$.

The consequence: random networks with the same $\langle k\rangle$ have the same $p_k$, whatever their size.

When is Poisson a good approximation?

For small $N$ the binomial variance $\langle k\rangle(1-p)$ is below $\langle k\rangle$, because no degree can exceed $N-1$. With $N = 20$ and $\langle k\rangle = 10$ the variances are 4.7 and 10. Once $N$ is a few dozen times $\langle k\rangle$, the two distributions coincide. All the book's networks have $N > 100\langle k\rangle$ (the closest is E. coli metabolism, with 114).

  • The null model predicts that degrees lie within about $\pm 3\sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$ of the mean: there is a typical degree, a characteristic scale.
  • A deviation means that degrees spanning orders of magnitude cannot come from chance wiring.
  • Predicts the Part I $p_k$, and is tested on real data, where it fails.
  • Is replaced by the power law $p_k \sim k^{-\gamma}$ (Ch 4), whose tail falls like a power of $k$ instead of like $1/k!$, so hubs become possible.
  • Its second moment is $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1+\langle k\rangle)$. So the Molloy–Reed condition $\kappa = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 1 + \langle k\rangle > 2$ (Robustness) is the same as $\langle k\rangle > 1$, the giant-component threshold. For power laws with $\gamma < 3$ the second moment diverges (Moments).

$p_k$ depends only on $\langle k\rangle$. Only the largest degree $k_{\max}$ grows, and slowly: from 13 to 19.

For $N = 20$ and $\langle k\rangle = 10$, why is the binomial distribution much narrower than the Poisson?

  1. No degree can exceed 19, and the variance is $\langle k\rangle(1-p) \approx 4.7$, not 10.
  2. The binomial needs a large N.
  3. The links of small networks are correlated.
  4. The binomial has a smaller mean.
Here $p = 10/19 \approx 0.53$, while the Poisson approximation assumes $p \to 0$, that is, $N \gg \langle k\rangle$.

A random network has $\langle k\rangle = 4$. What is its $\langle k^2\rangle$, and is $\kappa = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle > 2$?

  1. 16, so κ = 4.
  2. 4, so κ = 1.
  3. 20, so κ = 5: a giant component exists.
  4. 8, so κ = 2.
$\langle k^2\rangle = \sigma_k^2 + \langle k\rangle^2 = 4 + 16 = 20$, so $\kappa = 1 + \langle k\rangle = 5$.

For a Poisson distribution with $\langle k\rangle = 2$, which is larger, $p_1$ or $p_2$?

  1. $p_1$, by a factor 2.
  2. $p_2$, by a factor 2.
  3. $p_0$ is larger than both.
  4. They are equal: both are $2e^{-2} \approx 0.27$.
$p_k/p_{k-1} = \langle k\rangle/k$, so $p_1 = p_2$ when $\langle k\rangle = 2$.

Why can a power-law distribution (Ch 4) have hubs while a Poisson distribution cannot?

  1. Its tail decays like a power of $k$, not like $1/k!$.
  2. It has a larger mean.
  3. It is defined only for large N.
  4. It has no isolated nodes.
$1/k!$ falls faster than any exponential, while $k^{-\gamma}$ falls slowly, so very large $k$ stays plausible.
