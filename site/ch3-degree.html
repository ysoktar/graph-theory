Degree distribution: binomial → Poisson

In a random network, how different can two nodes be? Every pair is treated alike, so the whole degree distribution can be computed exactly. It is the yardstick for every real degree distribution in the book.

  • Predicts the full $p_k$ (Part I), not just $\langle k\rangle$.
  • Tells us how improbable a real hub would be under chance wiring (next page).

Exact answer: binomial

  1. A node has $N-1$ candidates, each linked with probability $p$.
  2. A given set of $k$ neighbours (and no others): $p^k(1-p)^{N-1-k}$.
  3. There are $\binom{N-1}{k}$ such sets:
$$p_k = \binom{N-1}{k}\, p^k (1-p)^{N-1-k}.$$
$p_k$
probability that a node has degree $k$
$N-1$
potential partners of a node
$\binom{N-1}{k}$
ways to choose $k$ neighbours
$p$
link probability per pair

Mean $\langle k\rangle = p(N-1)$, variance $\sigma_k^2 = p(1-p)(N-1)$.

$N = 1{,}000$, $p = 0.01$: $p_{10} = \binom{999}{10}\,0.01^{10}\,0.99^{989} \approx 0.1257$.

Sparse limit: Poisson (Advanced Topic 3.A)

Real networks are sparse ($\langle k\rangle \ll N$). Fix $\langle k\rangle$, let $N \to \infty$ (so $p = \langle k\rangle/(N-1) \to 0$):

  1. $\binom{N-1}{k} \approx (N-1)^k/k!$ for $k \ll N$; $k! = 1\cdot2\cdots k$.
  2. $p^k = \langle k\rangle^k/(N-1)^k$; the $(N-1)^k$ cancels.
  3. $(1-p)^{N-1-k} \approx (1 - \langle k\rangle/(N-1))^{N-1} \to e^{-\langle k\rangle}$, since $(1-x/n)^n \to e^{-x}$ ($e \approx 2.718$).
  4. Multiply:
$$p_k = e^{-\langle k\rangle}\,\frac{\langle k\rangle^k}{k!}, \qquad \sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}.$$
$p_k$
Poisson distribution: count of rare independent events
$\langle k\rangle$
the only parameter; $N$ and $p$ have dropped out
$e^{-\langle k\rangle}$
$p_0$, fraction of isolated nodes
$\langle k\rangle^k/k!$
rises, then falls faster than any exponential
$\sigma_k$
standard deviation of $k$; variance = mean

$\langle k\rangle = 9.99$: Poisson $p_{10} \approx 0.1251$ (exact 0.1257). $\langle k\rangle = 10$: $\sigma_k \approx 3.16$, 73% of nodes have 7–13 links, $p_0 = e^{-10} \approx 4.5\times10^{-5}$.

Consequence: same $\langle k\rangle$ → same $p_k$, whatever the size.

When is Poisson a good approximation?

Small $N$: binomial variance $\langle k\rangle(1-p)$ is below $\langle k\rangle$ (degree ≤ $N-1$); $N = 20$, $\langle k\rangle = 10$: 4.7 vs 10. From a few dozen times $\langle k\rangle$ on they coincide. All book networks have $N > 100\langle k\rangle$ (closest: E. coli metabolism, 114).

  • Null-model prediction: degrees within about $\pm 3\sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$; a typical degree (characteristic scale) exists.
  • Deviation means: degrees spanning orders of magnitude cannot come from chance wiring.
  • Predicts the Part I $p_k$; tested on real data, where it fails.
  • Replaced by the power law $p_k \sim k^{-\gamma}$ (Ch 4): tail falls like a power, not like $1/k!$ → hubs.
  • Second moment $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1+\langle k\rangle)$ → Molloy–Reed $\kappa = 1 + \langle k\rangle > 2$ (Robustness) ⇔ $\langle k\rangle > 1$, the giant-component threshold. Diverges for power laws with $\gamma < 3$ (Moments).

$\langle k\rangle = 100$. Almost all degrees ($\pm 3\sigma_k$) lie between …

  1. 0 and 200
  2. 70 and 130
  3. 97 and 103
  4. 90 and 110
$\sigma_k = \sqrt{100} = 10$, so $100 \pm 30$.

Large random network, $\langle k\rangle = 3$. Fraction of isolated nodes?

  1. None
  2. About 5%
  3. About a third
  4. About 0.05%
$p_0 = e^{-3} \approx 0.050$. These nodes keep the network from being connected.

Random networks with $\langle k\rangle = 5$, $N = 10^3$ and $N = 10^6$. Their degree distributions are …

  1. much wider for the larger one
  2. richer in isolated nodes for the larger one
  3. practically identical
  4. 1,000 times higher for the larger one
$p_k$ depends only on $\langle k\rangle$. Only $k_{\max}$ grows, slowly: 13 → 19.

$N = 20$, $\langle k\rangle = 10$: why is the binomial much narrower than the Poisson?

  1. Degree ≤ 19; variance $\langle k\rangle(1-p) \approx 4.7$, not 10.
  2. The binomial needs large N
  3. Small networks have correlated links
  4. The binomial has a smaller mean
$p = 10/19 \approx 0.53$; Poisson assumes $p \to 0$, i.e. $N \gg \langle k\rangle$.

Random network, $\langle k\rangle = 4$. $\langle k^2\rangle$, and is $\kappa = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle > 2$?

  1. 16, κ = 4
  2. 4, κ = 1
  3. 20, κ = 5: giant component exists
  4. 8, κ = 2
$\langle k^2\rangle = 4 + 16 = 20$, $\kappa = 1 + \langle k\rangle = 5$.
