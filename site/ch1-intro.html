Why networks?

On 14 August 2003 a local failure in the power grid spread until 45 million people in eight US states and 10 million in Ontario were without power. The failure travelled along the network's links. Chapter 1 argues that we cannot understand such systems without understanding the networks behind them.

  • Many systems are too complicated to predict from their parts alone. Their wiring often decides what they do.
  • The same few tools work for cells, brains, the Web and power grids, because their networks follow common laws.
  • This chapter has no formulas. It explains why the rest of the course matters.

Vulnerability due to interconnectivity (Section 1.1)

The 2003 blackout was a cascading failure. Such a failure unfolds in three steps:

  1. A component fails and its load moves to its neighbours.
  2. If the extra load is small, the neighbours absorb it and nothing visible happens.
  3. If the extra load is too large, the neighbours fail too and pass the load on. The failure keeps spreading: this is the cascade.

How large the cascade gets depends on where the first failures sit and how much load each node can carry.

  • Internet: traffic is rerouted around broken routers and can overload routers that still work, making them unavailable (a denial of service).
  • Finance: in 1997, credit limits imposed in several Pacific nations made corporations default, and stock markets crashed worldwide. In 2009–2011 the US credit crisis paralysed the global economy.
  • On purpose: governments try to dry up the money supply of terrorist organisations, and cancer researchers try to induce cascades that kill cancer cells.

Linking the cities' separate grids into one network made electricity cheap, but it also let a local failure travel far. Information, viruses and business practices spread along networks in the same way. Networks therefore bring both benefits and risks.

To control a cascade we need to understand three things, each a part of this course:

  1. the structure of the network (Chapters 2–7);
  2. the dynamics on it, such as the flow of electricity;
  3. how structure and dynamics together set robustness (Chapter 8).

A network behind every complex system (Section 1.2)

A complex system is one whose collective behaviour is hard to derive from knowledge of its parts. Behind each such system is a network: its parts are the nodes, and their interactions are the links.

Why around the year 2000? (Section 1.3)

The study of networks is old. Graph theory goes back to Euler (Königsberg), random graphs to Erdős and Rényi (1959), and social network analysis to sociology. Yet citations to these classics exploded only after 2000. Two forces turned them into a new field:

  1. Network maps. The Internet made data cheap to store, share and analyse. Around 2000 the first large maps appeared: maps of the Internet itself (from the CAIDA and DIMES projects), of protein interactions in human cells, of friendships on Facebook, Twitter and LinkedIn, and of brain wiring (the Connectome project).
  2. Universality. Networks from very different domains turned out to share the same architecture, so one set of mathematical tools works for all of them.

Most early maps were by-products of other projects. Biochemists had found chemical reactions one by one over 150 years, IMDb listed the actors of each film, and Web of Science indexed the authors of each paper. Network scientists turned these lists into networks. The site's eight reference networks are maps of this kind.

What kind of science (Section 1.4)

  • Interdisciplinary: it gives different fields a shared language. Betweenness centrality (how many shortest paths pass through a node) came from sociology and now finds busy Internet routers. Graph partitioning came from computer science and now finds groups of disease genes.
  • Empirical: unlike graph theory, it tests every tool on real data and judges it by what it reveals.
  • Quantitative: it takes its formalism from graph theory, its treatment of randomness and universal laws from statistical physics, and further tools from engineering and statistics.
  • Computational: networks with millions of nodes need efficient algorithms and software (this course uses NetworkX).

What it lets us do (Sections 1.5–1.6)

  • Economy: Google's search relies on the Web's link structure, and Facebook, LinkedIn and Twitter recommend friends with network algorithms.
  • Health: a list of genes is not enough, because diseases arise when molecular networks break down. Network biology, network medicine and drug design study these networks.
  • Security: agencies map and disrupt terrorist networks. The same tools have also enabled mass surveillance, so ethical use is part of the job.
  • Epidemics: in 2009 the course of the H1N1 pandemic was predicted months ahead from the worldwide transportation network. The models predicted a peak in October, not January–February, so vaccines planned for November came too late.
  • Brain: the only fully mapped nervous system is that of the worm C. elegans, with 302 neurons. The US Connectome project (2010) aims to map mammalian brains.
  • Management: the informal network of who asks whom for advice often matters more than the official organisation chart.
  • Science: the papers of Watts and Strogatz (1998, small worlds) and of Barabási and Albert (1999, scale-free networks) gathered citations faster than the classics of complexity science.

A Hungarian company asked every employee whom they turn to for advice. The largest hub (the person named most often) was neither a director nor a top manager but the safety officer, who visited every site and talked to everyone except top management. Should the company fire him or promote him? The book leaves this to you.

Like evolution and quantum mechanics, network science is an enabling platform, a body of knowledge on which other fields build.

Roadmap: how the course builds up

Each step answers a question the previous one left open.

  1. Graph theory (Ch 2) provides the language: nodes, links, degree, paths and clustering.
  2. Random networks (Ch 3) are the null model: they show what pure chance produces. They explain giant components and small worlds, but they have no hubs.
  3. Scale-free networks (Ch 4): real degrees follow power laws. The hubs make the mean squared degree $\langle k^2\rangle$ huge and paths ultra-short.
  4. Barabási–Albert model (Ch 5): growth plus preferential attachment (new nodes prefer well-connected nodes) explain where power laws come from.
  5. Evolving networks (Ch 6): fitness, an intrinsic attractiveness of each node, explains why latecomers such as Google can win.
  6. Degree correlations (Ch 7) describe who links to whom, not only how many links each node has.
  7. Robustness (Ch 8) brings us back to the blackout. Hubs make networks hard to break by accident and easy to break on purpose.
  8. Graph machine learning and node embeddings (GraphML) turn each node's position into a vector, so that machine learning can predict node labels and missing links.
  • Cascades return in Robustness (Section 8.5), and spreading in Spreading (Ch 10, beyond the course).
  • In Universality, the claim of universality becomes a question we can test: does every network have a power-law tail?
  • Because maps are by-products, real data are incomplete and noisy. Link prediction tries to fill the gaps.

The book's homework (Section 1.8) asks you to name three real networks and say what their nodes and links are. Then pick the network you care most about. How large is it, and can it be mapped?

First decide what one link means: a single email, or a regular contact? Does a link have a direction (A emails B), and does it have a strength (how many emails)? Two maps of the same people, such as co-authorship and citation, can look very different. See Networks and graphs and Weighted & bipartite networks.

What made the 2003 blackout a cascading failure?

  2. Each failure shifted load to neighbours, which then failed in turn
  3. Demand suddenly exceeded the total capacity of the grid
  4. The failures were random and unpredictable
In a cascade the failure spreads along links: overloaded neighbours fail and pass the load on.

Which pair are the two forces that, according to the book, made network science emerge around 2000?

  1. Graph theory and random graph theory
  2. Faster computers and better visualisation
  3. Detailed network maps, and the universality of network properties
  4. Social media companies and network courses
Maps of real networks became available, and they turned out to share common properties, so common tools apply.

What separates network science from graph theory, according to Section 1.4?

  1. Its focus on real data: each tool is judged by what it reveals about real systems
  2. It does not use graphs
  3. It avoids mathematics
  4. It studies only small networks
Network science is empirical: it cares about data, function and usefulness.

What does "universality" mean in network science?

  1. All networks have the same kind of nodes.
  2. All networks have the same size.
  3. One network connects all systems in the universe.
  4. Networks from different domains share an architecture, so the same tools apply to all of them.
Metabolism, the Web and friendships were built by very different processes yet have similar networks. Chapter 4 tests this for degrees.

What did linking neighbouring cities' power grids into one network do?

  1. It only made the supply more reliable.
  2. It made power cheaper but let local failures travel far.
  3. It was needed to store electricity.
  4. It had no benefit.
Interconnectivity brings benefits and vulnerability at once, because failures no longer stay local.

In 2009 the H1N1 pandemic's peak was predicted months ahead. What made this possible?

  1. Compartment models in which everyone meets everyone
  2. Counting cases at the peak
  3. Models built on the worldwide transportation network
  4. Vaccination data
Travel links decide where and when a virus arrives. The models predicted an October peak, so November vaccines came too late.
