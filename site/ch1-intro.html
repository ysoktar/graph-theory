Why networks?

On 14 August 2003 a local failure in the power grid spread until 45 million people in eight US states and 10 million in Ontario were without power. The failure travelled along the network's links. Chapter 1 argues that we cannot understand such systems without understanding the networks behind them.

  • Many systems are too complicated to predict from their parts alone. Their wiring often decides what they do.
  • The same few tools work for cells, brains, the Web and power grids, because their networks follow common laws.
  • This chapter has no formulas. It explains why the rest of the course matters.

Vulnerability due to interconnectivity (Section 1.1)

The 2003 blackout was a cascading failure:

  1. A component fails and its load moves to its neighbours.
  2. If the extra load is small, the neighbours absorb it and nothing visible happens.
  3. If it is too large, the neighbours fail too and pass the load on: a cascade.

How large the cascade gets depends on where the first failures sit and how much load each node can carry.

  • Internet: traffic rerouted around broken routers can overload working ones (denial of service).
  • Finance: in 1997, credit limits in several Pacific nations made corporations default and stock markets crash worldwide; in 2009–2011 the US credit crisis paralysed the global economy.
  • On purpose: drying up the money supply of terrorist organisations; cancer researchers try to induce cascades that kill cancer cells.

Linking the cities' grids made electricity cheap and failures non-local. Information, viruses and business practices travel the same way. Networks bring both the benefit and the risk.

To control a cascade we need three things, each a part of this course:

  1. the structure of the network (Chapters 2–7);
  2. the dynamics on it, such as the flow of electricity;
  3. how structure and dynamics together set robustness (Chapter 8).

A network behind every complex system (Section 1.2)

A complex system is one whose collective behaviour is hard to derive from knowledge of its parts. Behind each one is a network of its parts and their interactions:

Why around the year 2000? (Section 1.3)

Graph theory goes back to Euler (Königsberg); random graphs to Erdős and Rényi (1959); social network analysis to sociology. Citations to these classics exploded only after 2000. Two forces made a new field:

  1. Network maps. The Internet made data cheap to store, share and analyse. Around 2000 came the first large maps: the Internet itself (CAIDA, DIMES), protein interactions in human cells, friendships on Facebook, Twitter and LinkedIn, and brain wiring (the Connectome project).
  2. Universality. Networks from very different domains turned out to share the same architecture, so one set of mathematical tools works for all of them.

Most early maps were by-products of other projects: reactions found one by one over 150 years by biochemists, actors listed on IMDb, authors indexed by Web of Science. Network scientists turned these lists into networks. The site's eight reference networks are such maps.

What kind of science (Section 1.4)

  • Interdisciplinary: a shared language. Betweenness centrality came from sociology and now finds busy Internet routers; graph partitioning from computer science now finds disease modules.
  • Empirical: unlike graph theory, every tool is tested on real data and judged by what it reveals.
  • Quantitative: graph theory for the formalism, statistical physics for randomness and universal laws, plus engineering and statistics.
  • Computational: networks with millions of nodes need algorithms and software (NetworkX in this course).

What it lets us do (Sections 1.5–1.6)

  • Economy: Google's search uses the Web's link structure; Facebook, LinkedIn and Twitter recommend friends with network algorithms.
  • Health: a gene list is not enough; disease comes from broken molecular networks (network biology, network medicine, drug design).
  • Security: mapping and disrupting terrorist networks. The same tools enabled mass surveillance, so ethics is part of the job.
  • Epidemics: in 2009 the course of the H1N1 pandemic was predicted months ahead from the worldwide transportation network. The models predicted a peak in October, not January–February, so vaccines planned for November came too late.
  • Brain: only the 302-neuron worm C. elegans was fully mapped; the US Connectome project (2010) aims at mammalian brains.
  • Management: the informal network of who asks whom for advice often matters more than the org chart.
  • Science: Watts–Strogatz (1998, small worlds) and Barabási–Albert (1999, scale-free networks) were cited faster than the classics of complexity science.

A Hungarian company asked every employee whom they turn to for advice. The largest hub was neither a director nor a top manager but the safety officer, who visited every site and talked to everyone except top management. Should the company fire him or promote him? The book leaves this to you.

Like evolution and quantum mechanics, network science is an enabling platform: a body of knowledge on which other fields build.

Roadmap: how the course builds up

Each step answers a question the previous one left open.

  1. Graph theory (Ch 2): the language: nodes, links, degree, paths, clustering.
  2. Random networks (Ch 3): the null model, what pure chance gives. It explains giant components and small worlds but has no hubs.
  3. Scale-free networks (Ch 4): real degrees follow power laws; hubs make $\langle k^2\rangle$ huge and paths ultra-short.
  4. Barabási–Albert model (Ch 5): growth + preferential attachment explain where power laws come from.
  5. Evolving networks (Ch 6): fitness explains why latecomers such as Google can win.
  6. Degree correlations (Ch 7): who links to whom, beyond how many links.
  7. Robustness (Ch 8): back to the blackout. Hubs make networks hard to break by accident and easy to break on purpose.
  8. Graph ML and node embeddings (GraphML): turn each node's position into a vector so that machine learning can predict labels and missing links.
  • The cascade theme returns in Robustness (Section 8.5); spreading in Spreading (Ch 10, beyond the course).
  • "Universality" becomes a measurable claim in Universality: does every network have a power-law tail?
  • "Maps from by-products" means real data are incomplete and noisy; link prediction tries to fill the gaps.

The book's homework (Section 1.8): name three real networks and say what their nodes and links are. Then pick the network you care most about: how large is it, and can it be mapped?

Decide what one link means: a single email or a regular contact? Directed or not? Weighted? Two maps of the same people (co-authorship vs. citation) can look very different. See Networks and graphs and Weighted & bipartite networks.

Network science is empirical: function, data and utility.

"Universality" in network science means that

  1. all networks have the same kind of nodes
  2. all networks have the same size
  3. one network connects all systems in the universe
  4. networks from different domains share an architecture, so the same tools apply
Metabolism, the Web and friendships were built by very different processes yet have similar networks. Chapter 4 tests this for degrees.
