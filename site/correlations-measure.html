Measuring degree correlations

The correlation matrix holds everything but is hard to read. Section 7.3 compresses it: first into a function, the average neighbour degree $k_{nn}(k)$, then into single numbers, the exponent $\mu$ and the coefficient $r$.

  • A rising, flat or falling $k_{nn}(k)$ tells the type of network at a glance.
  • The neutral value $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ is also the friendship paradox.
  • $\mu$ and $r$ let us compare networks, as long as we know what each assumes.

The degree correlation function (Section 7.3)

For each node, average its neighbours' degrees; then average over all nodes of degree $k$:

$$k_{nn}(k_i) = \frac{1}{k_i}\sum_{j} A_{ij}\, k_j \quad (7.6), \qquad k_{nn}(k) = \sum_{k'} k'\, P(k' \mid k) \quad (7.7).$$
$k_{nn}(k)$
mean neighbour degree of degree-$k$ nodes
$P(k' \mid k)$
probability that a neighbour of a degree-$k$ node has degree $k'$

For a neutral network, (7.4) and (7.5) give $P(k' \mid k) = e_{kk'}/q_k = q_{k'}$ (7.8): the neighbour does not care about $k$. Then

$$k_{nn}(k) = \sum_{k'} k'\,\frac{k' p_{k'}}{\langle k\rangle} = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}, \qquad (7.9)$$

a horizontal line. Rising $k_{nn}$: assortative; falling: disassortative. The book fits

$$k_{nn}(k) = a\,k^{\mu} \qquad (7.10)$$

and reads the type from the sign of the correlation exponent $\mu$: collaboration $\mu = 0.37 \pm 0.11$ (assortative), power grid $0.04 \pm 0.05$ (neutral), metabolism $-0.76 \pm 0.04$ (disassortative).

Worked example 3: the friendship paradox (Box 7.1). On average your friends have more friends than you do.

  1. Random network: $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1 + \langle k\rangle)$, so $k_{nn} = 1 + \langle k\rangle$: always one more than the average node.
  2. Collaboration: $178.2/8.08 = 22.1$. A scientist has 8 co-authors on average; a co-author has 22.
  3. Actors: $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 565$. The reason is (7.3): you are $k$ times more likely to know a hub than a degree-1 node. Acquaintance vaccination uses exactly this.

The degree correlation coefficient $r$ (Box 7.2)

Newman's single number is the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link:

$$\begin{gathered} r = \sum_{jk} \frac{jk\,(e_{jk} - q_j q_k)}{\sigma^2} \quad (7.11), \\ \sigma^2 = \sum_k k^2 q_k - \Big[\sum_k k\, q_k\Big]^2 \quad (7.12). \end{gathered}$$

$-1 \le r \le 1$: positive assortative, zero neutral, negative disassortative. Collaboration $r = 0.13$, proteins $r = -0.04$, power grid $r = 0$. $r$ assumes $k_{nn}(k)$ is linear in $k$, $\mu$ that it is a power law; both cannot hold exactly, but they usually agree in sign.

A network's $k_{nn}(k)$ is flat, at the neutral value $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 25$. What is its assortativity $r$, approximately?

  1. $1$
  2. $0$
  3. $-1$
  4. $25$
Flat at the neutral value: one end's degree says nothing about the other's.

A network has $\langle k\rangle = 3$ and $\langle k^2\rangle = 30$. Pick a random link and follow it: what is the average degree of the node you reach?

  1. 3
  2. 10
  3. 30
  4. 1
$\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 10$: the friendship paradox.

When does “your friends have more friends than you”, on average, hold?

  1. In every network whose degrees are not all equal
  2. Only in assortative networks
  3. Only in disassortative networks
  4. Only in scale-free networks
A link end lands on a node with probability $\propto k$, so neighbours average $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle \ge \langle k\rangle$, equal only if all degrees are equal.
