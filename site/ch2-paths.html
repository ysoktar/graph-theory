Paths and distances

In a network, physical distance rarely matters; what matters is how many links separate two nodes. Distance sets how fast news, viruses or failures can travel, and it is the basis of the small-world effect.

A path is a sequence of nodes, each linked to the next, that never revisits a node; its length is its number of links. A shortest path has the fewest links; its length is the distance $d_{ij}$. There can be several shortest paths. Two network-wide summaries:

$$d_{\max} = \max_{i,j} d_{ij}, \qquad \langle d\rangle = \frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i \ne j} d_{ij}.$$
$d_{ij}$
distance: links on a shortest path from $i$ to $j$
$d_{\max}$
diameter: the largest distance
$\max_{i,j}$
largest value over all pairs
$\langle d\rangle$
average distance
$\sum_{i \ne j}$
sum over all ordered pairs of different nodes
$N(N-1)$
number of ordered pairs

Between components $d_{ij} = \infty$ (no path), so both are computed within the giant component.

Links 1–2, 1–3, 2–3, 3–4: four pairs at distance 1, two (1–4, 2–4) at distance 2. $d_{\max} = 2$, $\langle d\rangle = 8/6 \approx 1.33$.

Breadth-first search

Breadth-first search (BFS, Box 2.5) labels the source $s$ with 0, its neighbours 1, their unlabelled neighbours 2, and so on. A node's label, given when first reached, is its distance from $s$. Each node and link is handled once: time $\propto N + L$.

Shortest paths between two nodes

Members 14 and 16, the furthest apart, have 14 shortest paths of length 5, all through node 0. Counting shortest paths through a node gives betweenness centrality (Network lab).

All distances at once

Karate, 561 pairs: 78 at distance 1, 265 at 2, 137 at 3, 73 at 4, 8 at 5. $\langle d\rangle = 2.41$, $d_{\max} = 5$.

Distances in real networks

All-pairs distances are expensive, so the notebook runs BFS from 300 random sources in the giant component; in the collaboration network that samples over six million distances.

Most scientists are 4–6 steps apart and 84% of pairs within six, among 23,133 nodes. The power grid averages about 19: its links join neighbouring places, so there are no long shortcuts. Small worlds explains the short distances.

  • Distance measures how quickly anything can spread between two nodes.
  • $\langle d\rangle$ against $N$ tests models: random networks predict $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$.
  • BFS, at cost $N + L$, makes distances computable on large sparse networks.
  • Same as the first $n$ with $(A^n)_{ij} > 0$ (Adjacency matrix).
  • Needs connectedness: $d = \infty$ between components.
  • Random-network prediction: $\langle d\rangle \approx \ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$ (Small worlds); hubs shrink it further (Ultra-small worlds).
