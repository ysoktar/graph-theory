Paths and distances

In a network, physical distance rarely matters; what matters is how many links separate two nodes. Distance sets how fast news, viruses or failures can travel, and it is the basis of the small-world effect.

A path is a sequence of nodes, each linked to the next, that never revisits a node; its length is its number of links. A shortest path has the fewest links; its length is the distance $d_{ij}$. A pair of nodes can have several shortest paths. Two numbers summarise the whole network:

$$d_{\max} = \max_{i,j} d_{ij}, \qquad \langle d\rangle = \frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i \ne j} d_{ij}.$$
$d_{ij}$
the distance: the number of links on a shortest path from $i$ to $j$
$d_{\max}$
the diameter: the largest distance in the network
$\max_{i,j}$
the largest value over all pairs $i, j$
$\langle d\rangle$
the average distance
$\sum_{i \ne j}$
the sum over all ordered pairs of different nodes
$N(N-1)$
the number of ordered pairs

Nodes in different components have $d_{ij} = \infty$, because no path joins them, so both numbers are computed within the giant component.

The network with links 1–2, 1–3, 2–3 and 3–4 has four pairs at distance 1 and two pairs (1–4 and 2–4) at distance 2, so $d_{\max} = 2$ and $\langle d\rangle = 8/6 \approx 1.33$.

Breadth-first search

Breadth-first search (BFS, Box 2.5) labels the source $s$ with 0, its neighbours 1, their unlabelled neighbours 2, and so on. A node gets its label when it is first reached, and that label is its distance from $s$. Each node and each link is handled once, so the running time grows like $N + L$.

Shortest paths between two nodes

Members 14 and 16 are the furthest apart: they are joined by 14 shortest paths of length 5, and all of them pass through node 0. Counting shortest paths through a node gives betweenness centrality (Network lab).

All distances at once

Of the karate club's 561 pairs, 78 are at distance 1, 265 at distance 2, 137 at 3, 73 at 4 and 8 at 5. So $\langle d\rangle = 2.41$ and $d_{\max} = 5$.

Distances in real networks

Computing the distances between all pairs is expensive, so the notebook runs BFS from 300 random sources in the giant component. In the collaboration network this samples more than six million distances.

Among 23,133 scientists, most pairs are 4 to 6 steps apart, and 84% of pairs are within six steps. The power grid's average distance is about 19: its links join neighbouring places, so it has no long shortcuts. Small worlds explains the short distances.

  • Distance measures how quickly anything can spread between two nodes.
  • How $\langle d\rangle$ grows with $N$ tests models: random networks predict $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$.
  • BFS costs time proportional to $N + L$, which makes distances computable on large sparse networks.
  • The distance is the first $n$ with $(A^n)_{ij} > 0$ (Adjacency matrix).
  • It needs connectedness: $d = \infty$ between different components.
  • Random-network prediction: $\langle d\rangle \approx \ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$ (Small worlds); hubs shrink it further (Ultra-small worlds).

  1. The grid's links join nearby places, so it has no long-range shortcuts.
  2. The grid is far larger.
  3. The grid is split into many pieces.
  4. The grid has a lower $\langle k\rangle$.
The two have the same $\langle k\rangle$ but very different distances, because geography limits the grid's shortcuts. The average degree alone does not set the distances.

In a random network $\langle d\rangle \approx \ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$. A random network with $\langle k\rangle = 10$ grows from $N = 10^4$ to $N = 10^8$ nodes, and its $\langle d\rangle$ starts at 4. What does it become?

  1. It stays at 4.
  2. 40,000
  3. 8
  4. 16
$\ln 10^8/\ln 10 = 8$: ten thousand times more nodes give only twice the distance. This is the small-world effect.
