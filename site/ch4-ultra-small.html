Ultra-small worlds

Random networks are small worlds: $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln N$. Hubs make scale-free networks smaller still, because most short paths can pass through a few very well-connected nodes (Section 4.6).

Four regimes

$$\langle d\rangle \sim \begin{cases} \text{const} & \gamma = 2 \\ \ln\ln N & 2 < \gamma < 3 \\ \dfrac{\ln N}{\ln\ln N} & \gamma = 3 \\ \ln N & \gamma > 3 \end{cases}$$
$\langle d\rangle$
average shortest-path distance
$N$
number of nodes
$\gamma$
degree exponent
$\ln\ln N$
logarithm of the logarithm: grows extremely slowly
  • $\gamma = 2$: the largest hub grows like $N$ and links a finite share of all nodes, so distances stay constant.
  • $2 < \gamma < 3$: hubs of decreasing size form a hierarchy that connects any two nodes in $\sim\ln\ln N$ steps (Cohen and Havlin, 2003).
  • $\gamma > 3$: the hubs are too small to matter; the network behaves like a random one.

$N = 10^6$: $\ln N = 13.8$, $\ln\ln N = 2.6$.

Even with a fair baseline, $\gamma = 2.1$ gives the shortest distances, growing most slowly. But $\ln\ln N$ changes only from 1.5 to 2.3 between $N = 100$ and $30{,}000$: the regimes are asymptotic statements.

The degree sequences here use an exact discrete power-law sampler. The notebook's helper uses a rounding shortcut that over-represents degree 2 at $k_{\min} = 2$ (see N3), which makes its networks slightly sparser.
