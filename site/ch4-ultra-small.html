Ultra-small worlds

Random networks are small worlds: $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln N$. Hubs make scale-free networks smaller still, because most short paths can pass through a few very well-connected nodes (Section 4.6).

Four regimes

$$\langle d\rangle \sim \begin{cases} \text{const} & \gamma = 2 \\ \ln\ln N & 2 < \gamma < 3 \\ \dfrac{\ln N}{\ln\ln N} & \gamma = 3 \\ \ln N & \gamma > 3 \end{cases}$$

For $\gamma = 2$ the largest hub grows linearly with $N$ and is linked to a finite share of all nodes, so distances stay constant. For $2 < \gamma < 3$ hubs of decreasing size form a hierarchy through which any two nodes connect in $\sim\ln\ln N$ steps (Cohen and Havlin, 2003). Above $\gamma = 3$ the hubs are too small to matter and the network behaves like a random one.

Even with a fair baseline, the network with $\gamma = 2.1$ has the shortest distances and they grow most slowly. The difference between $\ln N$ and $\ln\ln N$ is hard to see over two decades of $N$, though: $\ln\ln N$ changes from 1.5 to 2.3 between $N = 100$ and $30{,}000$. The regimes are asymptotic statements.

The degree sequences here are drawn with an exact discrete power-law sampler. The notebook's helper uses a rounding shortcut that over-represents degree 2 at $k_{\min} = 2$ (see N3), which makes its networks slightly sparser.

The heterogeneous degrees alone shorten paths. Six of the eight real networks are longer than their randomly wired versions: communities and geography use up links locally that would otherwise serve as shortcuts.
