Ultra-small worlds

Random networks are small worlds: the average distance grows only like $\ln N$. Scale-free networks are smaller still, because most short paths can run through a few very well-connected nodes, the hubs (Section 4.6).

Four regimes

$$\langle d\rangle \sim \begin{cases} \text{const} & \gamma = 2 \\ \ln\ln N & 2 < \gamma < 3 \\ \dfrac{\ln N}{\ln\ln N} & \gamma = 3 \\ \ln N & \gamma > 3 \end{cases}$$
$\langle d\rangle$
average shortest-path distance
$N$
number of nodes
$\gamma$
degree exponent
$\ln\ln N$
logarithm of the logarithm: grows extremely slowly
  • $\gamma = 2$: the largest hub grows like $N$ and links a finite share of all nodes, so distances stay constant.
  • $2 < \gamma < 3$: hubs of decreasing size form a hierarchy that connects any two nodes in $\sim\ln\ln N$ steps (Cohen and Havlin, 2003).
  • $\gamma = 3$: the borderline case; the hubs still shorten paths a little, by the factor $\ln\ln N$.
  • $\gamma > 3$: the hubs are too small to matter; distances grow like a random network's.

For a million nodes, $N = 10^6$: $\ln N = 13.8$ but $\ln\ln N = 2.6$. Ultra-small means paths about five times shorter, in this rough scaling.

Even against a random network with the same $\langle k\rangle$, $\gamma = 2.1$ gives the shortest distances, and they grow most slowly. Do not expect the curves to follow the formulas exactly: $\ln\ln N$ only goes from 1.5 to 2.3 between $N = 100$ and $30{,}000$. The four regimes describe very large $N$.

The degree sequences here use an exact discrete power-law sampler. The notebook's helper uses a rounding shortcut that over-represents degree 2 at $k_{\min} = 2$ (see N3), which makes its networks slightly sparser and so slightly longer.
