Universality

Cells, power grids, scientists, phones, email, routers and web pages: do networks this different share a degree distribution? Section 4.5 argues that heavy tails are universal. Here is the same test applied to all eight networks.

The fits

Each network was fitted with the Clauset–Shalizi–Newman method (Estimating the exponent): $k_{\min}$ by minimum KS distance, $\gamma$ by maximum likelihood, a bootstrap goodness-of-fit test with 1,000 synthetic data sets, and likelihood-ratio tests.

$k_{\min}$
the start of the fitted tail
$\hat\gamma$
the maximum-likelihood estimate of the exponent
$p$
the bootstrap goodness-of-fit p-value; the power law is ruled out if $p < 0.1$
$R$
the log-likelihood ratio; $R > 0$ favours the power law, and the sign is significant when its $p < 0.1$
  • Heavy tails are nearly universal. In seven of the eight networks the power law beats the exponential decisively; all but the power grid are far from Poisson.
  • Strict power laws are not. The bootstrap rules them out for collaboration, email and the WWW (narrowly also the Internet), where the log-normal fits significantly better. Where plausible, the power law often covers few nodes: 0.7% for the Internet, 2% for phone calls.
  • The power grid is the exception: $\hat\gamma \approx 7.6$ on 1% of the nodes, indistinguishable from an exponential. Geographic and engineering constraints leave no hubs.
  • Exponents vary from 1.9 (email) to 4.8 (phone calls), power grid aside. Only metabolism, protein interactions and the WWW fall inside $2 < \gamma < 3$ (see The role of γ).

The book fits in- and out-degrees of directed networks (WWW, email, metabolism, phone calls) separately in its Table 4.1. This site, like the notebook, treats every network as undirected, so for those four its exponents are not directly comparable. For undirected networks the agreement is close: Internet 3.40 against the book's 3.42.

If the log-normal often fits as well as or better than a power law, why does it matter which one we choose?

For many consequences it does not: hubs, short distances, robustness and fast spreading follow from a large $\langle k^2\rangle$, which both can produce. It matters for mechanism (preferential attachment, Chapter 5, predicts a power law; multiplicative random growth tends to give a log-normal) and for extrapolation (with $\gamma < 3$, $\langle k^2\rangle$ keeps growing with $N$; with a log-normal it does not).
  • A shared tail shape across unrelated systems suggests a shared mechanism.
  • Testing all eight networks with one method separates "heavy-tailed" from "strictly power-law".
  • Applies the method of Estimating the exponent to every network.
  • Places each network in a regime of the role of γ.
  • Motivates a mechanism: preferential attachment.
