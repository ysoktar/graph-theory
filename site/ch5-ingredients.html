Testing preferential attachment

The BA model has two ingredients. Are both needed? Do real networks actually attach preferentially, and does it have to be exactly linear? Sections 5.6–5.8 test the model piece by piece.

  • Removing one ingredient at a time shows what each one does.
  • Measuring attachment in real data checks the model's central assumption.
  • Nonlinear attachment shows how special the linear case is: only it gives a power law.

Both ingredients are needed (Section 5.6)

Is the power law caused by growth, by preferential attachment, or by both? Remove one at a time.

  • Model A, growth without preference: $\Pi(k_i) = 1/(m_0 + t - 1)$. The rate $dk_i/dt = m/(m_0 + t - 1)$ gives $k_i(t) = m\ln\big(e\,\frac{m_0 + t - 1}{m_0 + t_i - 1}\big) \approx m \ln(e\,t/t_i)$, only logarithmic growth, and an exponential distribution $$p_k = \frac{e}{m}\exp\Big(-\frac{k}{m}\Big).$$
  • Model B, preference without growth: $N$ is fixed and each step adds one link between a random node and a node chosen with $\Pi(k)$. Every degree grows linearly, $k_i(t) \approx 2t/N$; the distribution keeps changing and ends as a complete graph with $p_k = \delta(k - N + 1)$.

Both ingredients are needed: without preferential attachment the tail is exponential and no hubs form; without growth there is no stationary distribution at all. Together they are sufficient for a scale-free network with $\gamma = 3$.

Measuring preferential attachment (Section 5.7)

Preferential attachment makes two claims: the chance of gaining a link depends on $k$ (hypothesis 1), and the dependence is linear (hypothesis 2). Both can be tested with two maps of the same network taken a short time $\Delta t$ apart. For each node, the links it gained estimate $\Pi(k)$:

$$\Pi(k) \sim \frac{\Delta k_i}{\Delta t}, \qquad \pi(k) = \sum_{k_i = 0}^{k} \Pi(k_i).$$
$\Delta k_i$
links node $i$ gained between the two maps, grouped by its degree $k_i$ in the first map
$\pi(k)$
cumulative attachment function: summing smooths the noise of single degrees

If $\Pi(k) \sim k^\alpha$, then $\pi(k) \sim k^{\alpha + 1}$: no preference gives slope 1, linear preference slope 2. The Internet and citation networks give $\alpha \approx 1$; co-authorship and actors $\alpha = 0.9 \pm 0.1$, slightly sublinear.

Nonlinear preferential attachment (Section 5.8)

What if $\Pi(k) \sim k^\alpha$ with $\alpha \ne 1$?

  • $\alpha = 0$: no preference, Model A: exponential $p_k$.
  • $0 < \alpha < 1$, sublinear: a stretched exponential, $p_k \sim k^{-\alpha}\exp\!\big(-\frac{2\mu(\alpha)}{\langle k\rangle(1-\alpha)}k^{1-\alpha}\big)$, with fewer and smaller hubs; the largest grows only like $k_{\max} \sim (\ln t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.
  • $\alpha = 1$: the BA power law, $k_{\max} \sim t^{1/2}$.
  • $\alpha > 1$, superlinear: winner takes all. The earliest nodes become super-hubs holding a finite share of all links, $k_{\max} \sim t$, and the network turns hub-and-spoke.

Only exactly linear attachment gives a pure power law; a slightly sublinear $\Pi(k)$, as measured for collaborations, is one reason real tails bend down.

Worked example: reading α from data.

  1. Given: on log–log axes the measured $\pi(k)$ of a network rises from 0.01 at $k = 2$ to 0.79 at $k = 20$.
  2. Slope $= \log_{10}(0.79/0.01)/\log_{10}(20/2) = \log_{10} 79 = 1.9$.
  3. $\pi(k) \sim k^{\alpha+1}$, so $\alpha = 1.9 - 1 = 0.9$: sublinear.
  4. Meaning: expect a slightly curved tail (stretched exponential) and smaller hubs than the BA model predicts.

New nodes keep arriving, but each link attaches to an existing node chosen uniformly at random. What degree distribution results?

  1. A power law with $\gamma = 3$
  2. A power law with $\gamma < 3$
  3. An exponential distribution
  4. A complete graph
Without preference, old nodes gain only from their head start, and $p_k$ decays exponentially (Section 5.6).

Measured between two snapshots, a network's cumulative attachment function grows as $\pi(k) \sim k^{2.4}$. With $\pi(k) \sim k^{\alpha + 1}$, what kind of network should grow?

  1. A BA network with $\gamma = 3$
  2. $\alpha = 1.4$, superlinear: a few early super-hubs take a finite share of all links, a hub-and-spoke network
  3. $\alpha = 1.4$, sublinear: a stretched exponential with small hubs
  4. An exponential degree distribution
$\alpha = 2.4 - 1 = 1.4 > 1$: the rich-get-richer effect is amplified into winner takes all, and $k_{\max}$ grows linearly in time.
