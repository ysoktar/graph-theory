Clustering in random networks

Your friends tend to know each other. Do they in a random network? Almost never: clustering is the second property, after the degree distribution, on which the random model fails badly.

  • Predicts clustering (Part I) for chance wiring: a baseline for "a lot of triangles".
  • Real $C$ far above it → local mechanisms (shared contexts, triadic closure, teams).

The prediction (Section 3.9)

  1. Local clustering: $C_i = 2L_i/[k_i(k_i-1)]$ (Section 2.10).
  2. Node $i$'s $k_i$ neighbours form $k_i(k_i-1)/2$ pairs; each is linked with probability $p$, like any pair.
  3. So $\langle L_i\rangle = p\,k_i(k_i-1)/2$, and dividing:
$$\langle C_i\rangle = p = \frac{\langle k\rangle}{N-1} \approx \frac{\langle k\rangle}{N}.$$
$C_i$
fraction of pairs of $i$'s neighbours that are linked
$L_i$
links among $i$'s neighbours
$k_i$
degree of node $i$
$p$
link probability of any pair
  • Fixed $\langle k\rangle$: $C$ vanishes like $1/N$ as the network grows.
  • $C_i$ is independent of $k_i$: hubs and small nodes alike.

$\langle k\rangle = 10$: $C = 0.01$ at $N = 10^3$, $10^{-5}$ at $N = 10^6$. Collaboration: $8.08/23{,}133 = 3.5\times10^{-4}$.

Real networks

Every real network is far more clustered than random, and the gap grows with $N$. Collaboration: 0.633 vs $3.5\times10^{-4}$ (1,800×). A paper with three or more authors links all of them, creating triangles wholesale.

Clustering depends on the degree

  • Real $C(k)$ (average $C_i$ over nodes of degree $k$) falls with $k$, in several networks roughly like $1/k$: small nodes sit in tight groups, hubs bridge groups (hierarchical modularity, Ch 9).
  • Random wiring of the same degrees (orange triangles) recovers some clustering where hubs are big (E. coli: nearly half), not for collaboration. See Generating networks.
  • Builds on $C_i$ and $\langle C\rangle$ from Part I; $p$ from the model.
  • Fixed by Watts–Strogatz: high $C$ + short paths, but still no hubs.
  • Better null model: the configuration model keeps degrees; excess $C$ over it is beyond what degrees explain.
  • Leads to $C(k) \sim 1/k$ and communities (Ch 9).

Random networks predict $\langle C\rangle \to 0$ as $N$ grows. Why don't large social networks behave like that?

Ties form locally: workplaces, families, classes, friends introducing friends (triadic closure). Whether two of your friends know each other depends on shared context, not on world population, so $C$ stays roughly constant as $N$ grows. Watts–Strogatz builds this in.

Random network, $N = 10^5$, $\langle k\rangle = 20$. Expected $C_i$ of a node with $k = 200$?

  1. About $2\times10^{-4}$
  2. About $2\times10^{-3}$
  3. About $1/200$
  4. About $0.1$
$C = p \approx 20/10^5 = 2\times10^{-4}$ for every node.

Keep $\langle k\rangle = 10$, grow a random network from $10^3$ to $10^6$ nodes. $\langle C\rangle$ …

  1. stays at 0.01
  2. falls about 30×
  3. falls 1,000×, to $10^{-5}$
  4. grows
$\langle C\rangle = \langle k\rangle/N$: $10/10^3 \to 10/10^6$.

Collaboration: $\langle C\rangle = 0.633$, random 0.00035. Main cause?

  1. a higher average degree
  2. multi-author papers link all their authors to each other
  3. its hubs
  4. its large N
Each paper with $m$ authors adds a complete subgraph: all $m(m-1)/2$ pairs are linked.

Real $C(k) \sim 1/k$ means …

  1. all nodes are equally clustered
  2. hubs are the most clustered
  3. clustering is a sampling artefact
  4. small nodes sit in tight groups; hubs link groups that are not linked to each other
A hub's neighbours belong to different groups, so few of its neighbour pairs are linked.

Node with $k_i = 5$ and $L_i = 4$ links among its neighbours. $C_i$?

  1. 0.4
  2. 0.8
  3. 0.2
  4. 4
$C_i = 2 \times 4/(5 \times 4) = 0.4$: 4 of 10 neighbour pairs are linked.

In a random network, compare the expected $C_i$ of a hub ($k = 100$) and a small node ($k = 3$).

  1. Hub higher
  2. Hub lower, like $1/k$
  3. Equal, both $p$
  4. Undefined for the hub
Each neighbour pair is linked with probability $p$, whatever $k_i$.
