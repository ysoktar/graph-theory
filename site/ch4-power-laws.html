Power laws & hubs

The random network model failed most clearly on the degree distribution: real networks have hubs, nodes with far more links than the Poisson distribution allows. Chapter 4 replaces the Poisson distribution with a power law.

Scale-free networks (Section 4.2)

A network is called scale-free if its degree distribution follows a power law,

$$p_k \sim k^{-\gamma},$$

where $\gamma$ is the degree exponent. Taking logarithms gives $\log p_k \approx -\gamma \log k + \text{const}$, so a power law is a straight line of slope $-\gamma$ on log-log axes.

Degrees are integers, so the exact form is the discrete power law. For $k \ge k_{\min}$,

$$p_k = \frac{k^{-\gamma}}{\zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})}, \qquad \zeta(\gamma, k_{\min}) = \sum_{k = k_{\min}}^{\infty} k^{-\gamma},$$

where $\zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})$ is the Hurwitz zeta function, which ensures that the probabilities add up to 1 (for $k_{\min} = 1$ it is the Riemann zeta function $\zeta(\gamma)$). It is often easier to calculate with the continuous approximation, in which $k$ is any positive real number:

$$p(k) = (\gamma - 1)\, k_{\min}^{\gamma - 1}\, k^{-\gamma}, \qquad P(K \ge k) = \left(\frac{k}{k_{\min}}\right)^{-(\gamma - 1)}.$$

The two agree in the tail, but they can differ noticeably at small $k$. That matters when a fit is drawn or simulated (see Universality).

Where the hubs live

Compared with the Poisson distribution, the power law has more small-degree nodes, fewer nodes near the average, and vastly more high-degree nodes. With $\gamma = 2.5$ and $k_{\min} = 2$, a network of a million nodes should contain about 2,000 nodes with $k \ge 100$. The Poisson distribution expects about $10^{-87}$ of them.

Real data: the Internet

On linear axes the two distributions look similar: both are bumps at small $k$. On log-log axes the difference is enormous. The Internet has 2,273 routers with more than 50 links; a random network of the same size and density would almost certainly not have a single one.

In a random network $\langle k\rangle$ is a "typical" degree: most nodes have about that many links. Is that true for a power law with $\gamma = 2.5$?

No. Most nodes have fewer links than the average (with $k_{\min} = 1$, about 74% have $k = 1$, while $\langle k\rangle \approx 1.95$), and a few have vastly more. The average is pulled up by the hubs and describes almost nobody. Because no degree is typical, the distribution has no characteristic scale, hence "scale-free". The No typical degree page makes this precise.

On log-log axes, $p_k$ of a network falls on a straight line with slope $-2.3$. What is $\gamma$, and what is the slope of $P(K \ge k)$?

  1. $\gamma = -2.3$; slope $-2.3$
  2. $\gamma = 2.3$; slope $-1.3$
  3. $\gamma = 2.3$; slope $-3.3$
  4. $\gamma = 1.3$; slope $-2.3$
$p_k \sim k^{-2.3}$ means $\gamma = 2.3$. Summing (or integrating) over all degrees at least $k$ raises the exponent by one: $P(K\ge k) \sim k^{-(\gamma-1)} = k^{-1.3}$.
