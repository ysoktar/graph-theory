Power laws & hubs

The random network model failed most clearly on one property: real networks have hubs, nodes with far more links than a Poisson distribution allows. Chapter 4 replaces the Poisson distribution with a power law, which allows them.

Scale-free networks (Section 4.2)

The degree distribution $p_k$ (Part I) is called scale-free if it follows a power law:

$$p_k \sim k^{-\gamma}.$$
$p_k$
share of nodes with exactly $k$ links
$k$
degree (number of links)
$\gamma$
degree exponent; real networks: about 2 to 3.5
$\sim$
proportional to, for large $k$
  • Straight line on log-log axes: $\log p_k \approx -\gamma \log k + \text{const}$, slope $-\gamma$.
  • No characteristic scale: doubling $k$ always divides $p_k$ by $2^{\gamma}$, from 2 → 4 links as from 200 → 400. A Poisson distribution has a scale, $\langle k\rangle$, beyond which it collapses.

$\gamma = 2.5$: doubling $k$ divides $p_k$ by $2^{2.5} \approx 5.7$; multiplying $k$ by 10 divides it by $10^{2.5} \approx 316$.

Degrees are integers, so the exact form is the discrete power law, starting at a smallest degree $k_{\min}$ (it cannot start at 0, where $k^{-\gamma}$ is infinite):

$$p_k = \frac{k^{-\gamma}}{\zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})} \quad (k \ge k_{\min}), \qquad \zeta(\gamma, k_{\min}) = \sum_{k = k_{\min}}^{\infty} k^{-\gamma}.$$
$k_{\min}$
smallest degree the power law describes
$\zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})$
Hurwitz zeta function: normalising sum, finite only for $\gamma > 1$
$\zeta(\gamma)$
Riemann zeta function, the case $k_{\min} = 1$

$\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 1$: $\zeta(2.5) = 1.342$, so $p_1 = 0.745$, $p_2 = 0.132$, and $\langle k\rangle = \zeta(1.5)/\zeta(2.5) = 1.95$.

It is often easier to treat $k$ as a positive real number. The continuous power law and its complementary cumulative distribution (CCDF) are

$$p(k) = (\gamma - 1)\, k_{\min}^{\gamma - 1}\, k^{-\gamma}, \qquad P(K \ge k) = \left(\frac{k}{k_{\min}}\right)^{-(\gamma - 1)}.$$
$p(k)$
probability density of degree $k$
$(\gamma - 1)\,k_{\min}^{\gamma - 1}$
normalising constant (continuous counterpart of $1/\zeta$)
$K$
degree of a randomly chosen node
$P(K \ge k)$
share of nodes with at least $k$ links; exponent $\gamma - 1$

The two agree in the tail but differ at small $k$, which matters when a fit is drawn or simulated (see Universality).

$\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 1$: share with $k \ge 2$ is 0.255 (discrete) against 0.354 (continuous).

Where the hubs live

Against a Poisson distribution with the same average, the power law has more small-degree nodes, fewer near the average and vastly more hubs. With $\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 2$ and $N = 10^6$: about 2,000 nodes with $k \ge 100$; Poisson expects $10^{-87}$.

Real data: the Internet

On linear axes both look like bumps at small $k$; on log-log axes the difference is enormous. The Internet has 2,375 routers with $k \ge 50$, 534 with $k \ge 100$ and one with 1,071. A random network of the same size and density expects $10^{-22}$ with $k \ge 50$.

  • The Poisson model is fixed by $\langle k\rangle$ alone and cannot produce hubs.
  • The power law adds one parameter, $\gamma$, that controls the tail.
  • From $\gamma$ we can predict the largest hub, the degree variance, path lengths, robustness and epidemic spreading.

In a random network $\langle k\rangle$ is a "typical" degree. Is it for a power law with $\gamma = 2.5$?

No. With $k_{\min} = 1$, 75% of nodes have $k = 1$ while $\langle k\rangle \approx 1.95$; the hubs pull the average up. On the Internet 76% of routers are below the average 6.3 and the median is 3. No degree is typical, hence "scale-free"; see No typical degree.
  • Replaces the Poisson $p_k$ of the random network, which failed on real data.
  • Needs correct plotting and fitting before $\gamma$ can be trusted.
  • Predicts hub size, moments and distances.
  • Explained by growth and preferential attachment in Chapter 5.

$p_k$ is a straight line of slope $-2.3$ on log-log axes. What is $\gamma$, and the slope of $P(K \ge k)$?

  1. $\gamma = -2.3$; slope $-2.3$
  2. $\gamma = 2.3$; slope $-1.3$
  3. $\gamma = 2.3$; slope $-3.3$
  4. $\gamma = 1.3$; slope $-2.3$
$p_k \sim k^{-2.3}$ means $\gamma = 2.3$. Summing over all degrees $\ge k$ lowers the exponent by one: $P(K\ge k) \sim k^{-1.3}$.

With $\gamma = 3$, 8,000 nodes have 10 links. About how many have 20?

  1. 4,000
  2. 2,000
  3. 1,000
  4. 800
Doubling $k$ divides $p_k$ by $2^3 = 8$: $8{,}000/8 = 1{,}000$. The same factor holds from 100 to 200 or 1,000 to 2,000 links.

A random and a scale-free network both have $N = 10^6$ and $\langle k\rangle = 4.7$. Which is true?

  1. The scale-free one has more nodes with very few links and many more with very many
  2. Their degree distributions are the same
  3. The scale-free one has more nodes near the average
  4. Only the number of hubs differs
Same average, different shape: the power law has more small-$k$ nodes, fewer near $\langle k\rangle$, vastly more hubs. Try $\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 2$ ($\langle k\rangle \approx 4.7$) in the first figure.

Why can $p_k = k^{-\gamma}/\zeta(\gamma, k_{\min})$ not be used with $\gamma = 0.8$?

  1. $\gamma$ must be a whole number
  2. There would be no hubs
  3. The network would need more than $N$ nodes
  4. $\zeta(0.8, k_{\min})$ is infinite, so the probabilities cannot sum to 1
For $\gamma \le 1$ the terms $k^{-\gamma}$ fall too slowly and their sum diverges, so no constant normalises them.
