The Barabási–Albert model

Chapter 4 showed that real networks have hubs, but not why. The random network has none; the configuration model only reproduces the hubs we feed in. What process, repeated as a network grows, makes hubs on its own?

  • Random and configuration models are descriptive: what does such a network look like?
  • The BA model is mechanistic: how did it get that way?
  • A mechanism predicts growth, explains why unrelated systems share a pattern, and says which rule change removes it.

Growth and preferential attachment (Section 5.3)

  • Growth: nodes keep arriving (pages, papers, users).
  • Preferential attachment: newcomers link to well-connected nodes more often.

Start with $m_0$ linked nodes; each step, add one node with $m \le m_0$ links, each attaching to node $i$ with probability

$$\Pi(k_i) = \frac{k_i}{\sum_j k_j}.$$

$t = 1{,}000$, $m = 2$: total degree $\approx 4{,}000$. A 40-link node: $\Pi = 1\%$; a 2-link node: $0.05\%$. Twenty times faster growth.

Why a power law (Sections 5.4–5.5)

Treat $t$ and $k$ as continuous (the continuum approximation):

  1. Growth rate: $\dfrac{dk_i}{dt} = m\,\Pi(k_i) = \dfrac{m\,k_i}{2mt} = \dfrac{k_i}{2t}$.
  2. Node $i$ arrives at $t_i$ with $m$ links, so $k_i(t) = m\,(t/t_i)^{1/2}$.
  3. $k_i < k$ means $t_i > m^2 t/k^2$; nodes arrive uniformly, so $P(K < k) = 1 - m^2/k^2$.
  4. Differentiate: $p_k \approx 2m^2 k^{-3}$.

power law with $\gamma = 3$

Older nodes are further ahead: the first-mover advantage. The exact solution (Eq. 5.11, Section 5.5; derived in Advanced Topic 5.A) is

$$p_k = \frac{2m(m+1)}{k(k+1)(k+2)} \qquad (k \ge m).$$
  • $\gamma = 3$ for every $m$ and starting network.
  • Largest hub: $k_{\max} \sim m N^{1/2}$, the natural cutoff for $\gamma = 3$ (The largest hub).

$m = 2$: $p_2 = \frac{2\cdot2\cdot3}{2\cdot3\cdot4} = \frac12$ (generally $p_m = 2/(m+2)$). $p_{10} = 0.009$ exact vs $0.008$ continuum.

Both ingredients are needed (Section 5.6). Growth without preference gives an exponential $p_k$ ("uniform attachment" above). Preference without growth never settles: the network fills up until complete.

  • Explains the power law of Ch 4, with $\gamma = 3$: the border of the scale-free regime ($\langle k^2\rangle \sim \ln N$).
  • Generates what the configuration model takes as input; its old nodes are also wired non-randomly.
  • Generalized by the fitness model: nodes grow at different rates, so latecomers can win.

BA gives $\gamma = 3$, yet the book's networks have 1.9 to 4.8, and more clustering. What is missing?

Fitness (Ch 6), links added or removed between existing nodes, nonlinear attachment $\Pi \sim k^\alpha$ ($\alpha \ne 1$ breaks the power law), ageing. BA clustering vanishes as $(\ln N)^2/N$.

Why is $\gamma = 3$ for every $m$?

  1. By definition
  2. The seed has three nodes
  3. Degrees are capped at 3
  4. $m$ cancels: $dk_i/dt = k_i/(2t)$
More links per newcomer also means more total degree to compete with. So $\beta = 1/2$ and $\gamma = 1 + 1/\beta = 3$.
