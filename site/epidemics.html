Spreading & vaccination

Infections travel along links. On a scale-free network the hubs that hold it together also carry outbreaks everywhere, which changes how epidemics behave and how to stop them (Chapter 10).

  • Classical epidemiology assumes everyone meets everyone; an epidemic needs $R_0 > 1$
  • The network model replaces that with "you meet your neighbours".
  • It predicts what mixing models cannot: with hubs, even weak diseases spread and random vaccination barely helps.
  • It tells us whom to vaccinate instead.

SIR on a network

Nodes are Susceptible, Infected or Recovered. An infected node tries once to infect each susceptible neighbour, succeeding with probability $T$, then recovers.

$T$
transmissibility: chance of passing the infection along one link
$\beta, \tau$
infection rate per link, recovery time; $T = 1 - e^{-\beta\tau}$ (Advanced Topic 10.D)

The epidemic threshold

  1. Infection arrives along a link, so it reaches a degree-$k$ node with probability $k\,p_k/\langle k\rangle$.
  2. One link leads back to the infector; $k-1$ remain. Average: $(\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle)/\langle k\rangle = \kappa - 1$.
  3. Each transmits with probability $T$: new infections per case $R = T(\kappa - 1)$.
  4. The outbreak grows if $R > 1$. Setting $R = 1$:
$$T_c = \frac{1}{\kappa - 1} = \frac{\langle k\rangle}{\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle}.$$
$T_c$
critical transmissibility
$p_k$
degree distribution
$\langle k\rangle, \langle k^2\rangle$
mean degree, mean squared degree
$\kappa$
$\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$, mean degree of a node reached along a link (as in Molloy–Reed)
$R$
new infections caused by one case
  • Random network: $\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle = \langle k\rangle^2$, so $T_c = 1/\langle k\rangle$ — the mixing-model answer.
  • Scale-free, $\gamma < 3$: $\langle k^2\rangle \to \infty$, so $T_c \to 0$ (Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani 2001).
  • Mean-field SIS gives $\langle k\rangle/\langle k^2\rangle$: same behaviour.

Email network: $\langle k\rangle = 3.23$, $\langle k^2\rangle = 1269$ → $\kappa = 392.6$, $T_c = 1/391.6 = 0.0026$. Random network, same $\langle k\rangle$: $T_c = 0.31$.

Whom to vaccinate?

A vaccinated node is, for the epidemic, a removed node: vaccination is robustness in reverse.

  • Random: a fraction $g$ cuts $R$ to $(1-g)\,T(\kappa-1)$, so $g_c = 1 - \dfrac{1}{T(\kappa - 1)}$ — near 1 when $\kappa$ is large.
  • Hubs: cuts $\langle k^2\rangle$ far more than $\langle k\rangle$, raising $T_c$. Needs the whole network.
  • Acquaintance (Cohen, Havlin and ben-Avraham 2003): vaccinate a random contact of a random person. Contacts average $\kappa$ links, so this finds hubs blindly.
$g_c$
random-vaccination coverage that stops the epidemic

Internet, $\kappa = 37.9$, worm with $T = 0.1$: $R = 3.7$, $g_c = 1 - 1/3.7 = 73\%$. Random network, same $\langle k\rangle = 6.34$: $R = 0.63$, no outbreak.

The $\kappa$ that makes heavy-tailed networks robust to failures (Ch 8) makes them vulnerable to epidemics; the remedy mirrors the attack: vaccinate the hubs.

  • Special case of percolation: keep each link with probability $T$; the outbreak is the starting node's cluster. $T_c = 1 - f_c$ (Robustness).
  • Explained by the friendship paradox: both infection and acquaintance vaccination find hubs (Correlations).
  • Builds on the divergent $\langle k^2\rangle$ (Ch 4), produced by preferential attachment.
  • Slowed by communities: few links between groups.

You can vaccinate 1% of a scale-free population. Whom, and why does $\langle k^2\rangle$ matter?

The hubs if known: removing them cuts $\langle k^2\rangle$ and raises $T_c$. Otherwise acquaintance vaccination, since a random contact has $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ contacts on average.

$\langle k\rangle = 4$, $\langle k^2\rangle = 84$. What is $T_c$?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.05
  3. 0.5
  4. 0.012
$T_c = 4/(84 - 4) = 0.05$, five times below the random network's $1/4$.

Why can a newly infected node pass the infection on along only $k - 1$ links?

  1. One link is always too weak.
  2. It recovers too early.
  3. One link leads back to its infector.
  4. Hubs lose a link.
The infector is already infected. The same −1 appears in Molloy–Reed.

The Internet loses its giant component after $f_c = 97.3\%$ random failures. What is a worm's $T_c$ there?

  1. $1 - f_c = 2.7\%$
  2. $f_c = 97.3\%$
  3. Unrelated to $f_c$
  4. About 16%
$f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa - 1)$ and $T_c = 1/(\kappa - 1)$, so $T_c = 1 - f_c$.

$T = 0.1$, $\kappa = 21$. What fraction must be vaccinated at random?

  1. 10%
  2. 50%
  3. 90%
  4. Everyone
$R = 0.1 \times 20 = 2$, so $g_c = 1 - 1/2 = 50\%$.

Why does vaccinating a named contact beat vaccinating the person asked?

  1. Contacts are healthier.
  2. It uses fewer doses.
  3. It needs the contact network.
  4. A random contact has $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ links on average.
Hubs appear in many contact lists. Email network: a person has 3.2 contacts, a contact 393.

Random network, $\langle k\rangle = 5$. $T_c$?

  1. 0.2
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.04
  4. 5
$T_c = \langle k\rangle/\langle k\rangle^2 = 1/5$.

  2. rises

  4. jumps to 1
