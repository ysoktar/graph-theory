No typical degree: what "scale-free" means

In a random network the average degree is a natural scale: almost every node has about $\langle k\rangle$ links. A power law has no such scale, because its second moment grows without bound (Section 4.4).

Moments of a power law

The $n$-th moment of the degree distribution is $\langle k^n\rangle = \sum_k k^n p_k$. For a continuous power law between $k_{\min}$ and $k_{\max}$:

$$\langle k^n\rangle = \int_{k_{\min}}^{k_{\max}} k^n p(k)\, dk = \frac{\gamma - 1}{n - \gamma + 1}\, k_{\min}^{\gamma - 1}\left(k_{\max}^{\,n - \gamma + 1} - k_{\min}^{\,n - \gamma + 1}\right).$$
$n$
order of the moment ($n = 1$: mean, $n = 2$: second moment)
$\langle k^n\rangle$
average of $k^n$ over all nodes
$k_{\min}, k_{\max}$
smallest and largest degree
$n - \gamma + 1$
sign decides whether the moment converges
  1. $k_{\max}$ grows with $N$ (previous page).
  2. If $n - \gamma + 1 < 0$, $k_{\max}^{\,n-\gamma+1} \to 0$: the moment converges.
  3. If $n - \gamma + 1 > 0$, $k_{\max}^{\,n-\gamma+1} \to \infty$: the moment diverges.

For the typical range $2 < \gamma < 3$:

  • $\langle k\rangle$ is finite;
  • $\langle k^2\rangle$, and with it the variance $\sigma_k^2 = \langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle^2$, diverges as $N \to \infty$.

$\gamma = 2.5$, $n = 2$: $n - \gamma + 1 = 0.5$, so $\langle k^2\rangle \propto k_{\max}^{0.5} \propto N^{1/3}$; a thousandfold larger network has a tenfold larger $\langle k^2\rangle$.

The sample mean settles quickly, but $\langle k^2\rangle$ keeps climbing with $N$ along the predicted slope, while the Poisson value stays put. With $\gamma > 3$ (try 3.5) both converge, and moments alone hardly separate the network from a random one.

The book's networks

In a random network $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$ ($\sigma_k$ — standard deviation of the degrees). If $\sigma_k \gg \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$, a random node can have one link or a thousand, and $\langle k\rangle$ says little about it.

Random, $\langle k\rangle = 9$: $\sigma_k = 3$, and 99.7% of nodes have 0–18 links. Email: $\langle k\rangle = 3.23$, $\sigma_k = 35.5$, eleven times the mean.

In six of the eight networks $\sigma_k$ is 2.9 to 20 times the random value, and in the same six it exceeds $\langle k\rangle$ itself (11 times for email). The power grid ($\sigma_k = 1.79$ against 1.63) behaves like a random network, and phone calls are only mildly broader (1.57 times). "No typical degree" describes most, not all, real networks.

$\langle k^2\rangle$ is the quantity that matters in the chapters ahead: the giant component of the configuration model ($\langle k^2\rangle > 2\langle k\rangle$, the Molloy–Reed criterion), robustness to random failures (Ch 8) and the epidemic threshold (Ch 10). With a divergent $\langle k^2\rangle$, removing random nodes hardly destroys the giant component.

  • "Scale-free" means exactly this: no finite variance, so no typical degree.
  • The random model sets $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$; real networks break that by factors up to 20.
  • $\langle k^2\rangle$, not $\langle k\rangle$, controls robustness and spreading.
  • Follows from the growing largest hub.
  • Classifies the regimes of the role of γ.
  • Enters distances and the configuration model.

A random network has $\langle k\rangle = 16$. What is $\sigma_k$?

  1. 16
  2. 4
  3. 8
  4. 256
Poisson variance equals the mean: $\sigma_k = \sqrt{16} = 4$.
