No typical degree: what "scale-free" means

In a random network the average degree is a natural scale: almost every node has about $\langle k\rangle$ links. A power law has no such scale, because its second moment grows without bound (Section 4.4).

Moments of a power law

The $n$-th moment of the degree distribution is $\langle k^n\rangle = \sum_k k^n p_k$. For a continuous power law between $k_{\min}$ and $k_{\max}$,

$$\langle k^n\rangle = \int_{k_{\min}}^{k_{\max}} k^n p(k)\, dk = \frac{\gamma - 1}{n - \gamma + 1}\, k_{\min}^{\gamma - 1}\left(k_{\max}^{\,n - \gamma + 1} - k_{\min}^{\,n - \gamma + 1}\right).$$

As the network grows, $k_{\max}$ grows with it (previous page). If $n - \gamma + 1 < 0$ the $k_{\max}$ term vanishes and the moment converges; if $n - \gamma + 1 > 0$ it grows without bound. For the typical range $2 < \gamma < 3$:

  • the first moment $\langle k\rangle$ is finite;
  • the second moment $\langle k^2\rangle$, and with it the variance $\sigma_k^2 = \langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle^2$, diverges as $N \to \infty$.

The power law's sample mean settles quickly, but its $\langle k^2\rangle$ keeps climbing with $N$ along the predicted slope, while the Poisson value stays put. With $\gamma > 3$ (try 3.5) both moments converge, and a large network is hard to tell apart from a random one by its moments alone.

The book's networks

For a random network $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$: with $\langle k\rangle = 9$, 99.7% of the nodes have between 0 and 18 links. If a network has $\sigma_k \gg \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$, a randomly chosen node can have one link or a thousand, and $\langle k\rangle$ tells us little about it.

In six of the eight networks $\sigma_k$ is 3 to 20 times the random value, and in five it exceeds $\langle k\rangle$ itself (11 times for email). The power grid ($\sigma_k = 1.79$ against 1.63) behaves like a random network, and the phone-call network is only mildly broader (1.57 times). "No typical degree" describes most, but not all, real networks.

$\langle k^2\rangle$ is the quantity that matters in the chapters ahead. It sets the threshold of the giant component in the configuration model ($\langle k^2\rangle > 2\langle k\rangle$, the Molloy–Reed criterion), the robustness of a network to random failures (Ch 8) and the epidemic threshold (Ch 10). A network with a divergent $\langle k^2\rangle$ has a giant component that is very hard to destroy by removing random nodes.
