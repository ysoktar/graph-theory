The evolution of a random network

Start with $N$ isolated nodes and add random links one at a time. For a while nothing much happens. Then, abruptly, a single component swallows most of the network. This is a phase transition, and it happens at exactly one link per node.

Watch it happen

  • Below $\langle k\rangle = 1$ the network is a dust of small trees; the largest piece holds a vanishing fraction of the nodes.
  • Just above $\langle k\rangle = 1$ a giant component emerges and grows quickly: at $\langle k\rangle = 2$ it already holds 80% of the nodes. The transition is continuous but abrupt.
  • The isolated nodes are the last to join. The network becomes connected only around $\langle k\rangle \approx \ln N$.

Four regimes (Section 3.6)

RegimeConditionLargest component $N_G$
Subcritical$\langle k\rangle < 1$no giant component; $N_G \sim \ln N$, small isolated trees
Critical$\langle k\rangle = 1$$N_G \sim N^{2/3}$: large, but a vanishing fraction of $N$
Supercritical$1 < \langle k\rangle < \ln N$a giant component with $N_G \approx S N$, coexisting with many small components
Connected$\langle k\rangle > \ln N$one component holding all nodes

The size of the giant component

A node is outside the giant component if none of its links leads into it. With probability $u = 1 - S$ a random neighbour is outside, and averaging over the Poisson-distributed degree gives $u = e^{-\langle k\rangle(1-u)}$ (book, Advanced Topic 3.C). In terms of $S$:

$$S = 1 - e^{-\langle k\rangle S}.$$

$S = 0$ is always a solution. A second, non-zero solution exists only when the curve $1 - e^{-\langle k\rangle S}$ starts out steeper than the diagonal. Its slope at $S = 0$ is $\langle k\rangle$, so a giant component exists if and only if $\langle k\rangle > 1$.

Finite networks

The theory is exact for $N \to \infty$. In a finite network the transition is smeared out. The size of the second-largest component is a good detector of it: it is tiny on both sides and largest near the critical point, where the giant component is still assembling.

On log-log axes the three regimes separate cleanly. At $\langle k\rangle = 2$, $N_G$ is proportional to $N$ (slope 1). At $\langle k\rangle = 1$ it grows like $N^{2/3}$. At $\langle k\rangle = 0.5$ it barely grows at all, like $\ln N$.

Real networks are supercritical (Section 3.7)

Where do the book's networks sit on this phase diagram? If they were random networks with their own $N$ and $\langle k\rangle$:

Every network has $\langle k\rangle > 1$, so all are supercritical, and seven of the eight have $\langle k\rangle < \ln N$. Random networks with these parameters would therefore not be connected: they would leave about $N e^{-\langle k\rangle}$ isolated nodes. That is a large share only for the sparse networks (power grid and protein, about 9% outside the giant component), and a tiny one for the denser ones (Internet 0.2%, collaboration 0.03%).

Why might a power grid be fully connected even though $\langle k\rangle < \ln N$? (Notebook II.5.)

A random network with the grid's $\langle k\rangle = 2.67$ would leave about 9% of its nodes outside the giant component. A power grid that left 9% of its substations unconnected would be useless, so it was designed to be connected. Real networks are built or grown under constraints and incentives; they are not wired at random. The protein network, at the same density, does leave 18% of its nodes outside the giant component. Partly this reflects incomplete measurement (undiscovered interactions), not only biology.
