The evolution of a random network

When does a random network hold together? Add random links to $N$ isolated nodes one at a time: for a while only small clusters form, then one component abruptly swallows most nodes. This phase transition happens at exactly one link per node.

  • Predicts connectedness (Part I) from $\langle k\rangle$ alone.
  • Explains why sparse networks can still be largely connected: $\langle k\rangle > 1$ suffices for a giant component.
  • Gives the template for later thresholds: robustness, epidemics.

Watch it happen

  • $\langle k\rangle < 1$: small trees only.
  • Just above 1 a giant component (a component holding a finite fraction of all nodes) appears; at $\langle k\rangle = 2$ it holds 80%.
  • Isolated nodes join last; full connection only near $\langle k\rangle \approx \ln N$.

Four regimes (Section 3.6)

RegimeConditionLargest component $N_G$
Subcritical$\langle k\rangle < 1$no giant component; $N_G \sim \ln N$
Critical$\langle k\rangle = 1$$N_G \sim N^{2/3}$: a vanishing fraction of $N$
Supercritical$1 < \langle k\rangle < \ln N$$N_G \approx S N$, plus many small components
Connected$\langle k\rangle > \ln N$one component holding all nodes

$\sim$ means "grows like, up to a constant factor"; $\ln$ is the natural logarithm (base $e$).

Size of the giant component (Advanced Topic 3.C)

  1. Let $u$ = probability that a node is not reached through one given other node: either no link ($1-p$) or a link to a node outside the giant component ($p\,u$).
  2. Outside ⇔ this holds for all $N-1$ others: $u = (1 - p + p\,u)^{N-1}$.
  3. With $p = \langle k\rangle/(N-1)$ and $N \to \infty$: $u = e^{-\langle k\rangle(1-u)}$.
  4. The fraction inside is $S = 1 - u$:
$$S = 1 - e^{-\langle k\rangle S}.$$

$S = 0$ always solves it. A non-zero solution needs the curve $1 - e^{-\langle k\rangle S}$ to start steeper than the diagonal; its slope at $S = 0$ is $\langle k\rangle$. So: giant component ⇔ $\langle k\rangle > 1$.

$S(1.5) = 0.583$, $S(2) = 0.797$, $S(3) = 0.941$, $S(5) = 0.993$; $S(0.8) = 0$.

When is it connected?

  1. Expected isolated nodes: $N p_0 = N e^{-\langle k\rangle}$.
  2. Fewer than one ⇔ $\langle k\rangle > \ln N$.

$N = 10^4$: $\ln N = 9.21$, i.e. $L \approx 46{,}050$ links. At $\langle k\rangle = 5$: $10^4 e^{-5} \approx 67$ isolated nodes.

Finite networks

The theory is exact for $N \to \infty$; finite networks smear the transition. The second-largest component detects it: small on both sides, largest near the critical point.

On log-log axes a power law $N_G \propto N^a$ is a straight line of slope $a$: slope 1 at $\langle k\rangle = 2$, $2/3$ at $\langle k\rangle = 1$, nearly flat ($\ln N$) at $\langle k\rangle = 0.5$.

Real networks are supercritical (Section 3.7)

All eight have $\langle k\rangle > 1$; seven have $\langle k\rangle < \ln N$, so their random counterparts would not be connected. Expected share outside the giant component: about 9% for power grid and protein, 0.2% Internet, 0.03% collaboration.

  • Builds on Poisson $p_0 = e^{-\langle k\rangle}$ and Part I components.
  • Special case of Molloy–Reed $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle > 2$ (Robustness): Poisson gives $1 + \langle k\rangle > 2$ ⇔ $\langle k\rangle > 1$.
  • Contrast: for scale-free networks with $\gamma < 3$, $\langle k^2\rangle$ diverges, so random node removal cannot break the giant component (Ch 8).
  • Analogue: the epidemic threshold is also set by $\langle k\rangle$ and $\langle k^2\rangle$.

Why might a power grid be fully connected although $\langle k\rangle < \ln N$? (Notebook II.5.)

Its random counterpart would leave about 9% of nodes outside the giant component; a grid like that would be useless, so it was designed to be connected. Real networks are built under constraints, not wired at random. The protein network, at the same density, does leave 18% outside, partly because of undiscovered interactions.
