The evolution of a random network

When does a random network hold together? Start from $N$ isolated nodes and add random links one at a time. For a while only small clusters form; then one component abruptly swallows most of the nodes. This phase transition happens at exactly one link per node.

  • It predicts connectedness (Part I) from the average degree $\langle k\rangle$ alone.
  • It explains why sparse networks can still be largely connected: $\langle k\rangle > 1$ is enough for a giant component.
  • It is the template for later thresholds, in robustness and epidemics.

Watch it happen

  • For $\langle k\rangle < 1$ there are only small trees.
  • Just above 1 a giant component appears (a component that holds a finite fraction of all nodes). At $\langle k\rangle = 2$ it holds 80% of them.
  • Isolated nodes join last, and the network becomes fully connected only near $\langle k\rangle \approx \ln N$.

Four regimes (Section 3.6)

RegimeConditionLargest component $N_G$
Subcritical$\langle k\rangle < 1$no giant component; $N_G \sim \ln N$
Critical$\langle k\rangle = 1$$N_G \sim N^{2/3}$: a vanishing fraction of $N$
Supercritical$1 < \langle k\rangle < \ln N$$N_G \approx S N$, plus many small components
Connected$\langle k\rangle > \ln N$one component holding all nodes

Here $\sim$ means "grows like, up to a constant factor", and $\ln$ is the natural logarithm (base $e$).

The size of the giant component (Advanced Topic 3.C)

  1. Let $u$ be the probability that a node is not connected to the giant component through one given other node. Either the two are not linked (probability $1-p$), or they are linked but the other node is outside the giant component (probability $p\,u$).
  2. A node is outside the giant component when this holds for all $N-1$ other nodes: $u = (1 - p + p\,u)^{N-1}$.
  3. With $p = \langle k\rangle/(N-1)$ and $N \to \infty$ this becomes $u = e^{-\langle k\rangle(1-u)}$.
  4. The fraction inside is $S = 1 - u$, so:
$$S = 1 - e^{-\langle k\rangle S}.$$

$S = 0$ always solves the equation. A non-zero solution exists only if the curve $1 - e^{-\langle k\rangle S}$ starts out steeper than the diagonal, and its slope at $S = 0$ is $\langle k\rangle$. So: a giant component exists exactly when $\langle k\rangle > 1$.

$S(1.5) = 0.583$, $S(2) = 0.797$, $S(3) = 0.941$ and $S(5) = 0.993$, while $S(0.8) = 0$.

When is it connected?

  1. The expected number of isolated nodes is $N p_0 = N e^{-\langle k\rangle}$.
  2. It drops below one exactly when $\langle k\rangle > \ln N$.

For $N = 10^4$, $\ln N = 9.21$, which takes $L \approx 46{,}050$ links. At $\langle k\rangle = 5$ there are still $10^4 e^{-5} \approx 67$ isolated nodes.

Finite networks

The theory is exact for $N \to \infty$; in finite networks the transition is smeared out. The second-largest component reveals where it is: it is small on both sides of the transition and largest near the critical point.

On log-log axes a power law $N_G \propto N^a$ is a straight line of slope $a$. The slope is 1 at $\langle k\rangle = 2$, $2/3$ at $\langle k\rangle = 1$, and nearly flat ($\ln N$) at $\langle k\rangle = 0.5$.

Real networks are supercritical (Section 3.7)

All eight networks have $\langle k\rangle > 1$, and seven have $\langle k\rangle < \ln N$, so their random counterparts would not be connected. The share of nodes expected outside the giant component is about 9% for the power grid and the protein network, 0.2% for the Internet and 0.03% for the collaboration network.

  • Builds on the Poisson value $p_0 = e^{-\langle k\rangle}$ and on the components of Part I.
  • Is a special case of the Molloy–Reed condition $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle > 2$ (Robustness): for Poisson degrees it reads $1 + \langle k\rangle > 2$, that is, $\langle k\rangle > 1$.
  • Contrast: for scale-free networks with $\gamma < 3$, $\langle k^2\rangle$ diverges, so removing nodes at random cannot break the giant component (Ch 8).
  • Analogue: the epidemic threshold is also set by $\langle k\rangle$ and $\langle k^2\rangle$.

Why might a power grid be fully connected although $\langle k\rangle < \ln N$? (Notebook II.5.)

Its random counterpart would leave about 9% of the nodes outside the giant component, and a grid like that would be useless, so it was designed to be connected. Real networks are built under constraints, not wired at random. The protein network, at the same density, does leave 18% outside, partly because some interactions have not been discovered yet.
