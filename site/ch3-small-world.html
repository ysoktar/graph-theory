Small worlds

Why are any two people on Earth only about six handshakes apart? Random networks show that such short chains are unavoidable once links are random; real networks mostly confirm it, with one instructive exception.

  • Predicts distances (Part I) from $N$ and $\langle k\rangle$.
  • Explains "six degrees": distance grows like $\ln N$, not like a power of $N$.
  • Real networks shorter than predicted → hubs; longer → spatial constraints.

Karinthy's 1929 story Láncszemek ("Chains") bet on five intermediaries. Milgram (1967) tested it: people in Nebraska and Kansas forwarded a letter, via first-name acquaintances only, to one of two targets in Massachusetts (in Boston and in Sharon). Completed chains had about five intermediaries: "six degrees of separation".

Why distances are short (Section 3.8)

  1. A node has about $\langle k\rangle$ neighbours, $\langle k\rangle^2$ nodes at distance 2, $\langle k\rangle^d$ at distance $d$ (assuming no node is counted twice).
  2. Nodes within distance $d$:
$$N(d) \approx 1 + \langle k\rangle + \langle k\rangle^2 + \dots + \langle k\rangle^d = \frac{\langle k\rangle^{d+1} - 1}{\langle k\rangle - 1}.$$
  1. $N(d)$ cannot exceed $N$: set $\langle k\rangle^{d} \approx N$, take logarithms, $d \ln\langle k\rangle \approx \ln N$:
$$\langle d\rangle \approx \frac{\ln N}{\ln \langle k\rangle}.$$
$N(d)$
number of nodes within $d$ steps of a node
$\langle d\rangle$
average distance (shortest-path length) between node pairs
$\ln$
natural logarithm: $\ln x = y$ ⇔ $e^y = x$
$\langle k\rangle$
average degree, the growth factor per step

This is the small-world property: $10\times$ more nodes adds only $\ln 10/\ln\langle k\rangle$ steps.

World, $N = 8\times10^9$, $\langle k\rangle = 1{,}000$: $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle = 22.8/6.91 = 3.3$. Facebook 2011, $N = 7.21\times10^8$, $\langle k\rangle \approx 189$: 3.9; measured 4.74.

At $d = 4$ the random network reaches most nodes, the lattice only a small diamond. In a $D$-dimensional lattice distances grow like $N^{1/D}$, a power of $N$.

How good is the formula?

Step 1 assumes the neighbourhood is a tree. A finer formula for random networks, not in the book, is due to Fronczak, Fronczak and Hołyst (2004):

$$\langle d\rangle \approx \frac{\ln N - \gamma_E}{\ln\langle k\rangle} + \frac12.$$
$\gamma_E$
Euler's constant, $0.5772\ldots$
$+\frac12$
offset that the tree estimate misses

$N = 10^4$, $\langle k\rangle = 10$: tree estimate 4.0, Fronczak et al. 4.25. Facebook: 4.28.

Real networks (Section 3.8)

Each network vs a simulated $G(N, L)$ with the same $N$ and $L$ (the notebook tests only the collaboration network against the formula).

Email and metabolism are much closer than their random counterparts; the power grid is much farther. What could explain each?

Shorter: hubs (an email address with 6,553 contacts, a metabolite in reactions with 638 others) are shortcuts most paths use; scale-free networks can be ultra-small (Ch 4). Longer: the grid is embedded in space and long lines are expensive, so links join nearby stations; without shortcuts it behaves like a lattice.

Small worlds with clustering: Watts–Strogatz (1998)

Random networks: short distances, almost no triangles (next page). Lattices: many triangles, long distances. Real social networks have both. Watts and Strogatz: rewire each link of a ring lattice with probability $p$.

A few shortcuts suffice: each shortens many paths, so $\langle d\rangle$ falls fast with $p$, while clustering (local) barely changes. The degree distribution stays narrow: no hubs.

  • Builds on distances and BFS (Part I) and the giant component (distances are measured inside it).
  • Refined by ultra-small worlds: scale-free with $2 < \gamma < 3$ gives $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln\ln N$ (Ch 4).
  • Watts–Strogatz interpolates lattice ↔ random network; it explains short paths + clustering, not hubs.

  2. about 2 steps

  4. a factor of 100
$\ln(10^6/10^4)/\ln 10 = 2$: from about 4 to about 6.

Which is not a small world ($\langle d\rangle \sim \ln N$)?

  3. A square lattice
  4. The email network
Square lattice: $\langle d\rangle \approx \frac23\sqrt N$, a power of $N$.

Scale-free networks with $2 < \gamma < 3$ (Ch 4) have $\langle d\rangle \sim$ …

  1. $\sqrt N$
  2. $\ln N / \ln\langle k\rangle$
  3. $N$
  4. $\ln\ln N$: ultra-small
Hubs act as shortcuts for most paths, so distances grow even slower than $\ln N$.
