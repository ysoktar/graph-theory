Small worlds

Any two people on Earth are linked by a short chain of acquaintances, about six handshakes long. Random networks explain why such short chains must exist, and real networks mostly confirm it, with one instructive exception.

The idea is old. Frigyes Karinthy's 1929 short story Láncszemek ("Chains") already bets that any two people are at most five acquaintances apart. Stanley Milgram tested it in 1967: people in Nebraska and Kansas were asked to forward a letter towards a stranger in Boston, only through people they knew by first name. The chains that arrived had about five intermediaries, which became the phrase "six degrees of separation".

Why distances are short (Section 3.8)

In a random network a node has on average $\langle k\rangle$ neighbours, about $\langle k\rangle^2$ nodes at distance two, $\langle k\rangle^3$ at distance three, and so on. The number of nodes within distance $d$ is

$$N(d) \approx 1 + \langle k\rangle + \langle k\rangle^2 + \dots + \langle k\rangle^d = \frac{\langle k\rangle^{d+1} - 1}{\langle k\rangle - 1}.$$

It cannot exceed $N$. Setting $N(d_{\max}) \approx N$ gives the small-world property:

$$\langle d\rangle \approx \frac{\ln N}{\ln \langle k\rangle}.$$

The distance grows only logarithmically with the size of the network: making the network ten times larger adds a constant number of steps, $\ln 10/\ln\langle k\rangle$.

Move the slider to $d = 4$: the random network has reached most of its nodes, the lattice only a small diamond around the source. Distances in a $D$-dimensional lattice grow like $N^{1/D}$, a power of $N$, not like $\ln N$.

How good is the formula?

The estimate $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$ assumes that the neighbourhood of a node is a tree, so that no node is counted twice. Advanced Topic 3.G gives a more precise result for random networks (Fronczak, Fronczak and Hołyst, 2004), with Euler's constant $\gamma_E \approx 0.5772$:

$$\langle d\rangle \approx \frac{\ln N - \gamma_E}{\ln\langle k\rangle} + \frac12.$$

Real networks (Section 3.8)

The notebook compares a single network, the collaboration network, with the formula $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$. A fairer test compares every network with a simulated random network of the same size.

Email and metabolism have much shorter distances than their random counterparts, and the power grid has much longer ones. What feature of each network could explain this?

Short distances: these networks have hubs (one email address has 6,553 contacts, one metabolite takes part in reactions with 638 others). A hub is a shortcut that most paths can use. Chapter 4 shows that in scale-free networks distances can grow even slower than $\ln N$ (ultra-small worlds). Long distances: the power grid is embedded in space, and long power lines are expensive, so almost all links join nearby stations. Without long-range shortcuts it behaves like a lattice.

Small worlds with clustering: the Watts–Strogatz model

Random networks have short distances but almost no triangles (next page). Regular lattices have plenty of triangles but long distances. Real social networks have both. Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz (1998) showed how little it takes to combine them: rewire a small fraction of a lattice's links at random.

A small world needs only a few long-range shortcuts. Because each shortcut shortens the paths of many pairs at once, distances fall quickly as $p$ grows, while clustering, which is a local property, hardly notices. The Watts–Strogatz model explains clustering and short paths together, but its degree distribution is still narrow, close to that of a random network. It has no hubs.

A random network has $\langle k\rangle = 10$. If $N$ grows from $10^4$ to $10^6$ nodes, by roughly how much does $\langle d\rangle$ grow?

  1. It does not change
  2. By about 2 steps
  3. By a factor of 10
  4. By a factor of 100
$\Delta\langle d\rangle \approx \ln(10^6/10^4)/\ln 10 = 2$. A hundred times more nodes adds only two steps: from about 4 to about 6.

Which network is not a small world in the sense of $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln N$?

  1. A random network with $\langle k\rangle = 3$
  2. A Watts–Strogatz network with $p = 0.05$
  3. A square lattice
  4. The email network
In a square lattice $\langle d\rangle \approx \frac23\sqrt N$. The other three all have distances that grow at most logarithmically.
