Small worlds

Why are any two people on Earth only about six handshakes apart? Random networks show that such short chains are unavoidable once links are random; real networks mostly confirm it, with one instructive exception.

  • It predicts distances (Part I) from $N$ and $\langle k\rangle$ alone.
  • It explains "six degrees": distance grows like $\ln N$, not like a power of $N$.
  • A real network with shorter distances than predicted points to hubs; one with longer distances points to spatial constraints.

Karinthy's 1929 story Láncszemek ("Chains") bet on five intermediaries. Milgram (1967) tested it: people in Nebraska and Kansas forwarded a letter, via first-name acquaintances only, to one of two targets in Massachusetts (in Boston and in Sharon). Completed chains had about five intermediaries: "six degrees of separation".

Why distances are short (Section 3.8)

  1. A node has about $\langle k\rangle$ neighbours, about $\langle k\rangle^2$ nodes at distance 2, and about $\langle k\rangle^d$ at distance $d$ (assuming that no node is counted twice).
  2. So the number of nodes within distance $d$ is:
$$N(d) \approx 1 + \langle k\rangle + \langle k\rangle^2 + \dots + \langle k\rangle^d = \frac{\langle k\rangle^{d+1} - 1}{\langle k\rangle - 1}.$$
  1. $N(d)$ cannot exceed $N$. Setting $\langle k\rangle^{d} \approx N$ and taking logarithms gives $d \ln\langle k\rangle \approx \ln N$, so:
$$\langle d\rangle \approx \frac{\ln N}{\ln \langle k\rangle}.$$
$N(d)$
the number of nodes within $d$ steps of a node
$\langle d\rangle$
the average distance (shortest-path length) between pairs of nodes
$\ln$
the natural logarithm: $\ln x = y$ means $e^y = x$
$\langle k\rangle$
the average degree, which is the growth factor per step

This is the small-world property: ten times more nodes add only $\ln 10/\ln\langle k\rangle$ steps.

For the world, with $N = 8\times10^9$ and $\langle k\rangle = 1{,}000$: $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle = 22.8/6.91 = 3.3$. For Facebook in 2011, with $N = 7.21\times10^8$ and $\langle k\rangle \approx 189$, the formula gives 3.9, against a measured 4.74.

At $d = 4$ the random network reaches most nodes, while the lattice reaches only a small diamond. In a $D$-dimensional lattice, distances grow like $N^{1/D}$, a power of $N$.

How good is the formula?

Step 1 assumes that the neighbourhood of a node is a tree, with no node reached twice. A more accurate formula for random networks, not in the book, is due to Fronczak, Fronczak and Hołyst (2004):

$$\langle d\rangle \approx \frac{\ln N - \gamma_E}{\ln\langle k\rangle} + \frac12.$$
$\gamma_E$
Euler's constant, $0.5772\ldots$
$+\frac12$
an offset that the tree estimate misses

For $N = 10^4$ and $\langle k\rangle = 10$, the tree estimate is 4.0 and the Fronczak et al. formula 4.25. For Facebook it gives 4.28.

Real networks (Section 3.8)

Each network is compared with a simulated $G(N, L)$ that has the same $N$ and $L$. (The notebook tests only the collaboration network, and against the formula.)

The email and metabolic networks have much shorter distances than their random counterparts, and the power grid much longer ones. What could explain each?

Shorter: hubs (an email address with 6,553 contacts, a metabolite that reacts with 638 others) are shortcuts that most paths use, and scale-free networks can be ultra-small (Ch 4). Longer: the grid is embedded in space and long lines are expensive, so its links join nearby stations. Without shortcuts it behaves like a lattice.

Small worlds with clustering: Watts–Strogatz (1998)

Random networks have short distances but almost no triangles (next page). Lattices have many triangles but long distances. Real social networks have both short distances and many triangles. Watts and Strogatz's idea: start from a ring lattice and rewire each link to a random node with probability $p$.

A few shortcuts suffice. Each one shortens many paths, so $\langle d\rangle$ falls fast as $p$ grows, while clustering, a local property, barely changes. The degree distribution stays narrow, so the model has no hubs.

  • Builds on distances and BFS (Part I) and the giant component (distances are measured inside it).
  • Refined by ultra-small worlds: scale-free networks with $2 < \gamma < 3$ have $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln\ln N$ (Ch 4).
  • The Watts–Strogatz model interpolates between a lattice and a random network. It explains short paths together with clustering, but not hubs.

  2. By about 2 steps.
  3. By a factor of 10.
  4. By a factor of 100.
$\ln(10^6/10^4)/\ln 10 = 2$: the distance goes from about 4 to about 6.

Which of these is not a small world, that is, does not have $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln N$?

  1. A random network, $\langle k\rangle = 3$
  2. A Watts–Strogatz network, $p = 0.05$
  3. A square lattice
  4. The email network
In a square lattice $\langle d\rangle \approx \frac23\sqrt N$, a power of $N$.

Treat the world as a random network with $N = 8\times10^9$ people and $\langle k\rangle = 1{,}000$ acquaintances each. What is $\ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$?

  1. 6
  2. 3.3
  3. 1,000
  4. 8 × 10⁶
$22.8/6.91 = 3.3$. Real acquaintances overlap, so actual chains are longer.

The power grid's observed average distance is 18.8, while its random counterpart's is 8.5. Why?

  1. Its ⟨k⟩ is too low.
  2. It is too small.
  3. It has hubs.
  4. Its links join nearby stations, so it has no long-range shortcuts.
Being embedded in space makes the grid lattice-like. It is random links that make distances logarithmic.

A Watts–Strogatz network has rewiring probability $p = 0.01$. How does it compare with the original ring ($p = 0$)?

  2. Both are much smaller.
  3. Both are almost unchanged.
  4. ⟨C⟩ is larger and ⟨d⟩ unchanged.
Distances collapse long before clustering does: this is the small-world regime.

$20.40/5.24 = 3.89$. The measured value is 4.74, because friends overlap.

How does the average distance $\langle d\rangle$ of scale-free networks with $2 < \gamma < 3$ (Ch 4) grow with $N$?
