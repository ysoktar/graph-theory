Degree correlations

The degree distribution counts hubs; it does not say whom they link to. Do hubs link to hubs, or to small nodes? Networks with identical degrees can differ here, and behave differently (Chapter 7).

  • Earlier pages used only the degree sequence; correlations are the first property beyond it.
  • They reveal how a network was built: like joining like, or hubs serving many small nodes.
  • They affect robustness: assortative networks better survive hub removal (Newman 2002).

Three kinds of network

  • Assortative: hubs link to hubs.
  • Disassortative: hubs link to small nodes.
  • Neutral: random, given the degrees.

The degree correlation function

$$k_{nn}(k) = \sum_{k'} k'\, P(k' \mid k).$$
$k_{nn}(k)$
mean neighbour degree of degree-$k$ nodes
$P(k' \mid k)$
probability a neighbour of a degree-$k$ node has degree $k'$

Neutral network: a link reaches a degree-$k'$ node with probability $\propto k' p_{k'}$, independent of $k$, so

$$k_{nn}(k) = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}.$$
$\langle k\rangle, \langle k^2\rangle$
mean degree, mean squared degree

Rising $k_{nn}$: assortative; falling: disassortative; flat: neutral. Fits $k_{nn} \approx a k^{\mu}$ give the sign as $\mu$.

Collaboration: $178.2/8.08 = 22.1$. A scientist has 8 co-authors; a co-author has 22. This is the friendship paradox, used by acquaintance vaccination.

The assortativity coefficient $r$

$r$ is the Pearson correlation of the degrees at the two ends of each link (Newman 2002), in $[-1, 1]$: positive assortative, negative disassortative, zero neutral.

Star with four leaves: every link joins (4, 1), so $r = -1$. Disjoint triangles: all degrees 2, $r$ undefined.

The book's networks

  • Social networks are assortative: collaboration $r = 0.13$, phone calls $0.21$.
  • Metabolism ($-0.25$), proteins ($-0.08$), email and WWW are disassortative; power grid and Internet near neutral.
  • Randomized, collaboration becomes neutral but metabolism stays at $-0.17$: that part is structural.

Structural disassortativity (Section 7.4)

Random wiring expects this many links between nodes of degrees $k$ and $k'$:

$$\frac{k\,k'}{\langle k\rangle N}.$$
$\langle k\rangle N$
total link ends, $2L$

Above 1, hubs "should" share several links; a simple network allows one, so the excess goes to small nodes. Setting it to 1 for $k = k'$ gives the structural cutoff

$$k_s \approx \sqrt{\langle k\rangle N}.$$
$k_s$
degree above which hubs cannot be linked as often as chance requires

Metabolism: $k_s = \sqrt{9.13 \times 1{,}039} \approx 97$. Top hubs, 638 and 460 links: expected $638 \times 460/9{,}486 \approx 31$ links between them; at most 1 allowed.

Only correlations that survive degree-preserving randomization, like collaboration's, say how a network was built.

  • Null model: degree-preserving randomization keeps degrees, erases correlations.
  • Same ratio $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$: neutral $k_{nn}$, Molloy–Reed $\kappa$ (Robustness), epidemic threshold (Spreading).
  • Models: random, configuration and large BA networks are neutral ($r \to 0$); assortativity needs extra ingredients such as communities.

Why are social networks assortative and technological or biological ones disassortative?

Homophily and group membership link similar people. Technological hubs (routers, airports, central metabolites) exist to serve many small nodes, and the structural cutoff adds disassortativity.

$r$ of a star with ten leaves?

  1. $0$
  2. $+1$
  3. $-1$
  4. Undefined
Every link joins 10 to 1: perfectly negative correlation.

Collaboration has $r = 0.13$; randomized, $r \approx 0$. This shows

  1. The assortativity comes from how collaborators choose each other
  2. Randomization changed $p_k$
  3. It is structural
  4. The network is neutral
What randomization destroys is not implied by the degrees. Metabolism's survives ($-0.25 \to -0.17$): largely structural.

$N = 10{,}000$, $\langle k\rangle = 4$, two hubs of 1,000 links. Expected links between them?

  1. At most 1
  2. 100
  3. 12.5
  4. 25
$10^6/(4 \times 10^4) = 25$; both hubs exceed $k_s = 200$.

  1. 3
  2. 10
  3. 30
  4. 1
$\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 10$: the friendship paradox.

$N = 2{,}500$, $\langle k\rangle = 4$. Structural cutoff $k_s$?

  1. 50
  2. 2,500
  3. 10,000
  4. 100
$k_s = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle N} = \sqrt{10{,}000} = 100$.

"Your friends have more friends than you" holds

  1. in every network with unequal degrees
  2. only in assortative networks
  3. only in disassortative networks
  4. only in scale-free networks
A link end lands on a node with probability $\propto k$, so neighbours average $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle \ge \langle k\rangle$, equal only if all degrees are equal.
