Degree correlations

The degree distribution counts hubs; it does not say whom they link to. Do hubs link to hubs, or to small nodes? Networks with identical degrees can differ here, and behave differently (Chapter 7).

  • Earlier pages used only the degree sequence; correlations are the first property beyond it.
  • They reveal how a network was built: like joining like, or hubs serving many small nodes.
  • They change how the network works: when the giant component appears, how far apart nodes are, how robust it is, how diseases persist.

Do hubs date hubs? (Section 7.1)

Celebrities marry celebrities, yet a celebrity choosing among $10^8$ people at random would pick one of 1,000 other celebrities with probability $10^{-5}$. In the yeast protein network the opposite happens: hubs avoid each other. To tell which is unusual we need the chance expectation. If every node links at random, two nodes with degrees $k$ and $k'$ are linked with probability

$$p_{k,k'} = \frac{k\,k'}{2L}. \qquad (7.1)$$
$k, k'$
degrees of the two nodes
$2L$
total number of link ends (stubs)

Worked example 1: hubs that avoid each other. An early yeast protein map has $N = 1{,}870$ proteins and $L = 2{,}277$ links; its two largest hubs have $k = 56$ and $k' = 13$.

  1. Chance link between the hubs: $p_{56,13} = \frac{56 \times 13}{2 \times 2277} = \frac{728}{4554} = 0.16$.
  2. Between a degree-2 and a degree-1 protein: $p_{1,2} = \frac{2}{4554} = 0.0004$, 400 times smaller.
  3. Yet the hubs are not linked, and the $k = 56$ hub has 46 degree-1 neighbours where chance expects about 12: four times too many. The network is disassortative.

Three kinds of network (Section 7.2)

  • Assortative: hubs link to hubs, small nodes to small nodes. Extreme case: every node links only to nodes of its own degree (a set of cliques).
  • Disassortative: hubs link to small nodes: a hub-and-spoke pattern.
  • Neutral: links follow (7.1): random, given the degrees.

The degree correlation matrix

The complete description is $e_{jk}$, the probability that a randomly chosen link has degrees $j$ and $k$ at its two ends. Three facts tie it to the degree distribution:

$$\sum_{j,k} e_{jk} = 1 \quad (7.2), \qquad q_k = \frac{k\,p_k}{\langle k\rangle} \quad (7.3), \qquad \sum_j e_{jk} = q_k \quad (7.4).$$
$q_k$
probability that the end of a random link is a degree-$k$ node (the excess view: hubs are reached $k$ times as often)

A neutral network has no preference, so the two ends are independent:

$$e_{jk} = q_j\, q_k. \qquad (7.5)$$

Assortative networks put extra weight on the diagonal $j \approx k$; disassortative ones on the anti-diagonal. Perfect assortativity is $e_{jk} = \delta_{jk} q_k$.

Worked example 2: everything for a star. A hub linked to three leaves: degrees 3, 1, 1, 1; $L = 3$; $\langle k\rangle = 6/4 = 1.5$.

  1. Every link joins a 3 and a 1. Counting each link once in each direction: $e_{31} = e_{13} = \frac12$, $e_{33} = e_{11} = 0$.
  2. $q_3 = \frac{3 \times 1/4}{1.5} = \frac12$ and $q_1 = \frac{1 \times 3/4}{1.5} = \frac12$. Check (7.4): $e_{31} + e_{33} = \frac12 = q_3$.
  3. Neutral would be $q_3 q_1 = \frac14$ for every cell. The star has twice that on the anti-diagonal and nothing on the diagonal: disassortative.
  4. $k_{nn}$: the hub's neighbours have degree 1, the leaves' neighbour has degree 3. It falls with $k$.
  5. $r$ (below): $\sum jk(e_{jk} - q_jq_k) = 2 \times 3(\frac12 - \frac14) - 9 \times \frac14 - 1 \times \frac14 = -1$, and $\sigma^2 = (9 + 1)\frac12 - (1.5 + 0.5)^2 = 1$, so $r = -1$.

The matrix holds about $k_{\max}^2/2$ numbers: complete but hard to read or compare. Section 7.3 compresses it.

The degree correlation function (Section 7.3)

For each node, average its neighbours' degrees; then average over all nodes of degree $k$:

$$k_{nn}(k_i) = \frac{1}{k_i}\sum_{j} A_{ij}\, k_j \quad (7.6), \qquad k_{nn}(k) = \sum_{k'} k'\, P(k' \mid k) \quad (7.7).$$
$k_{nn}(k)$
mean neighbour degree of degree-$k$ nodes
$P(k' \mid k)$
probability that a neighbour of a degree-$k$ node has degree $k'$

For a neutral network, (7.4) and (7.5) give $P(k' \mid k) = e_{kk'}/q_k = q_{k'}$ (7.8): the neighbour does not care about $k$. Then

$$k_{nn}(k) = \sum_{k'} k'\,\frac{k' p_{k'}}{\langle k\rangle} = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}, \qquad (7.9)$$

a horizontal line. Rising $k_{nn}$: assortative; falling: disassortative. The book fits

$$k_{nn}(k) = a\,k^{\mu} \qquad (7.10)$$

and reads the type from the sign of the correlation exponent $\mu$: collaboration $\mu = 0.37 \pm 0.11$ (assortative), power grid $0.04 \pm 0.05$ (neutral), metabolism $-0.76 \pm 0.04$ (disassortative).

Worked example 3: the friendship paradox (Box 7.1). On average your friends have more friends than you do.

  1. Random network: $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1 + \langle k\rangle)$, so $k_{nn} = 1 + \langle k\rangle$: always one more than the average node.
  2. Collaboration: $178.2/8.08 = 22.1$. A scientist has 8 co-authors on average; a co-author has 22.
  3. Actors: $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 565$. The reason is (7.3): you are $k$ times more likely to know a hub than a degree-1 node. Acquaintance vaccination uses exactly this.

The degree correlation coefficient $r$ (Box 7.2)

Newman's single number is the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link:

$$r = \sum_{jk} \frac{jk\,(e_{jk} - q_j q_k)}{\sigma^2} \quad (7.11), \qquad \sigma^2 = \sum_k k^2 q_k - \Big[\sum_k k\, q_k\Big]^2 \quad (7.12).$$

$-1 \le r \le 1$: positive assortative, zero neutral, negative disassortative. Collaboration $r = 0.13$, proteins $r = -0.04$, power grid $r = 0$. $r$ assumes $k_{nn}(k)$ is linear in $k$, $\mu$ that it is a power law; both cannot hold exactly, but they usually agree in sign.

Structural cutoffs (Section 7.4)

All the book's networks are simple: at most one link between two nodes. That clashes with (7.1) for the largest hubs. The expected number of links between degrees $k$ and $k'$ is

$$E_{kk'} = e_{kk'}\,\langle k\rangle N \quad (7.13) \qquad\xrightarrow{\text{neutral}}\qquad E_{kk'} = \frac{k\,k'}{\langle k\rangle N}. \quad (7.14)$$

Worked example 4: two hubs that need three links. A scale-free network with $N = 300$, $L = 450$ ($\langle k\rangle = 3$) has hubs of degree 55 and 46.

  1. $E = \frac{55 \times 46}{3 \times 300} = \frac{2530}{900} = 2.8$: neutral wiring needs about three links between them.
  2. A simple network allows one. The missing links must go elsewhere, that is, to smaller nodes.
  3. Stub picture: nodes with 8 and 7 stubs among 28 stubs in total expect $8 \times 7/28 = 2$ links. Forbid the second, and the network becomes disassortative without anyone asking for it: structural disassortativity.

The conflict starts where $E_{kk} = 1$, at the structural cutoff

$$k_s(N) \sim (\langle k\rangle N)^{1/2}. \qquad (7.15)$$

Compare it with the largest degree, the natural cutoff $k_{\max} \sim k_{\min} N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$:

  • Random networks and $\gamma \ge 3$: $1/(\gamma - 1) \le 1/2$, so $k_{\max}$ stays below $k_s$. No conflict.
  • $2 < \gamma < 3$: $1/(\gamma - 1) > 1/2$, so for large $N$ the biggest hubs exceed $k_s$. Simple scale-free networks are structurally disassortative.

Worked example 5: when does it bite? $\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 2$, so $\langle k\rangle \approx \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}k_{\min} = 6$.

  1. $N = 10^4$: $k_s = \sqrt{6 \times 10^4} = 245$, $k_{\max} = 2 \times 10^{4/1.5} = 928$.
  2. $N = 10^6$: $k_s = 2{,}449$, $k_{\max} = 2 \times 10^{4} = 20{,}000$. The range of conflicted hubs widens with $N$.
  3. Metabolism: $k_s = \sqrt{9.13 \times 1{,}039} \approx 97$. Its top hubs have 638 and 460 links: $638 \times 460/9{,}482 \approx 31$ expected links between them, at most 1 allowed.

Two ways out: allow multi-links (the network becomes neutral), or remove hubs above $k_s$. To decide whether a real network's correlations are structural, randomize it while keeping its degrees:

  • R-S (randomization keeping the network simple): if $k_{nn}^{R\text{-}S}(k)$ matches the real $k_{nn}(k)$, the correlations are all structural, explained by the degrees.
  • R-M (multi-links allowed): always neutral; a consistency check.

Structural effects can only produce disassortativity. Assortativity always needs a real cause.

Correlations in real networks (Section 7.5)

  • Power grid: the only truly neutral network; flat and equal to its randomized version.
  • Social networks (phone calls, collaboration, actors) are assortative, and randomization erases it: a real social mechanism. Collaboration $r = 0.13$, phone calls $0.21$.
  • Email, proteins, metabolism are disassortative, but their R-S versions decay too: structural. Metabolism stays at $r = -0.17$ after randomization.
  • WWW: disassortative, only partly explained by its degrees. Internet: assortative up to $k \approx 30$, then flattened by the cutoff. Citations: assortative for $k \le 20$, disassortative above.

Why are social networks assortative? People form communities (Chapter 9), society brings hubs together (committees, boards, TV shows), and homophily makes similar people, including similarly connected ones, associate. Directed networks need four functions, $k_{nn}^{\text{in,in}}$, $k_{nn}^{\text{in,out}}$, $k_{nn}^{\text{out,in}}$, $k_{nn}^{\text{out,out}}$ (Box 7.3).

Tuning correlations: the Xalvi-Brunet–Sokolov algorithm

  1. Link selection. Pick two links at random. Label their four end nodes so that $k_a \ge k_b \ge k_c \ge k_d$.
  2. Rewiring. With probability $p$: for assortativity link $a$–$b$ and $c$–$d$ (similar degrees together); for disassortativity link $a$–$d$ and $b$–$c$ (big with small). Otherwise pair them at random.
  3. Simplicity. Reject a rewiring that would create a multi-link, and repeat.

Every node keeps its degree. $p = 1$ drives the network to the strongest correlations its degrees allow; smaller $p$ tunes the strength. Assortativity is harder than disassortativity: in a simple scale-free network the hubs run out of hub partners above $k_s$, so a maximally assortative network has $k_{nn}$ rising for small $k$ and falling for large $k$, like the citation network.

Why correlations matter (Section 7.7)

  • Giant component: assortative networks form it at a smaller $\langle k\rangle$, because the hubs find each other; at larger $\langle k\rangle$ their giant component is smaller, because the hubs are busy with each other and fail to pull in the many small nodes. Disassortative networks do the opposite.
  • Distances: assortativity shortens the average path a little but stretches the diameter: small nodes link to small nodes and form long chains. Disassortative networks have the smallest diameter.
  • Epidemics: an assortative hub core is a reservoir that keeps a disease alive even when the network as a whole is too sparse (Chapter 10).
  • Robustness: removing hubs does less damage in assortative networks, where the hubs back each other up, and more in disassortative ones, where each hub holds its own spokes (Chapter 8).
  • Correlations also change synchronization, the minimum vertex cover (Box 7.4) and how many inputs are needed to control a network.

Correlations are two-point information: who is linked to whom by degree. Clustering by degree, $C(k)$, is three-point information, and in principle there is no end to the hierarchy (Box 7.6).

  • Null model: degree-preserving randomization keeps degrees, erases correlations.
  • Same ratio $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$: neutral $k_{nn}$, Molloy–Reed $\kappa$ (Robustness), epidemic threshold (Spreading).
  • Models: random, configuration and large BA networks are neutral ($r \to 0$); assortativity needs extra ingredients such as communities.

Why are social networks assortative and technological or biological ones disassortative?

Homophily and group membership link similar people. Technological hubs (routers, airports, central metabolites) exist to serve many small nodes, and the structural cutoff adds disassortativity.
