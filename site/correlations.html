Degree correlations

Do hubs link to hubs, or to small nodes? Two networks with exactly the same degree sequence can answer this question in opposite ways (Chapter 7).

Measuring correlations

The degree correlation function is the average degree of the neighbours of the nodes with degree $k$:

$$k_{nn}(k) = \sum_{k'} k'\, P(k' \mid k).$$

If links ignore degrees (a neutral network), a link ends at a node of degree $k'$ with probability $k' p_{k'}/\langle k\rangle$, and $k_{nn}(k) = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ for every $k$. In an assortative network $k_{nn}(k)$ increases with $k$ (hubs link to hubs); in a disassortative network it decreases (hubs link to small nodes). The book often fits $k_{nn}(k) \approx a k^{\mu}$, with $\mu > 0$ assortative and $\mu < 0$ disassortative.

A single number summarises the same information: the assortativity coefficient $r$ (Newman 2002), the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of a link, $-1 \le r \le 1$.

The book's networks

  • The social networks are assortative: collaboration ($r = 0.13$) and phone calls ($r = 0.21$). Well-connected scientists write with well-connected scientists.
  • The biological networks are disassortative: metabolism ($r = -0.25$) and protein interactions ($-0.08$), as are email and the WWW sample. The power grid and the Internet are close to neutral.
  • Wiring the same degrees at random leaves the social networks neutral but metabolism still strongly disassortative ($-0.17$). That part is structural, explained next.

Structural disassortativity (Section 7.4)

In a simple network two hubs can share at most one link. When the expected number of links between the largest hubs, $k k'/(\langle k\rangle N)$, exceeds 1, the configuration model would need repeated links between them; forbidding those pushes hubs to link to small nodes instead. This happens above the structural cutoff

$$k_s \approx \sqrt{\langle k\rangle N}.$$

Metabolism ($k_{\max} = 638$ against $k_s \approx 97$), email and the WWW all have hubs far above it, so part of their disassortativity is forced by the degree sequence. Only correlations that survive degree-preserving randomization, like those of the collaboration network, say something about how the network was built.

Why might social networks be assortative while technological and biological ones are disassortative?

Social ties form by homophily: people link to similar people, including people of similar popularity, and groups (co-author teams, clubs) link everyone in them to each other. In technological networks hubs are expensive, specialised nodes (routers, hub airports, central metabolites) whose job is to serve many small nodes, and the structural cutoff adds disassortativity whenever hubs are very large.

A flat $k_{nn}(k)$ at the neutral value means the degree at one end of a link says nothing about the degree at the other end: no correlation, $r \approx 0$.
