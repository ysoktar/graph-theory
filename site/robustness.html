Robustness & attacks

Routers fail, proteins are knocked out, power stations go offline. How many nodes can a network lose before it falls apart? The answer depends on which nodes are lost, and scale-free networks give two opposite answers (Chapter 8).

Percolation and the Molloy–Reed criterion

Removing a random fraction $f$ of the nodes is a percolation problem: above a critical fraction $f_c$ the giant component disappears. A randomly wired network has a giant component when a node reached by following a link has, on average, at least one further link to follow:

$$\kappa = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} > 2 \qquad \text{(Molloy–Reed criterion)}.$$

Removing nodes at random lowers $\kappa$, and the giant component vanishes at

$$f_c = 1 - \frac{1}{\kappa - 1}.$$

For a random network $\kappa = \langle k\rangle + 1$ and $f_c = 1 - 1/\langle k\rangle$. For a scale-free network with $\gamma < 3$, $\langle k^2\rangle$ diverges with $N$, so $f_c \to 1$: an infinite scale-free network cannot be broken by random failures.

The book's networks

  • Under random failures, heavy-tailed networks shrink slowly and keep a giant component far beyond the random network's threshold.
  • Under attack the same networks collapse after a few per cent of their nodes are removed, far earlier than a random network of the same size. Their robustness and their fragility have the same cause: the hubs.
  • The power grid has no hubs: random failures and attacks do similar damage, and it is fragile to both.

Scale-free networks are robust yet fragile: robust to random failures, because a random node is almost never a hub, and fragile to targeted attacks, because removing the few hubs disconnects everything they held together. The same $\langle k^2\rangle$ that makes them robust appears in the epidemic threshold (Spreading).

A network operator can add a few links but not remove hubs. How would you make a scale-free network more resistant to attacks without making it much more fragile to random failures?

Section 8.7 discusses optimal designs: networks with a bimodal degree distribution (a few hubs plus many nodes of equal degree) maximise the combined threshold for failures and attacks. In practice, adding links between medium-degree nodes creates alternative paths that do not run through the hubs, and protecting the hubs themselves is cheaper than protecting everything.

A random network has $\langle k\rangle = 4$. What fraction of randomly chosen nodes must be removed to destroy its giant component?

  1. 25%
  2. 50%
  3. 75%
  4. 100%
$f_c = 1 - 1/\langle k\rangle = 1 - 1/4 = 75\%$. The remaining network then has $\langle k\rangle = 1$, the critical point of the random network.
