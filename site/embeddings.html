Node embeddings and graph neural networks

Hand-made features must be designed for every task. A node embedding learns a vector for every node instead, so that nodes similar in the network are close in space; graph neural networks learn it for the task at hand.

  • One framework covers every method: an encoder, a decoder and a choice of what "similar" means.
  • Random-walk embeddings are word2vec on walks; spectral embeddings are eigenvectors of the Laplacian.
  • Graph neural networks fix the limits of shallow embeddings: they share weights, use node attributes and handle new nodes.

Node embeddings: learning the features

Hand-made features must be designed for each task. A node embedding learns a vector $\mathbf z_v \in \mathbb R^d$ for every node, with $d$ small (2 to a few hundred), so that similar nodes get similar vectors. Every method has the same three parts:

  1. Encoder $\text{ENC}(v) = \mathbf z_v$. For "shallow" embeddings it is a lookup in a learned table: $\mathbf z_v = Z\mathbf e_v$, with one column of $Z$ per node.
  2. Decoder $\text{DEC}(\mathbf z_u, \mathbf z_v)$, usually the dot product $\mathbf z_u^{T}\mathbf z_v$.
  3. A notion of similarity in the network, which the decoder should reproduce: $\mathbf z_u^{T}\mathbf z_v \approx \text{similarity}(u, v)$. The choice of similarity is what distinguishes the methods.

Spectral embedding: similarity = being linked

Place linked nodes close: minimise $\sum_{(u,v)} \lVert\mathbf z_u - \mathbf z_v\rVert^2$ over the links, with a constraint that stops all vectors collapsing to one point. The solution is given by eigenvectors of the normalised Laplacian with the smallest eigenvalues (Laplacian eigenmaps):

$$\mathcal L = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}.$$
$A$
adjacency matrix (Ch 2)
$D$
diagonal matrix of degrees
$I$
identity matrix

The first eigenvector is trivial (constant after rescaling). The second, the Fiedler vector, is the best two-group cut relaxed to real values: its sign splits the network (spectral bisection). Further eigenvectors add coordinates.

Random-walk embeddings: similarity = appearing on the same walks

DeepWalk (Perozzi, Al-Rfou and Skiena 2014) and node2vec (Grover and Leskovec 2016) run short random walks from every node and call $v$ a context of $u$ if it appears within a window of $u$ on a walk. Then they maximise the probability of the observed contexts:

$$\max_Z \sum_u \sum_{v \in N_R(u)} \log P(v \mid \mathbf z_u), \qquad P(v \mid \mathbf z_u) = \frac{e^{\mathbf z_u^{T}\mathbf z_v}}{\sum_{n} e^{\mathbf z_u^{T}\mathbf z_n}}.$$
$N_R(u)$
the contexts of $u$ collected from the walks (with repetitions)
softmax
turns dot products into probabilities; its denominator sums over all $N$ nodes

The denominator costs $O(N)$ for every pair, too slow. Negative sampling replaces it: reward the true context and penalise $k$ random nodes (typically 5 to 20, drawn more often for high degree):

$$\log \sigma(\mathbf z_u^{T}\mathbf z_v) + \sum_{i=1}^{k}\log \sigma(-\mathbf z_u^{T}\mathbf z_{n_i}), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}.$$

This is word2vec with walks as sentences and nodes as words.

The club split between Mr. Hi (node 0) and the officer (node 33). Both embeddings recover it from the links alone: one coordinate's sign places nearly everyone; the exceptions sit between the groups in the network too.

node2vec's biased walk. A walk that just moved from $t$ to $v$ picks the next node $x$ with weight

$$\alpha_{pq}(t, x) = \begin{cases} 1/p & d_{tx} = 0 \ (\text{back to } t) \\ 1 & d_{tx} = 1 \ (\text{a common neighbour of } t \text{ and } v) \\ 1/q & d_{tx} = 2 \ (\text{further away}) \end{cases}$$

Small $q$ pushes the walk outward (depth-first): embeddings capture communities. Large $q$ keeps it local (breadth-first): embeddings capture structural roles, such as "a hub" or "a bridge", wherever they are. Small $p$ makes it backtrack. $p = q = 1$ is DeepWalk.

Worked example 5: one node2vec step. The walk came from $t$ to $v$. $v$'s neighbours are $t$, $x_1$ (also linked to $t$) and $x_2$, $x_3$ (not linked to $t$). Take $p = 2$, $q = 0.5$.

  1. Weights: $t$: $1/p = 0.5$; $x_1$: 1; $x_2$, $x_3$: $1/q = 2$ each. Total 5.5.
  2. Probabilities: back to $t$ 9%, to $x_1$ 18%, to $x_2$ or $x_3$ 36% each. The walk heads away from where it came: depth-first.

Using embeddings, and their limits

  • Node classification: train a classifier on $\mathbf z_v$ for the labelled nodes, predict the rest.
  • Link prediction: score a pair by $\mathbf z_u^{T}\mathbf z_v$, or train a classifier on $\mathbf z_u \odot \mathbf z_v$ (element-wise product) or on the two vectors concatenated.
  • Graph classification: sum or average the node vectors.

Shallow embeddings have three limits: one learned vector per node ($O(Nd)$ parameters, nothing shared between nodes); they are transductive (a new node has no vector until everything is retrained); and they ignore node attributes. Graph neural networks fix all three.

Graph neural networks

A GNN layer passes messages: each node combines its vector with its neighbours' (graph convolutional network, Kipf and Welling 2017):

$$h_v^{(l+1)} = \sigma\Big(W^{(l)} \cdot \operatorname{mean}\{h_u^{(l)} : u \in \{v\} \cup N(v)\}\Big).$$
$h_v^{(l)}$
vector of node $v$ after layer $l$; $h_v^{(0)}$ are the node's attributes
$N(v)$
neighbours of $v$
$W^{(l)}$
learned weight matrix, shared by all nodes
$\sigma$
non-linearity (e.g. ReLU)
  • Shared weights: the same $W^{(l)}$ for every node, so the model is inductive: it computes a vector for a node it never saw.
  • Depth = reach: after $l$ layers a node sees nodes up to distance $l$. With the small-world distances of Ch 3, a few layers already cover most of the network.
  • Over-smoothing: many layers average everything into the same vector (the figure below).
  • Expressiveness: a message-passing GNN cannot distinguish two graphs that the WL test cannot; the GIN architecture matches WL exactly (Xu et al. 2019).

Common-neighbour scores assume friends of friends become friends. Where should that fail?

It suits clustered social networks (collaboration, phone calls). Proteins sharing a partner often compete for the same binding site and do not bind each other; Kovács et al. (Nat. Commun. 2019) count paths of length three instead. The power grid has almost no triangles.
