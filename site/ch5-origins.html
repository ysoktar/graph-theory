Where preferential attachment comes from

A new web page does not know every page's degree, yet links behave as if it did. Section 5.9 shows how preferential attachment arises from simple local rules; Section 5.10 checks the BA model's distances and clustering against real networks.

  • Preferential attachment is not a rule nodes follow; it emerges from random or local choices.
  • The model's distances match real networks, its clustering does not.
  • Knowing what the model gets right and wrong is what Chapter 6 builds on.

Where does preferential attachment come from? (Section 5.9)

Nobody computes $\Pi(k)$ before linking. Two kinds of mechanism produce it anyway.

  • Link selection (local, random): a new node picks a random link and attaches to one of its two ends. The end of a random link is a degree-$k$ node with probability $$q_k = \frac{k\,p_k}{\langle k\rangle},$$ linear in $k$: preferential attachment without knowing any degree.
  • Copying (local, random): a new web page picks a random page $u$; with probability $p$ it links to $u$, otherwise it copies one of $u$'s links. A node is reached with probability $$\Pi(k) = \frac{p}{N} + (1-p)\frac{k}{2L},$$ again linear. Friends of friends, copied references and duplicated genes work this way.
  • Optimization (global, rational): a new node at a random point of a unit square links to the node $j$ that minimises a cost $C_j = \delta\,d_{ij} + h_j$, trading the distance $d_{ij}$ against the network distance $h_j$ of $j$ from a central node. Small $\delta$ gives a star, large $\delta$ ($\ge \sqrt N$) a network without hubs, and in between ($4 \le \delta \le \sqrt N$) a scale-free network: each node attracts the newcomers that land in its basin, and bigger nodes have bigger basins.

Luck or reason: in the 1960s Herbert Simon (random, preferential growth) and Benoit Mandelbrot (optimization) fought over the origin of power laws in word frequencies. Both mechanisms turn out to produce linear preferential attachment.

Distances and clustering (Section 5.10)

$$d_{\max} \sim \frac{\ln N}{\ln\ln N}\ (m > 1), \qquad \langle C\rangle \sim \frac{(\ln N)^2}{N}.$$
$d_{\max}$
diameter; the average distance scales the same way, slower than the random network's $\ln N$; for $m = 1$ the network is a tree and distances grow like $\ln N$
$\langle C\rangle$
average clustering; above the random $\langle k\rangle/N$ but still vanishing for large $N$

The BA model's lesson is that structure and evolution are inseparable. Random, small-world and configuration models are like a photograph of a painting: they reproduce what a network looks like. The BA model repaints it with the original brush strokes: to explain a network's topology, model how it came to be.

  • The BA model explains the power law of Chapter 4. Its exponent $\gamma = 3$ lies on the border of the scale-free regime, where $\langle k^2\rangle$ grows only like $\ln N$.
  • It generates the degree sequence that the configuration model takes as input; its old nodes are also linked to each other in a non-random way.
  • The fitness model generalizes it: nodes grow at different rates, so latecomers can win.

The BA model always gives $\gamma = 3$, yet the book's networks have exponents from 1.9 to 4.8 and much higher clustering. What is the model missing?

Possible ingredients are fitness (Ch 6), links that are added or removed between existing nodes, nonlinear attachment $\Pi \sim k^\alpha$ (with $\alpha \ne 1$ the power law breaks down) and ageing. The clustering of the BA model vanishes as $(\ln N)^2/N$.

In the link selection model a new node picks a random link and attaches to one of its ends. The end of a random link is a degree-$k$ node with probability $q_k = k\,p_k/\langle k\rangle$. Why does this model produce a scale-free network?

  1. The new node chooses the node with the most links
  2. Because the network grows
  3. It minimises the distance to the centre
  4. Hubs sit at the end of many links, so a random link reaches a node with probability proportional to its degree: preferential attachment without any rule
$q_k \propto k$ is linear preferential attachment in disguise. Together with growth it reproduces the BA model and $\gamma = 3$.

The BA model gives $\langle C\rangle \sim (\ln N)^2/N$. What happens to its clustering when the network grows from $10^4$ to $10^6$ nodes, and what does that say about the model?

  1. It stays about the same, like real networks
  2. It falls about 40-fold, from $8.5\times10^{-3}$ to $1.9\times10^{-4}$, while real networks keep $\langle C\rangle$ roughly constant: the model does not explain clustering
  3. It grows, because $(\ln N)^2$ grows
  4. It falls exactly like a random network, $\propto 1/N$
$(\ln 10^4)^2/10^4 = 84.8/10^4$ and $(\ln 10^6)^2/10^6 = 191/10^6$. BA reproduces hubs and small distances but not the clustering of real networks.
