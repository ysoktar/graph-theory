Correlated networks: models and impact

Which models produce correlations, and how can we build a network with the correlations we want? Section 7.6 answers both; Section 7.7 asks why it matters, and finds that correlations move the giant-component transition itself.

  • Every model of the course has a known correlation pattern; most are neutral or structurally disassortative.
  • The Xalvi-Brunet–Sokolov algorithm tunes correlations while keeping every degree.
  • Assortative and disassortative networks with identical degrees connect, spread and break differently.

Tuning correlations: the Xalvi-Brunet–Sokolov algorithm

  1. Link selection. Pick two links at random. Label their four end nodes so that $k_a \ge k_b \ge k_c \ge k_d$.
  2. Rewiring. With probability $p$: for assortativity link $a$–$b$ and $c$–$d$ (similar degrees together); for disassortativity link $a$–$d$ and $b$–$c$ (big with small). Otherwise pair them at random.
  3. Simplicity. Reject a rewiring that would create a multi-link, and repeat.

Every node keeps its degree. $p = 1$ drives the network to the strongest correlations its degrees allow; smaller $p$ tunes the strength. Assortativity is harder than disassortativity: in a simple scale-free network the hubs run out of hub partners above $k_s$, so a maximally assortative network has $k_{nn}$ rising for small $k$ and falling for large $k$, like the citation network.

Why correlations matter (Section 7.7)

  • Giant component: assortative networks form it at a smaller $\langle k\rangle$, because the hubs find each other; at larger $\langle k\rangle$ their giant component is smaller, because the hubs are busy with each other and fail to pull in the many small nodes. Disassortative networks do the opposite.
  • Distances: assortativity shortens the average path a little but stretches the diameter: small nodes link to small nodes and form long chains. Disassortative networks have the smallest diameter.
  • Epidemics: an assortative hub core is a reservoir that keeps a disease alive even when the network as a whole is too sparse (Chapter 10).
  • Robustness: removing hubs does less damage in assortative networks, where the hubs back each other up, and more in disassortative ones, where each hub holds its own spokes (Chapter 8).
  • Correlations also change synchronization, the minimum vertex cover (Box 7.4) and how many inputs are needed to control a network.

Correlations are two-point information: who is linked to whom by degree. Clustering by degree, $C(k)$, is three-point information, and in principle there is no end to the hierarchy (Box 7.6).

  • Null model: degree-preserving randomization keeps degrees, erases correlations.
  • Same ratio $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$: neutral $k_{nn}$, Molloy–Reed $\kappa$ (Robustness), epidemic threshold (Spreading).
  • Models: random, configuration and large BA networks are neutral ($r \to 0$); assortativity needs extra ingredients such as communities.

Why are social networks assortative and technological or biological ones disassortative?

Homophily and group membership link similar people. Technological hubs (routers, airports, central metabolites) exist to serve many small nodes, and the structural cutoff adds disassortativity.
