Evolving networks: fitness

In the BA model the oldest node is always the richest. Yet latecomers win: Google arrived after AltaVista and overtook it. The missing ingredient: some nodes are more attractive than others.

  • BA ties success to age alone, so it predicts the oldest pages are most linked and $\gamma = 3$ always. Both are false.
  • Fitness adds one number per node, keeping growth and preferential attachment.
  • It explains late winners, exponents other than 3, and winner-takes-all networks.

The Bianconi–Barabási model (Section 6.2)

Each node gets a fixed fitness $\eta_i$ at birth, drawn from $\rho(\eta)$. A new link attaches to node $i$ with probability

$$\Pi_i = \frac{\eta_i k_i}{\sum_j \eta_j k_j}.$$
$\eta_i$
fitness ("eta") of node $i$: intrinsic attractiveness, here in [0, 1]
$k_i$
degree of node $i$
$\sum_j \eta_j k_j$
total attractiveness (normalisation)
$\rho(\eta)$
fitness distribution (here uniform)

Equal fitnesses cancel and give back the BA model.

Each node grows at its own speed

The BA continuum calculation (previous page) now gives

$$k_i(t) = m\left(\frac{t}{t_i}\right)^{\beta(\eta_i)}, \qquad \beta(\eta) = \frac{\eta}{C},$$

with $C$ fixed by requiring the total degree to grow like $2mt$ (Eq. 6.7):

$$\int \rho(\eta)\,\frac{\eta}{C - \eta}\,d\eta = 1 .$$
$t_i$
arrival time of node $i$
$\beta(\eta)$
dynamical exponent of fitness $\eta$ (BA: ½)
$C$
constant set by $\rho(\eta)$
  • Uniform fitness: $C \ln\frac{C}{C-1} = 2$ → $C = 1.255$; $\beta(1) = 0.80$, $\beta(0.3) = 0.24$.
  • Equal fitness $\eta = 1$: $\frac{1}{C-1} = 1$ → $C = 2$, $\beta = \frac12$ (BA).
  • A: $t_A = 10$, $\eta = 0.3$; B: $t_B = 1{,}000$, $\eta = 1$. Equal when $0.239\ln(t/10) = 0.797\ln(t/1000)$ → $t \approx 7{,}200$; then B leads.

The degree distribution

A mixture of power laws, one per fitness. For uniform fitness:

$$p_k \sim \frac{k^{-(1+C)}}{\ln k} .$$
$1 + C$
degree exponent: 2.255 here, below BA's 3
$\ln k$
slow correction; fitted exponents come out higher

A hub is either early (BA) or fit. The fitness distribution sets the exponent; growth rates measured from data estimate fitness (Section 6.3).

Bose–Einstein condensation (Section 6.4)

The model maps onto a Bose gas: node $i$ ↔ energy level $\varepsilon_i = -\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\eta_i$, link end ↔ particle. ($\beta$ here is inverse temperature, not the growth exponent.) Two regimes:

  • Fit-get-rich: the fittest become the largest hubs, each with a vanishing share of links.
  • Winner-takes-all (condensation, for some $\rho(\eta)$): one node keeps a finite share of all links as $N \to \infty$; the network becomes star-like.
  • Generalizes the BA model: equal $\eta$ gives $\beta = \frac12$, $\gamma = 3$.
  • Explains exponents other than 3 (Universality).
  • Predicts the order of hubs: age and quality, not age alone.

Why do the oldest web pages not have the most links? What else from Chapter 6 matters?

Fitness, node and link deletion, internal links between old nodes, ageing. Each shifts the exponent; strong ageing destroys the power law.

Uniform fitness: a node with $\eta = 1$ arrives at $t = 1{,}000$. Its degree grows as

  1. $(t/1000)^{0.8}$
  2. $(t/1000)^{0.5}$
  3. Linearly
  4. Not at all
$\beta = 1/1.255 \approx 0.8$.

All nodes have equal fitness. The prediction?

  1. $\gamma = 2.255$
  2. The BA model: $\beta = \frac12$, $\gamma = 3$
  3. Exponential $p_k$
  4. Winner-takes-all
Equal $\eta$ cancel from $\Pi_i$; $C = 2$, so $\beta = \frac12$ and $\gamma = 1 + 1/\beta = 3$.

A: $t = 10$, $\eta = 0.3$. B: $t = 1{,}000$, $\eta = 1$. Which is right?

  1. A stays ahead
  2. B leads at once
  3. B overtakes A near $t \approx 7{,}000$
  4. They converge
$(t/10)^{0.239} = (t/1000)^{0.797}$ at $t \approx 7{,}200$.

In a winner-takes-all network of $10^6$ nodes:

  1. The top hub's share of links vanishes
  2. One node keeps a finite share of all links
  3. Hubs link only to hubs
  4. Degrees are equal
The analogue of Bose–Einstein condensation: a finite fraction of particles in the lowest level.
