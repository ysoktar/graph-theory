Evolving networks

In the BA model the oldest node is always the richest, every network has $\gamma = 3$, and nothing ever disappears. Real networks break all three rules. Chapter 6 adds the missing processes one at a time and shows what each one does to $p_k$.

  • BA ties success to age alone. It predicts that the oldest web pages are the most linked and that $\gamma = 3$ always. Both are false.
  • Each extension adds one elementary process to growth and preferential attachment: fitness, initial attractiveness, internal links, node deletion, accelerated growth, aging.
  • The method is the same every time: write how one node's degree changes, solve for $k_i(t)$, read off $\gamma$. Get the dynamics right and the topology follows.

The Bianconi–Barabási model (Section 6.2)

Each node gets a fixed fitness $\eta_i$ at birth, drawn from $\rho(\eta)$. A new link attaches to node $i$ with probability

$$\Pi_i = \frac{\eta_i k_i}{\sum_j \eta_j k_j}. \qquad (6.1)$$
$\eta_i$
fitness ("eta") of node $i$: intrinsic attractiveness, here in [0, 1]
$k_i$
degree of node $i$
$\sum_j \eta_j k_j$
total attractiveness (normalisation)
$\rho(\eta)$
fitness distribution

Equal fitnesses cancel and give back the BA model. Unequal ones let a fit newcomer outgrow an old hub.

Each node grows at its own speed

The same continuum calculation as for BA (previous page), in five steps:

  1. Rate equation. Each new node brings $m$ links: $\dfrac{dk_i}{dt} = m\,\dfrac{\eta_i k_i}{\sum_j \eta_j k_j}$.
  2. Guess. Try $k_i(t) = m\,(t/t_i)^{\beta(\eta_i)}$ and assume the denominator grows linearly, $\sum_j \eta_j k_j = C m t$.
  3. Exponent. Then $\dfrac{dk_i}{dt} = \dfrac{\eta_i k_i}{C t}$, so $\beta(\eta) = \eta / C$ (6.4).
  4. Self-consistency. Summing the guess over all nodes, $\sum_j \eta_j k_j \approx m t \int \rho(\eta)\,\frac{\eta}{1 - \eta/C}\,d\eta$. Setting this equal to $C m t$ fixes $C$ (6.5):
    $$\int \rho(\eta)\,\frac{\eta}{C - \eta}\,d\eta = 1, \qquad C > \eta_{\max}.$$
  5. Degree distribution. A node of fitness $\eta$ has degree above $k$ if it arrived before $t\,(m/k)^{C/\eta}$. Averaging over fitness gives a mixture of power laws (6.6):
    $$p_k \approx C \int \frac{\rho(\eta)}{\eta}\left(\frac{m}{k}\right)^{C/\eta + 1} d\eta .$$
    Fitness $\eta$ contributes $k^{-(1 + C/\eta)}$, so the fittest nodes dominate the tail: $\gamma \approx 1 + C/\eta_{\max}$.
$t_i$
arrival time of node $i$
$\beta(\eta)$
dynamical exponent of fitness $\eta$ (BA: ½)
$C$
constant set by $\rho(\eta)$ alone

Worked example 1: equal fitness. $\rho(\eta) = \delta(\eta - 1)$: every node has $\eta = 1$.

  1. The integral has one term: $\frac{1}{C - 1} = 1$, so $C = 2$.
  2. $\beta = 1/2$ and $\gamma = 1 + C = 3$: the BA model, as it must be.

Worked example 2: uniform fitness on [0, 1].

  1. $\int_0^1 \frac{\eta}{C - \eta}\,d\eta = -1 + C \ln\frac{C}{C-1}$. Set it to 1: $C \ln\frac{C}{C-1} = 2$.
  2. Solve numerically: $C = 1.255$ (check: $1.255 \times \ln(1.255/0.255) = 1.255 \times 1.594 = 2.00$).
  3. Growth exponents run from $\beta(0) = 0$ to $\beta(1) = 1/1.255 = 0.797$, faster than any BA node.
  4. The tail is $p_k \sim k^{-(1+C)}/\ln k$ (6.8): $\gamma = 2.255$, with a slow $1/\ln k$ correction that makes fitted exponents come out higher.

Worked example 3: two classes. Half the nodes have $\eta = 0.5$, half $\eta = 1$.

  1. $\frac12 \cdot \frac{0.5}{C - 0.5} + \frac12 \cdot \frac{1}{C - 1} = 1$.
  2. Solve: $C = 1.640$. Then $\beta(1) = 0.610$ and $\beta(0.5) = 0.305$.
  3. The fit class sets the tail: $\gamma = 1 + 1.640 = 2.64$, between BA (3) and uniform fitness (2.255).

Worked example 4: the race. Uniform fitness, $C = 1.255$. Node A arrives at $t_A = 10$ with $\eta = 0.3$; node B arrives at $t_B = 1{,}000$ with $\eta = 1$.

  1. Exponents: $\beta_A = 0.3/1.255 = 0.239$, $\beta_B = 1/1.255 = 0.797$.
  2. Equal degrees: $m(t/10)^{0.239} = m(t/1000)^{0.797}$. Take logs: $0.239(\ln t - 2.303) = 0.797(\ln t - 6.908)$.
  3. $\ln t = (5.505 - 0.550)/0.558 = 8.88$, so $t \approx 7{,}200$.
  4. Meaning: B starts with $m$ links when A already has $m \cdot 100^{0.239} \approx 3m$, yet B passes A after about 6,000 more nodes and stays ahead.

A hub is either early (BA) or fit. The fitness distribution $\rho(\eta)$ alone fixes $C$, the growth exponents and $\gamma$.

Measuring fitness (Section 6.3)

Fitness is not assigned by anyone: it is the network's collective judgement, visible in how fast a node grows. Take the logarithm of $k_i(t) = m(t/t_i)^{\beta(\eta_i)}$:

$$\ln k_i(t) = \beta(\eta_i)\,\ln t + B_i, \qquad B_i = \ln\big(m / t_i^{\beta(\eta_i)}\big). \qquad (6.9)$$

On a log-log plot each node's degree history is a straight line whose slope is $\beta(\eta_i) = \eta_i / C$. Tracking many nodes gives the distribution of slopes, which is $\rho(\eta)$ up to the factor $C$.

Worked example 5: fitness from two snapshots. A page has 12 links when the network has 1,000 nodes and 48 links at 16,000 nodes.

  1. Slope: $\beta = \frac{\ln(48/12)}{\ln(16000/1000)} = \frac{\ln 4}{\ln 16} = 0.5$.
  2. With uniform fitness ($C = 1.255$): $\eta = \beta C = 0.63$.
  3. Without $C$ only ratios are known: a page with slope 0.25 has half this fitness. Ranking pages needs no more.

The Web. About 22 million documents were crawled monthly for 13 months. Most did not change their degree; the 6.5% that did gave $\rho(\eta)$, which turned out to be exponential: high fitness is rare and fitness varies over a narrow range. The huge differences in degree are made by the dynamics. Two nodes that arrive together with $\eta_2 > \eta_1$ drift apart as

$$\frac{k_2 - k_1}{k_1} \sim t^{(\eta_2 - \eta_1)/C}. \qquad (6.10)$$

Worked example 6: small differences, large effects. $\eta_2 - \eta_1 = 0.1$ and $C = 1.255$ give an exponent of 0.08. While the network grows a million-fold, the fitter node's degree pulls ahead by a factor $10^{6 \times 0.08} \approx 3$.

Citations. Papers also age: their novelty fades. The probability that paper $i$ is cited at time $t$ after publication is

$$\Pi_i \sim \eta_i\, c_i^t\, P_i(t), \qquad P_i(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_i t}\, e^{-\frac{(\ln t - \mu_i)^2}{2\sigma_i^2}}, \qquad (6.11\text{–}6.12)$$

with $c_i^t$ the citations so far (preferential attachment) and $P_i(t)$ a log-normal aging factor. Solving gives

$$c_i^t = m\left(e^{\eta_i' \,\Phi\left(\frac{\ln t - \mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right)} - 1\right), \qquad c_i^\infty = m\left(e^{\eta_i'} - 1\right), \qquad (6.13)$$
$\Phi(x)$
cumulative normal distribution (6.14); it rises from 0 to 1
$\mu_i$
immediacy: when the paper reaches its citation peak
$\sigma_i$
longevity: how slowly its attention decays
$\eta_i' = \eta_i\beta/A$
relative fitness; alone it fixes the ultimate impact $c_i^\infty$
$m, \beta, A$
global constants, the same for all papers

Fitting (6.13) to each paper of a journal gives the journal's fitness distribution: Cell papers sit to the right (high fitness), Physical Review B to the left. Fitness can even be inherited: in a study of 1,110 twins, genes explained 46% of the variation in how many classmates named a student as a friend (in-degree), but not out-degree (Box 6.1).

Bose–Einstein condensation (Section 6.4)

The fitness model maps exactly onto a Bose gas:

NetworkBose gas
node $i$ with fitness $\eta_i$energy level $\varepsilon_i = -\frac{1}{\beta_T}\ln\eta_i$ (fitter = lower)
a link pointing to node $i$a particle on level $\varepsilon_i$
a new node with $m$ linksa new level, and $m$ new particles

Then $k_i = m(t/t_i)^{f(\varepsilon_i)}$ with $f(\varepsilon) = e^{-\beta_T(\varepsilon - \mu)}$, and the self-consistency condition (6.5) becomes the equation for the chemical potential $\mu$ of a Bose gas:

$$\int g(\varepsilon)\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta_T(\varepsilon - \mu)} - 1}\,d\varepsilon = 1 .$$

$g(\varepsilon)$ is the density of levels (set by $\rho(\eta)$) and the fraction is the Bose occupation number. The "temperature" $1/\beta_T$ is a dummy: it cancels, and only $\rho(\eta)$ decides the phase.

  • Fit-get-rich (the equation for $C$ has a solution with $C > \eta_{\max}$): the fittest nodes become the largest hubs, closely trailed by the next fittest. Each holds a vanishing share of the links; the network is scale-free.
  • Bose–Einstein condensation (no such solution): $C$ sticks at $\eta_{\max}$ and the nodes of finite fitness absorb only part of the new links. The rest, a finite fraction, condenses on the fittest nodes: winner-takes-all, a hub-and-spoke network that is no longer scale-free.

With $\eta_{\max} = 1$, condensation happens when even $C = 1$ leaves the integral short of 1:

$$I = \int \frac{\eta\,\rho(\eta)}{1 - \eta}\,d\eta < 1, \qquad \text{condensate share} = 1 - I .$$

Worked example 7: when does a network condense? Take $\rho(\eta) = (1 + \zeta)(1 - \eta)^{\zeta}$, which puts less weight near $\eta = 1$ as $\zeta$ grows.

  1. $I = (1+\zeta)\int_0^1 \eta (1-\eta)^{\zeta - 1} d\eta = (1 + \zeta)\,\frac{1}{\zeta(\zeta+1)} = \frac{1}{\zeta}$.
  2. $I < 1$ exactly when $\zeta > 1$. So $\zeta = 0.1$ is fit-get-rich and $\zeta = 10$ condenses, with share $1 - 1/10 = 90\%$.
  3. Uniform fitness ($\zeta = 0$): $\int_0^1 \frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\,d\eta$ diverges at $\eta = 1$, so $I = \infty$: no condensation.

Paradox: fewer very fit nodes means condensation. When fit nodes are rare, the one that does arrive has no competitors.

Who holds the condensate? The book, following the 2001 physics paper, says the fittest node keeps a finite fraction of all links. For the tree version of the model ($m = 1$) this was later proved false: the condensation is non-extensive. The condensed share is spread over a succession of ever fitter latecomers, each of which is eventually overtaken, so the largest hub's share still vanishes, very slowly. The condition $I < 1$ and the share $1 - I$ are unaffected; only "one node takes it all" is. The simulations in the figure above show it: even at $10^6$ nodes the top hub of a $\zeta = 10$ network holds a few percent, not 90%.

Other elementary processes (Section 6.5)

Each process below is added to the BA model ($m$ links per new node, $\Pi \propto k$) and changes the exponent in a predictable direction: extra advantage for the large lowers $\gamma$, anything random raises it.

Initial attractiveness

In BA a node with $k = 0$ can never get a link. In reality a new paper does get its first citation. Add a constant:

$$\Pi(k) \sim A + k \quad (6.23) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \gamma = 3 + \frac{A}{m} \quad (6.24), \qquad p_k = C\,(k + A)^{-\gamma}. \quad (6.25)$$

Two effects: $\gamma$ rises (the random part $A$ dilutes the preference), and $p_k$ flattens for $k \ll A$ (small-degree saturation). Citations have $A \approx 7$: a new paper is about as likely to be cited as one with seven citations.

Internal links

On the Web most new links join two existing pages. Let each step add a node with $m$ links plus $n$ links between existing nodes, with $\Pi(k, k') \sim (A + Bk)(A + Bk')$ (6.26):

$$\text{double preferential } (A = 0):\ \gamma = 2 + \frac{m}{m + 2n} \quad (6.27) \qquad\quad \text{random } (B = 0):\ \gamma = 3 + \frac{2n}{m} \quad (6.28)$$

Worked example 8: one model, two answers. $m = 1$, $n = 2$.

  1. Double preferential: $\gamma = 2 + \frac{1}{1 + 4} = 2.2$. Internal links feed the hubs at both ends.
  2. Random: $\gamma = 3 + \frac{4}{1} = 7$. Internal links spread evenly and wash the hubs out.
  3. Why: in the double case every degree grows as $t^{\beta}$ with $\beta = \frac{m + 2n}{2(m+n)} = \frac56$, and $\gamma = 1 + 1/\beta = 2.2$.

Node deletion

Each step add a BA node, and remove a random node with rate $r$:

  • $r < 1$: the network still grows and stays scale-free, with $\gamma = 3 + \dfrac{2r}{1-r}$ (6.29, corrected).
  • $r = 1$: fixed size; $\gamma \to \infty$, the distribution becomes exponential.
  • $r > 1$: a declining network (Box 6.5). The New York garment industry's largest component shrank from 3,249 firms in 1985 to 190 in 2003, while its degree distribution stayed the same. Newcomers still attached superlinearly ($\alpha = 1.20$), and firms lost links with probability $\sim k^{-0.41}$: the weak got weaker.

Combined with initial attractiveness, deletion has a critical rate $r^*(A)$: below it scale-free, at it a stretched exponential, above it exponential (Figure 6.12).

Accelerated growth

Average degrees grow: the Internet from $\langle k\rangle = 3.42$ (November 1997) to 3.96 (December 1998), the WWW from 7.22 to 7.86 in five months. Let each node bring more links than the last:

$$m(t) = m_0\, t^{\theta} \quad (6.30) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \gamma = 3 + \frac{2\theta}{1 - \theta}. \quad (6.31)$$

Why: $L \approx m_0 t^{1+\theta}/(1+\theta)$, so every degree grows as $t^{(1+\theta)/2}$, but late nodes also start bigger, at $m_0 t_i^{\theta}$. The net degree of a node born at $t_i$ scales as $t_i^{-(1-\theta)/2}$, giving $\gamma = 1 + \frac{2}{1-\theta}$, the same as (6.31). At $\theta = 1$ the exponent diverges.

Aging

Actors and scientists retire; old papers stop being cited. Let attractiveness fade with age:

$$\Pi(k_i, t - t_i) \sim k_i\,(t - t_i)^{-\nu}. \qquad (6.32)$$
  • $\nu < 0$: old nodes are favoured even more; still scale-free with $\gamma < 3$. As $\nu \to -\infty$ every node links to the oldest one: a star.
  • $0 < \nu < 1$: still scale-free, $\gamma > 3$, growing without bound as $\nu \to 1$.
  • $\nu > 1$: aging wins and the power law is lost. As $\nu \to \infty$ every node links to the newest one: a chain.

To understand a network's structure, first get its dynamics right; the topology is the bonus. Fitness explains who becomes a hub, the other processes explain which exponent and shape appear.

  • Generalizes the BA model: equal $\eta$ gives $\beta = \frac12$, $\gamma = 3$; $A = n = r = \theta = \nu = 0$ gives it back too.
  • Explains exponents other than 3 (Universality) and the deviations a fit must handle (Fitting a power law).
  • Matters later: $\gamma < 3$ makes networks robust to failures (Ch 8) and lowers the epidemic threshold (Ch 10).
