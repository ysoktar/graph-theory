Real networks are not random

Chapter 3 built the random network as a null model. Now we hold it against the data, property by property. The verdict decides where Chapter 4 goes next.

Erdős and Rényi never claimed that real networks are random; the model is a baseline. A property that the random network reproduces needs no special explanation. A property it misses tells us that some non-random process shaped the network.

The random network model fails on the two most local features of real networks: how many links a node has (hubs) and whether neighbours are linked (triangles). Chapter 4 takes up the first failure and replaces the Poisson distribution with a power law. Clustering returns in Chapter 9.

The random model gets distances roughly right for six of the eight networks. Does that make it a good model of how those networks are wired?

No. Short distances are generic: almost any network with a few random-looking long-range links is a small world (see the Watts–Strogatz model). Agreement on a property that nearly every model reproduces is weak evidence. The properties that discriminate between models, here the degree distribution and clustering, are the ones the random network gets wrong.

Which observation about the collaboration network could a random network with the same $N$ and $L$ explain?

  1. A scientist with 279 collaborators
  2. $\langle C\rangle = 0.63$
  3. Most scientists are connected to each other through about five intermediaries
  4. 1,770 scientists outside the giant component
A random network with these parameters has $\langle d\rangle \approx 5.1$, close to the observed 5.3. It would have $k_{\max} \approx 22$, $\langle C\rangle \approx 3\times10^{-4}$, and only about 4 to 7 nodes outside the giant component.
