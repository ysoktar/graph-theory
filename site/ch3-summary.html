Real networks are not random

Which features of real networks need an explanation beyond chance? Hold the random network against the data, property by property. Its failures decide where Chapter 4 goes next.

  • Erdős and Rényi never claimed that real networks are random: their model is a baseline.
  • If the model reproduces a property, that property needs no special explanation. If it misses one, some non-random process is at work.

The model fails on the two most local features: how many links a node has (hubs) and whether its neighbours are linked (triangles). Chapter 4 replaces the Poisson distribution with a power law; clustering returns in Chapter 9.

  • Summarises degrees, components, distances and clustering: each a Part I quantity, first predicted and then tested.
  • Motivates scale-free networks (Ch 4) and the Barabási–Albert model (Ch 5), which explains the hubs.
  • A hierarchy of null models: $G(N, L)$ keeps $N$ and $L$; the configuration model also keeps every node's degree.

The random model gets distances roughly right for six of the eight networks. Does that make it a good model of how they are wired?

No. Almost any network with a few long-range links is a small world (Watts–Strogatz), so agreement on distances is weak evidence. The properties that discriminate between models, degrees and clustering, are the ones it gets wrong.

The random counterpart has $\langle d\rangle \approx 5.1$, against an observed 5.3. Every other value is far off.

$8.08 \times 9.08 = 73.3$. Hubs inflate $\langle k^2\rangle$: for the email network the real value is 90 times its Poisson value.

Why is agreement on distances weak evidence that a network is random?

  1. The distances are measured badly.
  2. The ln N formula is inaccurate.
  3. Real distances are always longer.
  4. Almost any network with some long-range links is a small world.
A property that nearly every model reproduces cannot tell models apart.
