The role of the degree exponent

Most properties of a scale-free network depend on $\gamma$, and they change qualitatively at $\gamma = 2$ and $\gamma = 3$ (Section 4.7). This page collects the results of the previous pages into three regimes.

Why $\gamma = 2$ is a wall: $k_{\max} \approx k_{\min} N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$; with $\gamma = 1.8$, $k_{\min} = 1$, $N = 10^4$: $10^{4/0.8} = 10^5 > N$.

Real networks

To tell a power law with $\gamma > 3$ from a random network's degree distribution you need a tail long enough to see the straight line. How large must $N$ be?

At least two orders of magnitude: $k_{\max}/k_{\min} = N^{1/(\gamma-1)} \gtrsim 100$, i.e. $N \gtrsim 100^{\gamma-1}$. For $\gamma = 3.5$, $N \gtrsim 10^5$; for $\gamma = 5$, $N \gtrsim 10^8$. Section 4.7: for large $\gamma$ the scale-free property is practically invisible at realistic sizes.
  • One number, $\gamma$, tells which of three qualitatively different behaviours to expect.
  • The boundaries 2 and 3 are where $\langle k\rangle$ and $\langle k^2\rangle$ start to diverge.
  • Summarises hubs, moments and distances.
  • Special case: $\gamma \to \infty$ recovers random-network behaviour.
  • Later: the BA model of Chapter 5 gives $\gamma = 3$, right on the boundary.

Which regime is the phone-call network in, given $\hat\gamma = 4.76 \pm 0.13$?

  1. Anomalous
  2. Random-like: $\langle k^2\rangle$ stays finite as the network grows
  3. Scale-free with divergent $\langle k^2\rangle$
  4. It cannot be decided
$\gamma > 3$, so both moments converge. Its $\sigma_k$ is only 1.6 times the random value (No typical degree).

Which network falls in the scale-free regime $2 < \gamma < 3$?

  1. Email ($\hat\gamma = 1.91$)
  2. Internet ($\hat\gamma = 3.40$)
  3. WWW ($\hat\gamma = 2.16$)
  4. Collaboration ($\hat\gamma = 3.43$)
Only metabolism (2.71), protein interactions (2.78) and the WWW (2.16) lie between 2 and 3.

About how many nodes are needed to see two decades of power-law tail with $\gamma = 4$?

  1. $10^6$
  2. $10^4$
  3. $100$
  4. $10^8$
$N \gtrsim 100^{\gamma - 1} = 100^3 = 10^6$.

Why can a large simple network not keep $\gamma < 2$ up to its largest degrees?

  1. Too many nodes would have degree 1
  2. $\langle k^2\rangle$ would diverge
  3. Distances would be too long
  4. The largest hub would need more than $N - 1$ links
$k_{\max} \propto N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$ grows faster than $N$ for $\gamma < 2$, impossible without repeated links.

$\gamma = 3$, $k_{\min} = 1$, $N = 10^6$. What is the natural cutoff?

  1. 100
  2. 1,000
  3. 10,000
  4. 10⁶
$k_{\max} \approx N^{1/(\gamma-1)} = (10^6)^{1/2} = 1{,}000$.

A network with $\gamma = 3.5$ is called \"not really scale-free\". What is the most precise statement?

  1. Its degrees can follow a power law, but at realistic sizes its moments and distances look random-like
  2. Its degree distribution must be Poisson
  3. It has no hubs at all
  4. $\langle k\rangle$ diverges
For $\gamma > 3$ both $\langle k\rangle$ and $\langle k^2\rangle$ converge and $\langle d\rangle \sim \ln N$, as in a random network.

Why is $\gamma = 3$ a boundary?

  1. $\langle k\rangle$ diverges below it
  2. Hubs disappear above it
  3. Networks cannot exist below it
  4. $\langle k^2\rangle$ diverges below it and converges above it
$\langle k^2\rangle$ converges when $2 - \gamma + 1 < 0$, i.e. $\gamma > 3$.
