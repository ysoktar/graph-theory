Weighted and bipartite networks

Links differ in strength, and many data sets link two kinds of node, such as people and the events they attend. Weighted and bipartite networks record this. Projecting a bipartite network onto one kind of node loses some of that information.

Weighted networks (Section 2.6)

In a weighted network each link carries a number, its weight: minutes of phone calls, the current on a power line, the number of joint papers. The matrix stores the weight, $A_{ij} = w_{ij}$, and the degree generalises to the strength:

$$s_i = \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{ij}.$$
$w_{ij}$
the weight of the link $i$–$j$ (0 if there is no link)
$s_i$
the strength: the sum of the weights of node $i$'s links
$s_i/k_i$
the average weight of $i$'s links

Zachary's link weights count the number of contexts (classes, a campus bar, tournaments and so on) in which two members interacted, from 1 to 7.

Member 0 has $k = 16$ links with total weight $s = 42$, so $s/k \approx 2.6$. Member 25 has $k = 3$ links with weights 5, 2 and 7, so $s = 14$ and $s/k \approx 4.7$.

  • Strength grows with degree, but not in proportion: some members have few links that are all strong.
  • Raise the threshold. Links between the factions are weak: 11 of the 78 links cross, but only 4 of the 48 links with $w \ge 3$ and none with $w \ge 5$. The thresholded network depends on an arbitrary cut-off.
  • Weights keep what a 0/1 network throws away: how strong or busy a tie is.
  • They let us ask which ties hold a group together, or which lines carry most of the load.

Bipartite networks (Section 2.7)

A bipartite network has two sets of nodes, $U$ and $V$, and links only between the sets: actors and films, genes and diseases, women and events. It is stored in the biadjacency matrix $B$, with one row per $U$-node and one column per $V$-node, and $B_{ue} = 1$ when $u$ is linked to $e$.

The projection onto $U$ links two $U$-nodes whenever they share a $V$-neighbour, and gives the link a weight equal to the number of neighbours they share:

$$W = B\,B^{\mathsf T}, \qquad W_{uv} = \sum_{e \in V} B_{ue}B_{ve}, \qquad W_{uu} = \text{degree of } u.$$
$U$, $V$
the two sets of nodes (here, women and events)
$B$
the $|U| \times |V|$ biadjacency matrix
$B^{\mathsf T}$
the transpose of $B$: rows and columns swapped
$B_{ue}B_{ve}$
1 if both $u$ and $v$ attended event $e$
$W_{uv}$
the number of events that $u$ and $v$ both attended
$W_{uu}$
the number of events that $u$ attended

Projecting onto $V$ uses $B^{\mathsf T}B$ instead. The data are Davis, Gardner and Gardner's Southern Women study (1941): 18 women and the 14 social events they attended.

Evelyn (woman 1) attended 8 events and Laura (woman 2) attended 7, six of them together, so $W_{11} = 8$, $W_{22} = 7$ and $W_{12} = 6$. Event E8 was attended by 14 women, so it alone creates $\binom{14}{2} = 91$ links in the projection.

  • The 89 links of the bipartite network become 139 links among the 18 women (density 0.91), because a single shared event is enough for a link.
  • Keep only pairs that share at least 4 events. Two groups appear, women 1–7 and 9, and women 10–15, close to the two cliques reported in the original study.
  • Many networks are projections: co-authorship comes from authors and papers, the Hollywood network from actors and films.
  • The bipartite form keeps what the projection loses: which paper, which film.
  • It shows which features the projection itself creates, such as dense clusters.

The Hollywood actor network in the book is the projection of an actor–film bipartite network. Which of its properties are produced by the projection itself, rather than by how actors choose to work together?

Every film with $n$ actors becomes a complete subgraph of $n$ nodes, so the projection is full of triangles (high clustering) and large cliques. Large casts inflate the degrees. A null model for a projected network should be a randomised bipartite network, projected the same way.
  • Generalises the degree: with all weights equal to 1, the strength is the degree.
  • Uses the same algebra as $A^2$: $W = BB^{\mathsf T}$ counts shared neighbours.
  • Explains part of the high clustering of real networks: each paper or film becomes a clique.
  • Null model: randomise the bipartite network, then project it (compare the configuration model).

A node's three links have weights 2, 5 and 7. What are its degree and its strength?

  1. $k = 3$ and $s = 14$.
  2. $k = 14$ and $s = 3$.
  3. $k = 3$ and $s \approx 4.67$.
  4. $k = 3$ and $s = 7$.
The node has $k = 3$ links and strength $s = 2 + 5 + 7 = 14$ (this is karate member 25).

Can a bipartite network contain a triangle?

  1. Yes, if both kinds of node are involved.
  2. No: every cycle alternates between the two kinds, so its length is even.
  3. Only if it is weighted.
  4. Only in the matrix of its projection.
A cycle $U \to V \to U \to \dots$ can close only after an even number of steps. Projections, by contrast, are full of triangles.

Five women attend one party together and share no other event. How many links does this party create in the projection onto the women?

  1. 5
  2. 10
  3. 20
  4. 4
$\binom{5}{2} = 10$: the event becomes a complete subgraph of 5 nodes.

In $W = BB^{\mathsf T}$, what is the diagonal entry $W_{uu}$?

  1. 0, because there are no self-loops.
  2. The number of events that woman $u$ attended.
  3. The number of women she shares an event with.
  4. Always 1.
$W_{uu} = \sum_e B_{ue}^2 = \sum_e B_{ue}$, the row sum of $B$ (8 for Evelyn).

11 of the karate club's 78 links join members of different factions, but none of the 9 links with weight $w \ge 5$ does. What does this suggest?

  1. Ties between the factions were mostly weak.
  2. The factions never interacted.
  3. The weights are unrelated to the factions.
  4. A threshold of $w \ge 5$ hides the factions.
Strong ties stay within groups, so dropping weak links separates the groups. The choice of threshold decides what you see.

Woman $u$ attended events E1, E2, E3 and E5; woman $v$ attended E2, E3 and E4. What is $W_{uv}$?

  1. 7
  2. 3
  3. 1
  4. 2
They share E2 and E3, so $W_{uv} = 2$.

In the projection onto the events, when are two events linked?

  1. When $(BB^{\mathsf T})_{ef} > 0$.
  2. When they took place on the same day.
  3. When at least one woman attended both; the weight is the number of such women.
  4. When every woman attended both.
The event projection is $B^{\mathsf T}B$: $(B^{\mathsf T}B)_{ef}$ counts the women who attended both $e$ and $f$.
