Weighted and bipartite networks

Links differ in strength, and many data sets link two kinds of node (people and the events they attend). Weights and bipartite networks record this; projecting a bipartite network loses some of it.

Weighted networks (Section 2.6)

In a weighted network each link carries a number, its weight: phone minutes, current on a power line, joint papers. The matrix stores it, $A_{ij} = w_{ij}$, and the degree generalises to the strength:

$$s_i = \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{ij}.$$
$w_{ij}$
weight of the link $i$–$j$ (0 if none)
$s_i$
strength: sum of node $i$'s link weights
$s_i/k_i$
average weight of $i$'s links

Zachary's link weights count the contexts (class, a campus bar, tournaments …) in which two members interacted: 1 to 7.

Member 0: $k = 16$, $s = 42$, $s/k \approx 2.6$. Member 25: $k = 3$, weights 5, 2, 7, so $s = 14$, $s/k \approx 4.7$.

  • Strength grows with degree, but not proportionally: some members have few links that are all strong.
  • Raise the threshold. Cross-faction links are weak: 11 of the 78 links cross, 4 of the 48 with $w \ge 3$, none with $w \ge 5$. The thresholded network depends on an arbitrary cut-off.
  • Weights keep what a 0/1 network discards: how strong or busy a tie is.
  • They let us ask which ties hold a group together, or which lines carry most load.

Bipartite networks (Section 2.7)

A bipartite network has two node sets $U$ and $V$, with links only between them: actors–films, genes–diseases, women–events. It is stored in the biadjacency matrix $B$, one row per $U$-node and one column per $V$-node, $B_{ue} = 1$ if $u$ is linked to $e$.

The projection onto $U$ links two $U$-nodes that share a $V$-neighbour, with weight = number shared:

$$W = B\,B^{\mathsf T}, \qquad W_{uv} = \sum_{e \in V} B_{ue}B_{ve}, \qquad W_{uu} = \text{degree of } u.$$
$U$, $V$
the two node sets (women, events)
$B$
$|U| \times |V|$ biadjacency matrix
$B^{\mathsf T}$
transpose: rows and columns swapped
$B_{ue}B_{ve}$
1 if both $u$ and $v$ attend $e$
$W_{uv}$
events shared by $u$ and $v$
$W_{uu}$
events attended by $u$

Projecting onto $V$ uses $B^{\mathsf T}B$. Data: Davis, Gardner and Gardner's Southern Women (1941), 18 women and the 14 social events they attended.

Evelyn (1) attended 8 events, Laura (2) 7, six together: $W_{11} = 8$, $W_{22} = 7$, $W_{12} = 6$. Event E8 had 14 women, so it alone gives $\binom{14}{2} = 91$ projected links.

  • 89 bipartite links become 139 links among 18 women (density 0.91): one shared event is enough.
  • Keep pairs sharing at least 4 events: two groups appear, women 1–7 and 9, and 10–15, close to the two cliques of the original study.
  • Many networks are projections: co-authorship (authors–papers), Hollywood (actors–films).
  • The bipartite form keeps what projection loses (which paper, which film).
  • It shows which features the projection itself creates, such as dense clusters.

The Hollywood actor network in the book is the projection of an actor–film bipartite network. Which of its properties are produced by the projection itself, rather than by how actors choose to work together?

Every film with $n$ actors becomes a complete subgraph of $n$ nodes, so the projection is full of triangles (high clustering) and large cliques. Large casts inflate degrees. A null model for a projected network should be a randomised bipartite network, projected the same way.
  • Generalises degree: strength with all $w = 1$ is the degree.
  • Same algebra as $A^2$: $W = BB^{\mathsf T}$ counts shared neighbours.
  • Explains part of high clustering: each paper or film becomes a clique.
  • Null model: randomise the bipartite network, then project (compare configuration model).

A node's three links have weights 2, 5, 7. Degree and strength?

  1. $k = 3$, $s = 14$
  2. $k = 14$, $s = 3$
  3. $k = 3$, $s \approx 4.67$
  4. $k = 3$, $s = 7$
$k = 3$ links; $s = 2 + 5 + 7 = 14$ (karate member 25).

Can a bipartite network contain a triangle?

  1. Yes, if both kinds are involved
  2. No: every cycle alternates kinds, so its length is even
  3. Only if it is weighted
  4. Only in its projection's matrix
A cycle $U \to V \to U \to \dots$ closes only after an even number of steps. Projections, in contrast, are full of triangles.

Five women attend one party together and share no other event. How many projected links does it create?

  1. 5
  2. 10
  3. 20
  4. 4
$\binom{5}{2} = 10$: the event becomes a complete subgraph of 5 nodes.

In $W = BB^{\mathsf T}$, what is the diagonal entry $W_{uu}$?

  1. 0, since there are no self-loops
  2. The number of events woman $u$ attended
  3. The number of women she shares an event with
  4. Always 1
$W_{uu} = \sum_e B_{ue}^2 = \sum_e B_{ue}$, the row sum of $B$ (8 for Evelyn).

11 of the karate club's 78 links cross the factions, but none of the 9 with $w \ge 5$. What does this suggest?

  1. Cross-faction ties were mostly weak
  2. The factions never interacted
  3. Weights are unrelated to the factions
  4. Thresholding at $w \ge 5$ hides the factions
Strong ties stay within groups, so dropping weak links separates them; the threshold choice decides what you see.
