Random failures and the Molloy–Reed criterion

When nodes fail at random, when does the giant component vanish? The Molloy–Reed criterion answers from two numbers, $\langle k\rangle$ and $\langle k^2\rangle$, and shows why scale-free networks survive the loss of almost all their nodes (Section 8.3).

  • One inequality, $\kappa = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle > 2$, decides whether a giant component exists; $\kappa = 2$ exactly is the critical point.
  • Removing nodes lowers $\kappa$; the critical fraction is where it reaches 2.
  • A diverging $\langle k^2\rangle$ pushes that fraction towards 1: robustness comes from the hubs.

The Molloy–Reed criterion

When does a network with a given degree distribution have a giant component? Follow a path into the network: it continues only if the node it reaches has at least one more link.

  1. A random link leads to a node of degree $k$ with probability $q_k = k\,p_k/\langle k\rangle$: high-degree nodes are reached more often.
  2. The average degree of the node reached is therefore $\sum_k k\,q_k = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ (the friendship paradox).
  3. One of its links is the one we arrived on. For the path to go on, at least one more is needed on average: the node must have degree 2 or more.
$$\kappa = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} > 2 .$$
$\kappa$
mean degree of a node reached along a random link
$\langle k\rangle$, $\langle k^2\rangle$
mean degree and mean squared degree; $\langle k^2\rangle$ is large when there are hubs (Ch 4)
$2$
one link in, at least one out

It assumes random wiring given the degrees (the configuration model). For a random network $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1 + \langle k\rangle)$, so $\kappa = 1 + \langle k\rangle$ and the criterion becomes $\langle k\rangle > 1$, the giant-component threshold of Chapter 3.

The critical fraction

Remove a fraction $f$ of the nodes at random. A surviving node of degree $k$ keeps each of its links with probability $1 - f$, so its degree becomes binomial. The moments of the new distribution are (Advanced Topic 8.C)

$$\begin{gathered} \langle k\rangle_f = (1-f)\langle k\rangle, \\ \langle k^2\rangle_f = (1-f)^2\langle k^2\rangle + f(1-f)\langle k\rangle, \\ \kappa_f = (1-f)\,\kappa + f . \end{gathered}$$

The giant component disappears when $\kappa_f = 2$. Solving for $f$:

$$f_c = 1 - \frac{1}{\kappa - 1}, \qquad f_c^{ER} = 1 - \frac{1}{\langle k\rangle}\ \text{(random network)}.$$
$f_c$
fraction of random failures that destroys the giant component; it depends only on $\langle k\rangle$ and $\langle k^2\rangle$

For a power law $p_k \sim k^{-\gamma}$ starting at $k_{\min}$ (continuum approximation, Eq. 8.9):

$$f_c = \begin{cases} 1 - \dfrac{1}{\frac{\gamma-2}{3-\gamma}\,k_{\min}^{\gamma-2}\,k_{\max}^{3-\gamma} - 1} & 2 < \gamma < 3\\[3mm] 1 - \dfrac{1}{k_{\min}\ln(k_{\max}/k_{\min}) - 1} & \gamma = 3\\[3mm] 1 - \dfrac{1}{\frac{\gamma-2}{\gamma-3}\,k_{\min} - 1} & \gamma > 3 \end{cases}$$
$$\Rightarrow\quad f_c \approx 1 - \frac{C}{N^{(3-\gamma)/(\gamma-1)}}\ \ (2 < \gamma < 3).$$
$k_{\max}$
the natural cutoff $k_{\min}N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$; it grows with $N$, so $\kappa \to \infty$ and $f_c \to 1$
$C$
a constant that collects everything independent of $N$ (Eq. 8.10)

Worked example: the Internet.

  1. Given: $\langle k\rangle = 6.34$ and $\langle k^2\rangle = 240.1$ for the router map ($N = 192{,}244$).
  2. $\kappa = 240.1/6.34 = 37.9$, far above 2.
  3. $f_c = 1 - 1/(37.9 - 1) = 1 - 1/36.9 = 0.973$.
  4. A random network with the same $\langle k\rangle$: $f_c^{ER} = 1 - 1/6.34 = 0.842$.
  5. Meaning: 97% of the routers, about 187,000, must fail at random to fragment it. The hubs supply the extra robustness.

Worked example: when the network stops being special. For $\gamma = 4$ and $k_{\min} = 2$, the continuum formula gives $\kappa = \frac{2}{1}\cdot 2 = 4$ and $f_c = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3$, independent of $N$: above $\gamma = 3$ a scale-free network breaks after a finite fraction of failures, like a random one.

$f_c = 1 - 1/4 = 75\%$; the survivors have $\langle k\rangle = 1$.

A randomly wired network has $\langle k\rangle = 5$ and $\langle k^2\rangle = 255$. What fraction $f_c$ of random failures destroys its giant component?

  1. 80%
  2. 98%
  3. 51%
  4. 2%
$\kappa = 255/5 = 51$, so $f_c = 1 - 1/(51 - 1) = 98\%$. The hubs keep the network together until almost every node has failed.

The power grid ($\kappa = 3.9$) breaks well before the Molloy–Reed $f_c = 65\%$. Why?

  1. It is spatial, not randomly wired: long chains, few alternative paths
  2. It is too small
  3. Its hubs fail first
  4. Degrees are mismeasured
