The largest hub

How many links does the best-connected node have? In a random network the answer barely changes as the network grows. In a scale-free network it grows like a power of the network's size (Section 4.3).

The natural cutoff

The largest degree $k_{\max}$ is roughly the degree above which we expect a single node:

$$N \int_{k_{\max}}^{\infty} p(k)\, dk \approx 1 .$$
$k_{\max}$
largest degree in the network
$N$
number of nodes
$p(k)$
degree density (continuous)
$\int_{k_{\max}}^{\infty} p(k)\,dk$
share of nodes with degree above $k_{\max}$
  1. Power law: $\int_{k_{\max}}^{\infty} (\gamma-1)\,k_{\min}^{\gamma-1}k^{-\gamma}\,dk = (k_{\max}/k_{\min})^{-(\gamma-1)}$.
  2. Set $N\,(k_{\max}/k_{\min})^{-(\gamma-1)} = 1$.
  3. Solve: $k_{\max}/k_{\min} = N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$.

This is the natural cutoff:

$$k_{\max} \approx k_{\min}\, N^{1/(\gamma-1)} .$$
$k_{\min}$
smallest degree of the power law
$\gamma$
degree exponent
$1/(\gamma-1)$
growth exponent of the hub: $\tfrac23$ for $\gamma = 2.5$

For an exponential $p(k) = \lambda e^{-\lambda(k - k_{\min})}$ the same steps give $k_{\max} \approx k_{\min} + \ln N/\lambda$: logarithmic growth ($\lambda$ — decay rate, $1/\lambda$ — mean excess over $k_{\min}$). A Poisson tail falls faster still, so its maximum grows roughly like $\ln N/\ln\ln N$.

$N = 10^6$, $k_{\min} = 1$, $\langle k\rangle = 3$: power law ($\gamma = 2.5$) $10^{6 \cdot 2/3} = 10{,}000$; exponential $1 + 2\ln 10^6 \approx 29$; Poisson 14.

Simulation

The sampled maxima grow with the slopes the theory predicts: a power of $N$ for the power law, barely at all for the exponential and Poisson. Hubs follow from the heavy tail alone.

The book's networks

For a real network the natural cutoff of the fitted tail is $k_{\min}\,n_{\text{tail}}^{1/(\gamma-1)}$, with $n_{\text{tail}}$ the number of nodes with $k \ge k_{\min}$ (notebook cell 59, here for all eight networks).

  • Every observed hub is far beyond the Poisson value: about 7 to 510 times, except the power grid (19 against 10).
  • For $\hat\gamma$ near 2 (email, WWW) the cutoff overshoots by 8 to 10: real tails bend down at the highest degrees.
  • Not general: the metabolic hub (highlighted) is 2.9 times above its cutoff (638 against 218); phone calls and the power grid are slightly above too.
  • Hub size is the clearest testable difference between the random and scale-free models.
  • The cutoff tells how large a hub to expect for a given $N$ and $\gamma$: an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a bound.
  • $k_{\max}$ also sets how far the moments can grow (next page).
  • Predicted by the power law with the fitted $\hat\gamma$ and $k_{\min}$.
  • Contrasts with the slowly growing Poisson maximum of the random network.
  • Feeds divergent moments and ultra-small distances.

A network with $\gamma = 3$, $k_{\min} = 2$ grows from $10^4$ to $10^6$ nodes. By roughly what factor does its largest hub grow?

  1. About 2
  2. About 10
  3. About 100
  4. It does not grow
$k_{\max} \propto N^{1/(\gamma-1)} = N^{1/2}$: $\sqrt{100} = 10$, from about 200 to 2,000 links.

What happens to the natural cutoff as $\gamma \to 2$ from above?

  1. It becomes independent of $N$
  2. It grows like $\ln N$
  3. It approaches $k_{\min} N$: the largest hub becomes comparable to the whole network
  4. It shrinks to $k_{\min}$
$1/(\gamma-1) \to 1$, so $k_{\max} \propto N$. A node cannot have more than $N - 1$ links, which is why $\gamma = 2$ is a boundary (role of γ).

A random network has $N = 10^6$ and $\langle k\rangle = 3$. About how many links does its largest node have?

  1. About 14
  2. About 10,000
  3. About 29
  4. 3
Solving $N\,P(K > k_{\max}) = 1$ for a Poisson distribution with mean 3 gives about 14.

The metabolic network's largest hub (638) is 2.9 times its natural cutoff (218). What does that show?

  1. The power-law fit is wrong
  2. Real hubs always exceed the cutoff
  3. The network is random
  4. The natural cutoff is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a bound
The cutoff gives the expected size of the largest degree; a single sample can land well above or below it.
