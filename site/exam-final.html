One storyline runs through the course:

  1. The random network is the null model: what chance gives (Ch 3).
  2. Real networks are scale-free: hubs, huge $\langle k^2\rangle$ (Ch 4).
  3. The BA model explains where power laws come from (Ch 5).
  4. Fitness explains why latecomers win and why $\gamma \ne 3$ (Ch 6).
  5. Correlations refine who links to whom, beyond the degrees (Ch 7).
  6. Robustness depends on $\langle k^2\rangle$: hubs make failures harmless and attacks deadly (Ch 8). The epidemic threshold depends on the same ratio.
  7. Embeddings turn each node's position into a vector, so machine learning can use the structure (GraphML).

Ch 1–5 · Compact recap

Full treatment with problems: midterm guide. The results the later chapters build on:

  • $\langle k\rangle = 2L/N$; $p_k$; $C_i = 2L_i/[k_i(k_i-1)]$; $\langle d\rangle$ (Ch 2).
  • Random network: Poisson $p_k$, $\langle k^2\rangle = \langle k\rangle(1 + \langle k\rangle)$, giant component for $\langle k\rangle > 1$, $\langle d\rangle \approx \ln N/\ln\langle k\rangle$, $C = \langle k\rangle/N$ (Ch 3).
  • Power law $p_k \sim k^{-\gamma}$; $k_{\max} = k_{\min}N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$; $\langle k^2\rangle \to \infty$ for $\gamma < 3$; configuration model $p_{ij} = k_ik_j/2L$ (Ch 4).
  • BA: $\Pi(k) = k/\sum_j k_j$, $k_i = m(t/t_i)^{1/2}$, $p_k \approx 2m^2k^{-3}$; both growth and preference needed (Ch 5).

Exam-style problems

Problem. A network has $\langle k\rangle = 5$ and $\langle k^2\rangle = 255$. (a) Is its degree distribution Poisson? (b) Mean degree of a random neighbour? (c) Giant component? (d) Random failures needed to destroy it?

  1. (a) Poisson would give $\langle k^2\rangle = 5 \times 6 = 30$. 255 is far larger: heavy tail, hubs.
  2. (b) $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 51$ (friendship paradox, Ch 7).
  3. (c) $\kappa = 51 > 2$: yes (Molloy–Reed, Ch 8).
  4. (d) $f_c = 1 - 1/50 = 98\%$, assuming random wiring.

Problem. Which model fits each description: Erdős–Rényi, Watts–Strogatz, configuration model, Barabási–Albert? (a) Tests whether a property follows from the degrees alone. (b) Short paths and high clustering, no hubs. (c) Older nodes are larger; $\gamma = 3$. (d) Poisson degrees; the baseline for "chance".

  1. (a) Configuration model (or degree-preserving randomization): degrees fixed, all else random.
  2. (b) Watts–Strogatz.
  3. (c) Barabási–Albert.
  4. (d) Erdős–Rényi. Book Table 6.1: ER and WS are static, the configuration model is generative (degrees put in), BA is evolving (degrees explained).

Problem. In two or three sentences each: what does each model add to the previous one? Random → scale-free description → BA → fitness.

  1. Random network: explains giant component and small world; predicts no hubs and $C \to 0$.
  2. Power law: describes the hubs and predicts huge $\langle k^2\rangle$, $k_{\max} \sim N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$, ultra-small distances. It does not say why.
  3. BA: growth + preferential attachment produce the power law ($\gamma = 3$). But the oldest node always wins and $\gamma$ is fixed.
  4. Fitness: growth rate depends on quality too; latecomers can win and $\gamma$ changes with $\rho(\eta)$.

Ch 6 · Evolving networks

Why we need it: in the BA model age alone decides success, so the oldest node is always the largest and $\gamma = 3$. Real networks have late winners (Google was a latecomer to search) and exponents from 2 to 5. Chapter 6 adds the missing processes.

Must know

Bianconi–Barabási (fitness) model. Each new node gets a fitness $\eta$ drawn from $\rho(\eta)$, fixed for life. Links attach with

$$\Pi_i = \frac{\eta_ik_i}{\sum_j \eta_jk_j}$$
$\eta_i$
fitness of node $i$: its intrinsic attractiveness
$\rho(\eta)$
fitness distribution
$\sum_j \eta_jk_j$
normalisation over all nodes

Degree dynamics.

$$k_i(t) = m\Big(\frac{t}{t_i}\Big)^{\beta(\eta_i)}, \qquad \beta(\eta) = \frac{\eta}{C}, \qquad \int\rho(\eta)\,\frac{\eta}{C - \eta}\,d\eta = 1$$
$\beta(\eta)$
dynamical exponent, now different for each node: fitter nodes grow faster
$C$
constant fixed by $\rho(\eta)$; it makes the total $\sum_j\eta_jk_j$ grow like $Cmt$
$t_i$
arrival time of node $i$
  • Equal fitness: $C = 2$, $\beta = \frac12$: back to BA.
  • Uniform $\rho$ on $[0,1]$: $e^{-2/C} = 1 - 1/C$, so $C = 1.255$ and $p_k \sim k^{-(1+C)}/\ln k$: $\gamma = 2.255$ with a log correction.
  • In general $p_k$ is a mixture of power laws, one per fitness: $p_k \approx C\int d\eta\,\frac{\rho(\eta)}{\eta}\big(\frac mk\big)^{C/\eta+1}$.

Measuring fitness. Take logs: $\ln k_i(t) = \beta(\eta_i)\ln t + B_i$. The slope of $\ln k$ against $\ln t$ gives $\beta$, hence $\eta$ up to the factor $C$. On the Web, $\rho(\eta)$ is exponential: fitness differences are small, and growth amplifies them, $\frac{k_2-k_1}{k_1} \sim t^{(\eta_2-\eta_1)/C}$.

Bose–Einstein condensation. Map fitness to energy, $\varepsilon_i = -\frac{1}{\beta_T}\ln\eta_i$ (fitter = lower energy), and each link end to a particle. Two phases, decided only by $\rho(\eta)$:

  • Fit-get-rich (scale-free): the fittest node becomes the largest hub, closely followed by others.
  • Winner-takes-all (condensate): the fittest node keeps a finite share of all links; hub-and-spoke, not scale-free.

Other elementary processes (Section 6.5; each extends BA):

ProcessExponentEffect
Initial attractiveness $\Pi \sim A + k$$\gamma = 3 + A/m$raises $\gamma$; small-degree saturation $p_k \sim (k+A)^{-\gamma}$
$n$ internal links per step, double preferential$\gamma = 2 + \frac{m}{m+2n}$lowers $\gamma$ to between 2 and 3
$n$ internal links per step, random$\gamma = 3 + 2n/m$raises $\gamma$
Accelerated growth $m(t) = m_0t^\theta$$\gamma = 3 + \frac{2\theta}{1-\theta}$raises $\gamma$
Node deletion at rate $r < 1$$\gamma = 3 + \frac{2r}{1-r}$exponential $p_k$ at $r = 1$
Aging $\Pi \sim k\,(t - t_i)^{-\nu}$$\gamma < 3$ for $\nu < 0$$\gamma \to \infty$ as $\nu \to 1$; no power law for $\nu > 1$
$A$
initial attractiveness: lets nodes with $k = 0$ get links
$n$
links added between existing nodes per new node
$\theta$
acceleration: links per new node grow like $t^\theta$
$r$
node removal rate (nodes removed per node added)
$\nu$
aging exponent: attractiveness falls with age $t - t_i$

What this lets you do: predict $p_k$ from the processes that build the network. Book: "get the dynamics right; the topology is the bonus."

Exam-style problems

Problem. Half the nodes have $\eta = 1$, half $\eta = 0.5$. Find $C$, both dynamical exponents and the exponent of the tail.

  1. Condition: $\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{C-1} + \frac12\cdot\frac{0.5}{C-0.5} = 1$.
  2. Multiply out: $0.5(C-0.5) + 0.25(C-1) = (C-1)(C-0.5)$, i.e. $C^2 - 2.25C + 1 = 0$.
  3. Roots 1.640 and 0.610. $C$ must exceed $\eta_{\max} = 1$ (else $\beta > 1$), so $C = 1.640$.
  4. $\beta(1) = 1/1.640 = 0.61$; $\beta(0.5) = 0.30$.
  5. Each fitness class gives $p_k \sim k^{-(1 + C/\eta)}$; the fit class dominates the tail: $\gamma = 1 + 1/0.61 = 2.64$. Between BA (3) and uniform fitness (2.255).

Problem. Same network, $m = 2$. Node A arrives at $t = 100$ with $\eta = 0.5$; node B at $t = 2{,}000$ with $\eta = 1$. When does B overtake A?

  1. $k_A = 2(t/100)^{0.305}$, $k_B = 2(t/2000)^{0.610}$.
  2. Equal when $0.305\ln(t/100) = 0.610\ln(t/2000)$, i.e. $\ln(t/100) = 2\ln(t/2000)$.
  3. $t/100 = (t/2000)^2 \Rightarrow t = 2000^2/100 = 40{,}000$.
  4. After that B leads forever. In BA ($\beta$ equal) this never happens.

Problem. In a network with uniform fitness ($C = 1.255$), three pages have $\ln k$ against $\ln t$ slopes 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8. Find their fitness. What if $C$ were unknown?

  1. $\eta = C\beta$: $1.255 \times 0.4 = 0.50$, $0.75$, $1.00$.
  2. Without $C$ only ratios are known: $\eta$'s are in ratio 2 : 3 : 4. Ranking and relative fitness are what matter for prediction.

Problem. Compute $\gamma$ for (a) $m = 2$, $A = 2$; (b) $m = 1$ with $n = 1$ double-preferential internal link; (c) $m = 2$, $n = 1$ random internal link; (d) $\theta = 0.2$. Which can explain the WWW's $\gamma \approx 2.1$?

  1. (a) $3 + 2/2 = 4$. (b) $2 + 1/3 = 2.33$. (c) $3 + 2/2 = 4$. (d) $3 + 0.4/0.8 = 3.5$.
  2. Only (b) lowers $\gamma$ below 3. Extra advantage for the already-large (double preference, $\nu < 0$, uniform fitness) lowers $\gamma$; random ingredients raise it.
  3. Most new Web links join existing pages (Section 6.5). If they follow double preference, $\gamma$ falls toward 2.

Problem. Two growing networks of $10^6$ nodes. In X the largest hub holds 0.1% of all links and the share keeps falling as X grows; in Y it holds 30% and stays there. Interpret with the Bose gas mapping.

  1. X: fit-get-rich phase. Fitness decides the order of hubs but no node dominates; scale-free.
  2. Y: Bose–Einstein condensate. The fittest node keeps a finite share: winner-takes-all, hub-and-spoke.
  3. Which phase occurs depends only on $\rho(\eta)$, not on the "temperature" $\beta_T$, which drops out.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the growth exponent $\beta(\eta)$ with the Bose-gas $\beta_T$ (inverse temperature).
  • Taking the root $C < \eta_{\max}$: then the fittest node would grow faster than $t$.
  • "Fitness makes everything scale-free with $\gamma = 1 + C$": that is for uniform $\rho$ only, with a $1/\ln k$ correction.
  • Expecting every extension to lower $\gamma$: most random ingredients raise it.

Ch 7 · Degree correlations

Why we need it: the degree distribution counts hubs but not whom they link to. Networks with the same degrees can differ here and behave differently (giant component, distances, robustness).

Must know

Three types. Assortative: hubs link to hubs. Disassortative: hubs link to small nodes. Neutral: as random wiring given the degrees.

Who sits at the end of a link.

$$q_k = \frac{kp_k}{\langle k\rangle}, \qquad \text{neutral: } e_{jk} = q_jq_k$$
$q_k$
probability that a random link end sits at a degree-$k$ node: hubs are reached $k$ times more often
$e_{jk}$
probability that a random link joins degrees $j$ and $k$ (degree correlation matrix)

Degree correlation function.

$$k_{nn}(k_i) = \frac{1}{k_i}\sum_j A_{ij}k_j, \qquad k_{nn}(k) = \sum_{k'}k'P(k'\mid k), \qquad \text{neutral: } k_{nn}(k) = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}$$
$k_{nn}(k_i)$
average degree of node $i$'s neighbours
$k_{nn}(k)$
its average over all nodes of degree $k$
$P(k'\mid k)$
probability that a neighbour of a degree-$k$ node has degree $k'$; neutral: $q_{k'}$

Neutral value $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle > \langle k\rangle$: your friends have more friends than you (friendship paradox). Fit

$$k_{nn}(k) \approx a\,k^{\mu}$$
$\mu$
correlation exponent: $> 0$ assortative, $= 0$ neutral, $< 0$ disassortative
$a$
prefactor

Degree correlation coefficient (Newman): the Pearson correlation of the degrees at the two ends of a link.

$$r = \sum_{jk}\frac{jk\,(e_{jk} - q_jq_k)}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \sum_k k^2q_k - \Big(\sum_k kq_k\Big)^2$$
$r$
between $-1$ and $1$; sign as for $\mu$
$e_{jk} - q_jq_k$
excess of $j$–$k$ links over the neutral expectation
$\sigma^2$
variance of $q_k$, normalises $r$

By hand, from the link list (each link counted once, $j, k$ its end degrees):

$$r = \frac{\overline{jk} - \overline{\tfrac{j+k}{2}}^{\,2}}{\overline{\tfrac{j^2+k^2}{2}} - \overline{\tfrac{j+k}{2}}^{\,2}}$$
$\overline{\,\cdot\,}$
average over the $L$ links

Book values: collaboration $+0.13$, phone calls $+0.21$; metabolism $-0.25$, email $-0.08$; power grid $\approx 0$.

Structural cutoff. Random wiring expects $kk'/(\langle k\rangle N)$ links between two nodes of degrees $k$ and $k'$. A simple network allows at most 1. Two hubs of degree $k_s$ already expect one link:

$$k_s \sim (\langle k\rangle N)^{1/2}$$
$k_s$
structural cutoff: where the expected number of links between two equal hubs reaches 1
$\langle k\rangle N$
$= 2L$, the number of link ends

Above $k_s$, links between hubs are missing: structural disassortativity. It occurs when $k_{\max} > k_s$, i.e. for $\gamma < 3$. Test: degree-preserving randomization with simple links (R-S). If the randomized $k_{nn}(k)$ matches the real one, the correlations are structural; if it is flat, a real mechanism made them.

Models. ER and BA are neutral; with scale-free degrees, the simple configuration model and the hidden-parameter model are structurally disassortative ($k_{nn} \sim k^{-1}$ for the latter); the fitness model (uniform) is disassortative. Xalvi-Brunet–Sokolov rewiring tunes correlations at fixed degrees.

Impact. Assortative: giant component appears at lower $\langle k\rangle$ but is smaller for large $\langle k\rangle$; shorter $\langle d\rangle$, larger $d_{\max}$. Disassortative: the opposite.

Exam-style problems

Problem. Triangle a–b–c plus a pendant d linked to a. Find every $k_{nn}(k_i)$, the function $k_{nn}(k)$, the neutral value and $r$. Classify.

  1. Degrees: a 3, b 2, c 2, d 1. $k_{nn}$: a $(2+2+1)/3 = 1.67$; b, c $(3+2)/2 = 2.5$; d $3$.
  2. $k_{nn}(1) = 3$, $k_{nn}(2) = 2.5$, $k_{nn}(3) = 1.67$: falling, disassortative.
  3. Neutral: $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = (18/4)/(8/4) = 2.25$.
  4. Links $(j,k)$: (3,2), (3,2), (2,2), (3,1). $\overline{jk} = 19/4 = 4.75$; $\overline{(j+k)/2} = 9/4 = 2.25$ (equal to the neutral value, as it must be); $\overline{(j^2+k^2)/2} = 22/4 = 5.5$.
  5. $r = (4.75 - 5.0625)/(5.5 - 5.0625) = -0.3125/0.4375 = -0.71$.

Problem. Metabolism: $N = 1{,}039$, $\langle k\rangle = 9.13$, $k_{\max} = 638$. Power grid: $N = 4{,}941$, $\langle k\rangle = 2.67$, $k_{\max} = 19$. Which can show structural disassortativity? For metabolism's two top hubs (638 and 460), how many links does random wiring expect?

  1. Metabolism: $k_s = \sqrt{9.13 \times 1{,}039} = \sqrt{9{,}486} \approx 97 < 638$: yes.
  2. Power grid: $k_s = \sqrt{2.67 \times 4{,}941} \approx 115 > 19$: no conflict; it is neutral ($r \approx 0.003$).
  3. Expected links: $638 \times 460/9{,}486 \approx 31$. A simple network allows 1, so the hubs' other links go to small nodes.

Problem. Phone calls: $\langle k\rangle = 3.11$, $\langle k^2\rangle = 17.30$. What is the mean degree of a random person's contact if the network were neutral? Why does this matter for vaccination?

  1. $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle = 17.30/3.11 = 5.57$, almost twice $\langle k\rangle$.
  2. Following a link reaches a high-degree node with probability $\propto k$ ($q_k$).
  3. Vaccinating a random contact of a random person reaches hubs without knowing the network (acquaintance immunization).

Problem. A network has $k_{nn}(2) = 20$ and $k_{nn}(200) = 6$ on a straight log–log line. Find $\mu$ and classify.

  1. $\mu = \ln(6/20)/\ln(200/2) = \ln 0.3/\ln 100 = -1.204/4.605 = -0.26$.
  2. $\mu < 0$: disassortative. Next step: compare with R-S randomization to see if it is structural.

Problem. Suppose network X has $r = 0.15$ and its R-S randomization $r \approx 0$; network Y has $r = -0.25$ and its randomization $r = -0.20$. What do you conclude about each?

  1. X: the correlation disappears when only the degrees are kept, so it comes from how links were formed (homophily, communities). Typical of social networks.
  2. Y: most of the disassortativity survives randomization, so it is structural: forced by hubs above $k_s$ in a simple network. Typical of biological networks.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing $k_{nn}(k)$ with $\langle k\rangle$: the neutral reference is $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$.
  • Calling every negative $r$ a "mechanism": check structural disassortativity first.
  • Counting each link twice in the by-hand $r$ formula and then dividing by $L$.

Ch 8 · Network robustness

Why we need it: a network works while its nodes can reach each other. Robustness theory predicts, from the degrees alone, how much damage breaks the giant component, and why hubs make networks robust to accidents but fragile to attacks.

Must know

Percolation. Occupy each site of a lattice with probability $p$. At a critical $p_c$ a spanning cluster appears (square lattice: $p_c \approx 0.593$). Near $p_c$:

$$P_\infty \sim (p - p_c)^{\beta_p}, \qquad \langle s\rangle \sim |p - p_c|^{-\gamma_p}, \qquad \xi \sim |p - p_c|^{-\nu}$$
$P_\infty$
probability that a site belongs to the largest cluster (order parameter)
$\langle s\rangle$
average cluster size
$\xi$
correlation length: typical distance within a cluster
$\beta_p, \gamma_p, \nu$
critical exponents: universal (independent of lattice details), unlike $p_c$

Removing a fraction $f$ of nodes is percolation in reverse: at $f_c$ the giant component falls apart.

Molloy–Reed criterion. A node reached along a link must have, on average, at least one more link to pass the path on:

$$\kappa = \frac{\langle k^2\rangle}{\langle k\rangle} > 2$$
$\kappa$
mean degree of a node reached by following a random link
$2$
one link in, at least one out

Assumes random wiring (no correlations). Random network: $\kappa = 1 + \langle k\rangle$, so $\kappa > 2 \iff \langle k\rangle > 1$ (Ch 3).

Random failures. Remove a fraction $f$ at random. A degree-$k$ node keeps each link with probability $1 - f$, so

$$\begin{aligned}\langle k\rangle_f &= (1-f)\langle k\rangle\\ \langle k^2\rangle_f &= (1-f)^2\langle k^2\rangle + f(1-f)\langle k\rangle\\ \kappa_f &= (1-f)\kappa + f\end{aligned}$$

Set $\kappa_f = 2$:

$$f_c = 1 - \frac{1}{\kappa - 1}, \qquad f_c^{ER} = 1 - \frac{1}{\langle k\rangle}$$
$f_c$
critical fraction of randomly removed nodes
$f_c^{ER}$
for a random network, using $\kappa = 1 + \langle k\rangle$

Scale-free networks. For $2 < \gamma < 3$:

$$\kappa \approx \frac{\gamma-2}{3-\gamma}\,k_{\min}^{\gamma-2}\,k_{\max}^{3-\gamma}, \qquad f_c \approx 1 - \frac{C}{N^{(3-\gamma)/(\gamma-1)}}$$
$k_{\max}$
grows with $N$, so $\kappa \to \infty$ and $f_c \to 1$: random failures cannot break a large scale-free network
$C$
a constant (finite-size formula)

For $\gamma > 3$: $\kappa = \frac{\gamma-2}{\gamma-3}k_{\min}$, finite; $f_c < 1$.

Attacks (remove hubs first). The threshold solves (book Eq. 8.12)

$$f_c^{\frac{2-\gamma}{1-\gamma}} = 2 + \frac{2-\gamma}{3-\gamma}\,k_{\min}\Big(f_c^{\frac{3-\gamma}{1-\gamma}} - 1\Big)$$
$f_c$
here the fraction of highest-degree nodes removed; only a few per cent to some tens of per cent

Removing hubs cuts $\langle k^2\rangle$ fast, so $\kappa$ reaches 2 quickly. Robust yet fragile.

Cascading failures (Sections 8.5–8.6). Ingredients: a flow on the network, a local breakdown rule, redistribution of load. Avalanche sizes follow

$$p(s) \sim s^{-\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = \begin{cases}3/2 & \gamma \ge 3\\ \gamma/(\gamma-1) & 2 < \gamma < 3\end{cases}$$
$s$
avalanche size: number of failed nodes
$\alpha$
avalanche exponent; derived from the branching model at its critical point (mean offspring 1)

Failure propagation model: a node fails when a fraction $\varphi$ of its neighbours has failed. High $\langle k\rangle$: cascades die out; low: global cascades; in between: power-law sizes.

Building robustness (Section 8.7). Maximise $f_c^{\text{tot}} = f_c^{\text{rand}} + f_c^{\text{targ}}$. Optimum: a bimodal degree distribution, a few hubs ($k_{\max} \sim N^{2/3}$) and many nodes of equal degree.

Exam-style problems

Problem. Derive $f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa - 1)$ from $\kappa_f = (1-f)\kappa + f$. Apply it to a random network with $\langle k\rangle = 4$ and check that the survivors have $\langle k\rangle_f = 1$.

  1. $(1-f)\kappa + f = 2 \Rightarrow \kappa - 2 = f(\kappa - 1) \Rightarrow f_c = \frac{\kappa-2}{\kappa-1} = 1 - \frac{1}{\kappa-1}$.
  2. Random: $\kappa = 1 + 4 = 5$, $f_c = 1 - 1/4 = 0.75$.
  3. Check: $\kappa_f = 0.25 \times 5 + 0.75 = 2$ ✓; $\langle k\rangle_f = 0.25 \times 4 = 1$ ✓, the Ch 3 threshold.

Problem. Internet: $\langle k\rangle = 6.34$, $\langle k^2\rangle = 240.1$. Power grid: $\langle k\rangle = 2.67$, $\langle k^2\rangle = 10.33$. Compute $f_c$ for each and for random networks with the same $\langle k\rangle$. Interpret.

  1. Internet: $\kappa = 37.9$, $f_c = 1 - 1/36.9 = 97.3\%$. Random: $1 - 1/6.34 = 84.2\%$.
  2. Power grid: $\kappa = 3.87$, $f_c = 1 - 1/2.87 = 65.2\%$. Random: $62.5\%$.
  3. Hubs (large $\langle k^2\rangle$) give the Internet its extra robustness; the grid has no hubs and gains almost nothing.
  4. The real grid breaks earlier than 65%: it is spatial, not randomly wired, which Molloy–Reed assumes.

Problem. Scale-free network, $\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 1$. Find $\kappa$ and $f_c$ for $N = 10^4$ and $N = 10^6$.

  1. $k_{\max} = N^{1/1.5}$: 464 and $10^4$.
  2. $\kappa \approx \frac{0.5}{0.5}\,k_{\max}^{0.5}$: 21.5 and 100.
  3. $f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa - 1)$: 95.1% and 99.0%.
  4. $f_c \to 1$ as $N$ grows: the larger the network, the harder to break by random failures.

Problem. Attack on $\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 2$. Solve Eq. 8.12 exactly (hint: set $x = f_c^{1/3}$). Compare with random failures at $N = 10^6$.

  1. Exponents: $\frac{2-\gamma}{1-\gamma} = \frac13$, $\frac{3-\gamma}{1-\gamma} = -\frac13$; $\frac{2-\gamma}{3-\gamma} = -1$.
  2. $x = 2 - 2(1/x - 1) = 4 - 2/x \Rightarrow x^2 - 4x + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 2 - \sqrt2 = 0.586$ (need $x < 1$).
  3. $f_c = x^3 = 0.20$: removing the top 20% of nodes destroys it.
  4. Random: $k_{\max} = 2 \times 10^4$, $\kappa \approx \sqrt2 \times \sqrt{2\times10^4} = 200$, $f_c = 1 - 1/199 = 99.5\%$.

Problem. Cascades on two networks: one random, one scale-free with $\gamma = 2.5$. Give the avalanche exponents. Which has relatively more large blackouts?

  1. Random ($\gamma \ge 3$ behaviour): $\alpha = 3/2$.
  2. $\gamma = 2.5$: $\alpha = 2.5/1.5 = 1.67$.
  3. Larger $\alpha$ means a faster-decaying tail, so the scale-free network has relatively fewer very large avalanches in this model; both are heavy-tailed.

Common mistakes

  • Using $\kappa = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle^2$ or $\langle k\rangle$ in Molloy–Reed: it is $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$.
  • "Scale-free networks are robust": only to random failures; they are fragile to attacks.
  • Applying $f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa-1)$ to correlated or spatial networks without saying it assumes random wiring.
  • Expecting $f_c = 1$ exactly in a finite network: $f_c \to 1$ only as $N \to \infty$.

Intro to graph machine learning

Why we need it: earlier chapters describe whole networks with a few numbers. Prediction tasks need a description of every node or pair, learned from the links.

Must know

  • Tasks: node classification (label nodes from a few known labels), link prediction (find missing or future links), community detection, graph classification.
  • Features: hand-made (degree, clustering, distances) or learned (embeddings, GNNs).
  • Transductive methods embed only the nodes seen in training; inductive ones (GNNs) apply to new nodes.

Link prediction scores for an unlinked pair $u, v$:

$$\text{CN} = |N(u)\cap N(v)|, \quad \text{J} = \frac{|N(u)\cap N(v)|}{|N(u)\cup N(v)|}, \quad \text{AA} = \sum_{w \in N(u)\cap N(v)}\frac{1}{\ln k_w}, \quad \text{PA} = k_uk_v$$
$N(u)$
set of neighbours of $u$
CN, J
common neighbours; Jaccard normalises by the union
AA
Adamic–Adar: a shared low-degree neighbour counts more than a shared hub
PA
preferential attachment score, from the BA model

Evaluation. Hide some links, score hidden links and non-links:

$$\text{AUC} = P(\text{score of hidden link} > \text{score of non-link}), \quad \text{ties count } \tfrac12$$
AUC
0.5 = guessing, 1 = perfect ranking

Graph neural networks: each layer mixes a node's vector with its neighbours' (message passing):

$$h_v^{(l+1)} = \sigma\Big(W^{(l)}\cdot\operatorname{mean}\{h_u^{(l)} : u \in \{v\}\cup N(v)\}\Big)$$
$h_v^{(l)}$
vector of node $v$ after layer $l$; $h_v^{(0)}$ are input features
$W^{(l)}$
learned weight matrix
$\sigma$
non-linearity, e.g. ReLU

GCN (Kipf & Welling 2017) in matrix form: $H^{(l+1)} = \sigma\big(\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\tilde D^{-1/2}H^{(l)}W^{(l)}\big)$ with $\tilde A = A + I$ and $\tilde D$ its degree matrix. After $l$ layers a node sees its $l$-hop neighbourhood. Many layers: all vectors become alike (over-smoothing).

Exam-style problems

Problem. Links: A–B, A–C, B–C, B–D, C–D, D–E, D–F, E–F, D–G. Score the pairs A–D, B–E, E–G with CN, Jaccard, Adamic–Adar and PA. Which pair does each score rank first?

  1. Degrees: A 2, B 3, C 3, D 5, E 2, F 2, G 1.
  2. A–D: common {B, C}; union {B, C, E, F, G}. CN 2, J 2/5 = 0.40, AA $2/\ln3 = 1.82$, PA 10.
  3. B–E: common {D}; union {A, C, D, F}. CN 1, J 0.25, AA $1/\ln5 = 0.62$, PA 6.
  4. E–G: common {D}; union {D, F}. CN 1, J 0.50, AA 0.62, PA 2.
  5. CN, AA, PA rank A–D first; Jaccard ranks E–G first: low-degree nodes sharing their only hub look similar after normalising.

Problem. Hidden links score 0.9, 0.4, 0.7; non-links score 0.5, 0.1, 0.7. Compute the AUC.

  1. 9 pairs. 0.9 beats all three: 3.
  2. 0.4 beats only 0.1: 1.
  3. 0.7 beats 0.5 and 0.1, ties 0.7: $2 + \frac12 = 2.5$.
  4. AUC $= 6.5/9 = 0.72$.

Problem. Path 1–2–3–4, input $h^{(0)} = (1, 0, 0, 0)$. Apply two mean-aggregation layers (no $W$, no $\sigma$). Which nodes have a non-zero value? What happens after many layers?

  1. Layer 1 (mean over self + neighbours): $h_1 = (1+0)/2 = 0.5$, $h_2 = (0+1+0)/3 = 0.33$, $h_3 = 0$, $h_4 = 0$.
  2. Layer 2: $h_1 = (0.5+0.33)/2 = 0.42$, $h_2 = (0.33+0.5+0)/3 = 0.28$, $h_3 = (0+0.33+0)/3 = 0.11$, $h_4 = 0$.
  3. After 2 layers the signal reached distance 2 only (node 4 is at distance 3).
  4. Many layers: values converge to a common constant; differences vanish (over-smoothing).

Problem. With 10% of links hidden, common neighbours score AUC $\approx 0.96$ on the collaboration network but $\approx 0.57$ on the protein network. Explain using Ch 2–3 concepts.

  1. CN assumes triadic closure: friends of friends link. That is high clustering.
  2. Co-authorship is highly clustered (each paper is a clique), so CN works.
  3. Proteins sharing a partner often compete for the same binding site and do not bind each other; clustering is low. CN is near guessing.

Common mistakes

  • Evaluating link prediction on the links used to compute the scores: hide them first.
  • Reading AUC = 0.5 as "half the links found": it means no better than chance.
  • Stacking many GNN layers "to see more": over-smoothing erases the information.

Node embeddings

Why we need it: a classifier needs a fixed-length vector per node. An embedding $z_v \in \mathbb R^d$ places nodes so that nodes close in the network are close in space; any standard ML method can then use them.

Must know

Spectral embedding. Graph Laplacian and its quadratic form:

$$L = D - A, \qquad x^\top L x = \sum_{(i,j)\in E}(x_i - x_j)^2, \qquad \mathcal L = I - D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}$$
$D$
diagonal matrix of degrees $k_i$
$x$
one number per node (a 1-D embedding)
$E$
set of links; the sum is the "energy": small if linked nodes get similar values
$\mathcal L$
normalised Laplacian, used when degrees vary a lot
  • Smallest eigenvalue 0, eigenvector constant; number of zero eigenvalues = number of components.
  • Second eigenvector (Fiedler vector): lowest-energy non-trivial embedding; its sign gives a two-way cut. Further eigenvectors give more coordinates.

Random-walk embeddings. DeepWalk (Perozzi et al. 2014): run short uniform random walks, treat them as sentences and nodes as words, train word2vec's skip-gram:

$$\max \sum_{u}\sum_{v \in \mathcal N_w(u)}\log P(v\mid u), \qquad P(v\mid u) = \frac{e^{z_u\cdot z_v}}{\sum_{n}e^{z_u\cdot z_n}}$$
$\mathcal N_w(u)$
nodes within $w$ steps of $u$ on the walks (the context window)
$z_u\cdot z_v$
dot product: similarity of the two vectors
$\sum_n$
over all nodes; too costly, so approximated (hierarchical softmax in DeepWalk, negative sampling in node2vec)

node2vec (Grover & Leskovec 2016) biases the walk. Having stepped $t \to v$, the next step to $x$ gets weight

$$\alpha_{pq}(t,x) = \begin{cases}1/p & d_{tx} = 0 \ (\text{back to } t)\\ 1 & d_{tx} = 1\\ 1/q & d_{tx} = 2\end{cases}$$
$p$
return parameter: small $p$ = walks backtrack, stay local
$q$
in–out parameter: small $q$ = move outward (depth-first, finds communities); large $q$ = stay near $t$ (breadth-first, finds structural roles)
$d_{tx}$
distance from the previous node $t$ to the candidate $x$

$p = q = 1$ gives DeepWalk. Embeddings are transductive: a new node needs retraining.

Exam-style problems

Problem. Two triangles joined by a bridge (links 1–2, 1–3, 2–3, 3–4, 4–5, 4–6, 5–6). For $x = (1,1,1,-1,-1,-1)$ compute $x^\top Lx$ and the Rayleigh quotient $x^\top Lx/x^\top x$. The true $\lambda_2 = 0.44$; what does the Fiedler vector do?

  1. Only the bridge 3–4 joins different values: $(1-(-1))^2 = 4$. All other terms are 0. $x^\top Lx = 4$.
  2. $x^\top x = 6$; quotient $= 0.67$. It is an upper bound on $\lambda_2$ for vectors orthogonal to the constant ✓ ($0.67 \ge 0.44$).
  3. The Fiedler vector is about $(-0.47, -0.47, -0.26, 0.26, 0.47, 0.47)$: the same sign split {1, 2, 3} | {4, 5, 6}, with bridge nodes 3 and 4 nearer zero.

Problem. node2vec with $p = 4$, $q = 0.5$. The walk went $t \to v$. $v$'s neighbours: $t$, $x_1$ (also linked to $t$), $x_2$ (not linked to $t$). Give the transition probabilities. Local or exploratory walk?

  1. Weights: back to $t$: $1/p = 0.25$; $x_1$ ($d = 1$): 1; $x_2$ ($d = 2$): $1/q = 2$.
  2. Sum 3.25. Probabilities: 0.08, 0.31, 0.62.
  3. Rarely returns, prefers moving away: exploratory (depth-first-like). Embeddings then reflect communities.

Problem. Compare spectral embedding and DeepWalk: what does each need to compute, what similarity does it capture, and what can neither do for a node added tomorrow?

  1. Spectral: a few eigenvectors of $L$ or $\mathcal L$; captures smoothness over links (cuts, communities). Exact, but eigenvectors of huge networks are costly.
  2. DeepWalk: random walks plus skip-gram training; captures co-occurrence within a few steps. Scales to large sparse networks; results vary with random seeds.
  3. Neither embeds an unseen node without recomputation (transductive). GNNs with input features are inductive.

Common mistakes

  • Using the eigenvector of the largest Laplacian eigenvalue for the cut: it is the second smallest.
  • Mixing up $p$ and $q$: $p$ controls returning, $q$ controls moving outward.
  • Comparing embedding coordinates across separate training runs: they are defined only up to rotation.
