Structural cutoffs and real networks

Some correlations are forced on a network: a simple network cannot give its largest hubs the links to each other that chance requires. Section 7.4 shows when this happens; Section 7.5 separates forced from genuine correlations in real networks.

  • Above the structural cutoff $k_s$, scale-free networks become disassortative without any cause.
  • Degree-preserving randomization tells structural correlations from real ones.
  • Only genuine correlations, such as the assortativity of social networks, say how a network was built.

Structural cutoffs (Section 7.4)

All the book's networks are simple: at most one link between two nodes. That clashes with (7.1) for the largest hubs. The expected number of links between degrees $k$ and $k'$ is

$$E_{kk'} = e_{kk'}\,\langle k\rangle N \quad (7.13) \qquad\xrightarrow{\text{neutral}}\qquad E_{kk'} = \frac{k\,k'}{\langle k\rangle N}. \quad (7.14)$$

Worked example 4: two hubs that need three links. A scale-free network with $N = 300$, $L = 450$ ($\langle k\rangle = 3$) has hubs of degree 55 and 46.

  1. $E = \frac{55 \times 46}{3 \times 300} = \frac{2530}{900} = 2.8$: neutral wiring needs about three links between them.
  2. A simple network allows one. The missing links must go elsewhere, that is, to smaller nodes.
  3. Stub picture: nodes with 8 and 7 stubs among 28 stubs in total expect $8 \times 7/28 = 2$ links. Forbid the second, and the network becomes disassortative without anyone asking for it: structural disassortativity.

The conflict starts where $E_{kk} = 1$, at the structural cutoff

$$k_s(N) \sim (\langle k\rangle N)^{1/2}. \qquad (7.15)$$

Compare it with the largest degree, the natural cutoff $k_{\max} \sim k_{\min} N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$:

  • Random networks and $\gamma \ge 3$: $1/(\gamma - 1) \le 1/2$, so $k_{\max}$ stays below $k_s$. No conflict.
  • $2 < \gamma < 3$: $1/(\gamma - 1) > 1/2$, so for large $N$ the biggest hubs exceed $k_s$. Simple scale-free networks are structurally disassortative.

Worked example 5: when does it bite? $\gamma = 2.5$, $k_{\min} = 2$, so $\langle k\rangle \approx \frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}k_{\min} = 6$.

  1. $N = 10^4$: $k_s = \sqrt{6 \times 10^4} = 245$, $k_{\max} = 2 \times 10^{4/1.5} = 928$.
  2. $N = 10^6$: $k_s = 2{,}449$, $k_{\max} = 2 \times 10^{4} = 20{,}000$. The range of conflicted hubs widens with $N$.
  3. Metabolism: $k_s = \sqrt{9.13 \times 1{,}039} \approx 97$. Its top hubs have 638 and 460 links: $638 \times 460/9{,}482 \approx 31$ expected links between them, at most 1 allowed.

Two ways out: allow multi-links (the network becomes neutral), or remove hubs above $k_s$. To decide whether a real network's correlations are structural, randomize it while keeping its degrees:

  • R-S (randomization keeping the network simple): if $k_{nn}^{R\text{-}S}(k)$ matches the real $k_{nn}(k)$, the correlations are all structural, explained by the degrees.
  • R-M (multi-links allowed): always neutral; a consistency check.

Structural effects can only produce disassortativity. Assortativity always needs a real cause.

Correlations in real networks (Section 7.5)

  • Power grid: the only truly neutral network; flat and equal to its randomized version.
  • Social networks (phone calls, collaboration, actors) are assortative, and randomization erases it: a real social mechanism. Collaboration $r = 0.13$, phone calls $0.21$.
  • Email, proteins, metabolism are disassortative, but their R-S versions decay too: structural. Metabolism stays at $r = -0.17$ after randomization.
  • WWW: disassortative, only partly explained by its degrees. Internet: assortative up to $k \approx 30$, then flattened by the cutoff. Citations: assortative for $k \le 20$, disassortative above.

Why are social networks assortative? People form communities (Chapter 9), society brings hubs together (committees, boards, TV shows), and homophily makes similar people, including similarly connected ones, associate. Directed networks need four functions, $k_{nn}^{\text{in,in}}$, $k_{nn}^{\text{in,out}}$, $k_{nn}^{\text{out,in}}$, $k_{nn}^{\text{out,out}}$ (Box 7.3).

The collaboration network has $r = 0.13$; after degree-preserving randomization $r \approx 0$. What does this show?

  1. The assortativity comes from how scientists choose collaborators, not from the degrees
  2. Randomization changed $p_k$
  3. It is structural
  4. The network is neutral
What randomization destroys is not implied by the degrees. Metabolism's survives ($-0.25 \to -0.17$): largely structural.

A network has $N = 10{,}000$ nodes and $\langle k\rangle = 4$. Two hubs have 1,000 links each. How many links between them would random wiring expect?

  1. At most 1
  2. 100
  3. 12.5
  4. 25
$kk'/(\langle k\rangle N) = 10^6/(4 \times 10^4) = 25$. A simple network allows one, so both hubs, far above $k_s = \sqrt{40{,}000} = 200$, are pushed to link to small nodes.

A network has $N = 2{,}500$ and $\langle k\rangle = 4$. What is its structural cutoff $k_s$?

  1. 50
  2. 2,500
  3. 10,000
  4. 100
$k_s = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle N} = \sqrt{10{,}000} = 100$.

A simple scale-free network with $\gamma = 2.3$ is wired at random (degree-preserving randomization that keeps it simple). Its $k_{nn}(k)$ falls at large $k$. Why?

  1. Its largest hubs exceed the structural cutoff, and a simple network cannot give them the links to each other that chance requires
  2. The randomization made it disassortative on purpose
  3. It is secretly assortative
  4. It cannot happen for γ < 3
For $\gamma < 3$, $k_{\max} \sim N^{1/(\gamma-1)}$ outgrows $k_s \sim N^{1/2}$: structural disassortativity.
