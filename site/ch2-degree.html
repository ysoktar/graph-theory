Degree, average degree and degree distribution

How many links does a node have, and how unevenly are the links shared out among the nodes? A node's degree answers the first question and the network's degree distribution the second. Random and scale-free networks (Parts II and III) differ above all in this distribution, and it decides how robust a network is and how things spread on it.

Degree and average degree

The degree $k_i$ of node $i$ is its number of links. Adding up all degrees counts each link twice, once at each end (the handshaking identity), so

$$L = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} k_i, \qquad \langle k\rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} k_i = \frac{2L}{N}.$$
$N$, $L$
the number of nodes and the number of links
$k_i$
the degree of node $i$
$\sum_{i=1}^{N}$
the sum over all nodes, $k_1 + \dots + k_N$
$\langle k\rangle$
the average degree; angle brackets $\langle\cdot\rangle$ mean an average over all nodes

In a directed network, such as the WWW or email, each link points from one node to another. Node $i$ then has an in-degree $k_i^{\text{in}}$ and an out-degree $k_i^{\text{out}}$, and its total degree is $k_i = k_i^{\text{in}} + k_i^{\text{out}}$. Every link leaves exactly one node and enters exactly one node, so

$$L = \sum_{i} k_i^{\text{in}} = \sum_{i} k_i^{\text{out}}, \qquad \langle k^{\text{in}}\rangle = \langle k^{\text{out}}\rangle = \frac{L}{N}.$$
$k_i^{\text{in}}$
the number of links pointing to $i$
$k_i^{\text{out}}$
the number of links leaving $i$

The degree distribution gives the share of nodes that have each degree:

$$p_k = \frac{N_k}{N}, \qquad \sum_{k} p_k = 1, \qquad \langle k\rangle = \sum_{k} k\,p_k .$$
$N_k$
the number of nodes with degree $k$
$p_k$
the share of nodes with degree $k$, which is also the probability that a randomly chosen node has degree $k$
$\sum_k k\,p_k$
the average degree, computed from the distribution
  • The average degree needs only two counts, $N$ and $L$.
  • From $p_k$ alone we can predict the largest hub, the robustness of the network and the epidemic threshold, without knowing the full wiring.
  • Comparing a real $p_k$ with that of a random network shows that real networks are not random.

Build a network

  • Each new link raises two degrees by one, so $\sum_i k_i$ is always even and the odd-degree nodes come in pairs. Try to break this.
  • In Directed mode, $\sum k^{\text{in}} = \sum k^{\text{out}} = L$ holds however unbalanced single nodes are.
  • The Star preset links one hub to all other nodes, which become leaves (nodes with a single link). The hub has $k = N-1$, yet $\langle k\rangle < 2$: averages hide hubs.

A first real network: Zachary's karate club

Wayne Zachary followed the 34 members of a university karate club from 1970 to 1972. A link means that two members regularly met outside the club. A conflict split the club between the instructor ("Mr. Hi", node 0) and the president (node 33), and the colours show which side each member joined.

The average degree is $\langle k\rangle = 2 \times 78/34 \approx 4.59$. Eleven members have degree 2, so $p_2 = 11/34 \approx 0.32$. The median degree, 3, is below the mean because the two leaders ($k_0 = 16$, $k_{33} = 17$) pull the mean up.

A large network: scientific collaboration

The science collaboration network (book Table 4.1) links $N = 23{,}133$ scientists whenever two of them co-authored a paper. Here is its $p_k$ on linear axes:

The bars seem to end near $k \approx 50$, yet the largest degree is $k_{\max} = 279$. Each hub is a single node out of 23,133, so its bar is too short to see. Switch to a Log axis, on which equal steps mean equal ratios, to see them. Part III shows how to plot $p_k$ properly.

78% of the scientists have at most 10 collaborators, and 28 have 100 or more. The standard deviation $\sigma_k = 10.6$, the typical distance of a degree from the mean, is larger than the mean $\langle k\rangle = 8.08$ itself.

The median scientist has 5 collaborators, the average has 8.1, and the maximum is 279. Which of these numbers would you report as "the typical number of collaborators", and why?

A few very large values pull the mean up, so the median (5) describes a typical node better. Here the spread $\sigma_k$ exceeds $\langle k\rangle$, so no single number is "typical". This is the idea behind the name scale-free (Chapter 4).
  • Builds on Königsberg: dividing the handshaking identity $\sum_i k_i = 2L$ by $N$ gives $\langle k\rangle$.
  • Read from the adjacency matrix, whose row sums are the degrees. Sparsity compares $\langle k\rangle$ with the largest possible degree, $N-1$.
  • Random-network prediction: a random network has a Poisson $p_k$, and real networks deviate from it with heavy tails.
  • Heavy tails lead to power laws, which create hubs and a large $\langle k^2\rangle$ (No typical degree).

A network has $N = 10$ nodes and $L = 15$ links. What is its average degree $\langle k\rangle$?

  1. 1.5
  2. 3
  3. 30
  4. 0.67
$\langle k\rangle = 2L/N = 30/10 = 3$.

Which degree sequence can no network have?

  1. 2, 2, 2
  2. 3, 1, 1, 1
  3. 2, 2, 1
  4. 1, 1, 1, 1
The degrees must add up to $2L$, an even number, but $2+2+1 = 5$ is odd. An even sum is necessary but not sufficient: 3, 3, 3, 1 is impossible too.

A directed network of 5 web pages has 8 links. What is the average in-degree?

  1. 1.6
  2. 3.2
  3. 0.8
  4. It depends on which pages link to which
The in-degrees add up to $L = 8$, so $\langle k^{\text{in}}\rangle = 8/5 = 1.6$, the same as $\langle k^{\text{out}}\rangle$.

In the karate club, 11 of the 34 members have degree 2. What is $p_2$?

  1. $2/34$
  2. $11/34 \approx 0.32$
  3. 11
  4. $1/11$
$p_2 = N_2/N = 11/34 \approx 0.32$, so a randomly chosen member has degree 2 with probability 0.32.

A star network has one hub linked to 10 leaves (nodes with a single link). What is its average degree?

  1. 10
  2. 1
  3. About 1.82, with 10 of the 11 nodes below it
  4. 5.5
$\langle k\rangle = 2 \times 10/11 \approx 1.82$. No node is near the average, which is why we need the whole distribution $p_k$.

In a random network the degrees follow a Poisson distribution, whose standard deviation is $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\langle k\rangle}$. The collaboration network has $\langle k\rangle = 8.08$ but a measured $\sigma_k$ of 10.6. What does this show?

  1. The difference is noise
  2. Random networks spread the degrees more widely
  3. The median must equal the mean
  4. The degrees are far more uneven than in a random network: there is a heavy tail with hubs
A random network would give $\sigma_k \approx \sqrt{8.08} \approx 2.8$, far below the measured 10.6. This is the first sign that real networks are not random (Part II).

A new node joins a network and links to 3 existing nodes. How does $\sum_i k_i$ change?

  1. It grows by 3
  2. It grows by 6
  3. It stays the same
  4. It grows by 4
The 3 new links each have two ends, so $\sum_i k_i = 2L$ grows by 6: the newcomer gets 3, and three old nodes get 1 each.
