Königsberg and the birth of graph theory

In 18th-century Königsberg (today Kaliningrad), seven bridges joined the two banks of the river Pregel, an island and a piece of land between two arms of the river. Residents wondered whether one could take a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once. Nobody managed it. In 1736 Leonhard Euler explained why nobody ever would, and in doing so started graph theory.

Take the walk

Try it yourself before reading on. Click a piece of land to start, then click bridges to cross them; you can use the map or the graph next to it. The highlighted bridges are the ones you can still take from where you stand.

  • However you start, you get stuck with bridges left over.
  • Look at the numbers next to the nodes of the graph: every land mass has an odd number of bridges (5, 3, 3, 3).
  • Switch to Edit bridges and add or remove one bridge. Suddenly a walk exists, but only if it starts and ends at the two land masses that still have an odd number of bridges.

Step 1: throw away the map

Euler's first insight was to keep only what matters for the question. The shape of the island, the length of the bridges and the layout of the streets cannot change whether the walk exists. What matters is which piece of land is connected to which, and by how many bridges. So he replaced each piece of land by a point and each bridge by a line between two points. That drawing is a graph:

  • the points are nodes (also called vertices): here the four land masses A, B, C, D;
  • the lines are links (also called edges): here the seven bridges a–g;
  • the number of links attached to a node is its degree, written $k$. Island A has five bridges, so $k_A = 5$.

Two of the land-mass pairs are joined by two bridges each (A–B and A–C), so the Königsberg graph has repeated links. A graph that allows this is called a multigraph; most networks on this site are simple graphs, with at most one link between two nodes.

This step, replacing a messy real system by its pattern of connections, is the whole method of network science. Once the map is a graph, the same reasoning applies to anything with the same pattern: bridges, streets, power lines, friendships or chemical reactions. On the next pages the systems change, but the method does not.

Step 2: count the bridges at each node

Now follow a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once, and watch a single land mass that is neither the start nor the end. Every time the walk arrives there, it arrives over one bridge and must leave over a different one, because no bridge may be used twice. So the bridges at that land mass are used up in pairs: one in, one out. A land mass you only pass through must therefore have an even number of bridges.

Only the start and the end are different: the walk leaves the start once without arriving first, and arrives at the end once without leaving. They are the only places where an odd number of bridges can be used up. So:

A connected graph has a walk that uses every link exactly once, an Euler path, if and only if it has

  • no node of odd degree: then the walk can start anywhere, and it ends where it started (an Euler circuit); or
  • exactly two nodes of odd degree: then the walk must start at one of them and end at the other.

Königsberg has four odd-degree nodes (degrees 5, 3, 3, 3), so the walk is impossible.

"Connected" means that every node can be reached from every other node along links. Without it, a graph could satisfy the degree condition and still be impossible to cover in one walk, simply because it comes in separate pieces (see Connectedness).

Euler argued the "only if" part: four odd nodes make the walk impossible. That the condition is also sufficient, so that zero or two odd nodes always allow a walk, was proved by Carl Hierholzer in 1873. The Show a solution button uses Hierholzer's method.

Suppose a fifth bridge is built between B and C. Then $k_B$ goes from 3 to 4 and $k_C$ from 3 to 4, while A (5) and D (3) stay odd. Two odd nodes remain, so a walk now exists, and it must run between A and D. Try it: choose Edit bridges, add bridge h, and look for a walk from A to D.

Why the number of odd nodes is always even

You will never find a graph with exactly one (or three) odd-degree nodes. Every link has two ends, so adding up the degrees of all nodes counts every link twice:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} k_i = 2L .$$

For Königsberg: $5 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 14 = 2 \times 7$. The right-hand side is always even, so the odd degrees on the left must come in pairs. This little identity, the handshaking lemma, reappears on the next page as the formula for the average degree, $\langle k\rangle = 2L/N$.

  • Degree decided this problem. It is the most important single property of a node, and its distribution over all nodes is the thread of this whole course (next page).
  • Walks along links, here one that uses every link, become paths and distances in Paths & distances.
  • The lesson that the structure of connections can decide what is possible returns in later chapters: how robust a network is to failures (Ch 8) and how fast a disease spreads (Ch 10) are, like the bridge walk, decided largely by the degrees.

A connected network has nodes with degrees 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 2. Does it have an Euler path?

  1. No: it has odd-degree nodes
  2. Yes, and it can start and end anywhere
  3. Yes, starting at one degree-3 node and ending at the other
  4. We cannot tell without seeing the network
Exactly two nodes have odd degree, so an Euler path exists and must run between them.

A city has four districts joined by bridges, and every district has exactly four bridges. A tourist wants to cross every bridge once and end up back at the hotel. Is that possible?

  1. No, four bridges per district is too many
  2. Yes, if the bridges connect all districts into one piece, and the tour can start at any hotel
  3. Only if the tour starts in one specific district
  4. Only if there are more districts than bridges
All degrees are even and (assuming the bridges connect everything) the graph is connected, so an Euler circuit exists: a walk that uses every bridge once and returns to where it started, from any starting point.

Which change to the original seven bridges makes the walk possible?

  1. Removing both bridges between A and B
  2. Adding a bridge from A back to A
  3. Nothing: no single change can help
  4. Removing any one bridge
Every bridge joins two odd-degree land masses. Removing it makes both of them even, leaving exactly two odd nodes. Then a walk exists, between those two. (Adding any one bridge between two different land masses works for the same reason.)

Euler's argument ignores the lengths of the bridges and the shape of the land. Why is that allowed?

  1. Whether the walk exists depends only on which land masses each bridge joins
  2. The maps of 1736 were not accurate enough to use
  3. All the bridges happened to be the same length
  4. It is an approximation that works for small cities
The question is about the order in which bridges can be used, and that is fixed by the connections alone. Keeping only the connections is what makes the problem solvable, and it is the founding idea of network science: many properties of a system depend on its network of connections rather than on the details of its parts.

Networks and graphs (Section 2.2)

A network is a catalogue of a system's components, the nodes, and of the direct interactions between them, the links. Two numbers describe its size: the number of nodes $N$ and the number of links $L$. Network scientists tend to say network, node and link for real systems, and graph, vertex and edge for the mathematical object, but the terms are used interchangeably.

For the directed networks this merges $u\to v$ and $v\to u$ into a single link, so their $L$ and $\langle k\rangle$ are smaller than the book's directed values.

The same 23,133 scientists could be connected by co-authorship, by citations, by sharing an institution, or by email. Would these networks have the same structure? Which one would you study to understand how ideas spread?

The choice of links defines the network, and different link definitions give very different networks on the same nodes. Co-authorship captures close collaboration (and creates many triangles, because a paper with three authors is a triangle). Citations are directed and reach far more widely. Always ask what a link means before interpreting a network's structure.

Euler paths are still in daily use. Planning a route that covers every street once (a postman, a snow plough, a street-cleaning truck) is the Chinese postman problem, built on Euler's condition. Modern genome sequencing reassembles DNA from millions of short fragments by finding an Euler path through a graph of overlapping fragments. By contrast, the similar-looking task of visiting every node exactly once (a Hamiltonian path) has no simple degree test and is computationally hard in general. A small change in the question makes a huge difference.
